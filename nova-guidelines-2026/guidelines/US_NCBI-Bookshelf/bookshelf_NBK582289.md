# Family-Centered Rounds

### Abstract

This guideline applies to inpatients in C.S. Mott Children’s Hospital that are admitted to pediatric general or subspecialty medicine, PICU, and NICU services.

Patient Population: Inpatients in C.S. Mott Children’s Hospital that are admitted to pediatric general or subspecialty medicine, PICU, and NICU services.

Overview: Family-centered rounds (FCR) are interdisciplinary work rounds at the bedside in which the patient and family are actively involved and participate in optimizing the management plan. FCR encourages the provision of safe, high-quality patient- and family-centered care by creating a partnership with patients and families. It is rooted in the idea that the patient and family are important members of the care team and, as such, should receive open communication regarding their healthcare and participate in the process of medical decision making. Families desire to participate in FCR and report feeling better informed, more confident in the medical team, more satisfied, and less anxious with FCR. Rounding with families allows for new and more complete information to be provided to the medical team, may help prevent adverse events, allows for more efficient time utilization and less need for plan clarification, and does not increase overall healthcare costs.– Given increasing emphasis on the importance of patient-centered care and the benefits of FCR, experts have recommended that FCR be standard practice in pediatric hospitals. – A standardized approach to FCR has been shown to improve performance of key FCR elements, family engagement, and patient safety., The current lack of a standardized approach to FCR, however, leads to inconsistent practice of FCR, varying expectations for trainees, and communication gaps among medical team members and with families.

Purpose: To provide evidence-based guidelines and a standardized approach for the practice of FCR to facilitate communication and partnership with patients and families and promote shared decision-making.

Scope: This guideline presents recommendations for hospitalized patients on inpatient pediatric medical services and pediatric and neonatal intensive care units.

Definitions: Family-centered rounds (FCR) are defined as interdisciplinary bedside rounds that actively engage the patient/family and involve them in shared-decision making with the understanding that patients and families deserve open, honest, and unbiased communication regarding their healthcare.

### FCR Procedures and Actions

Inclusion of the family, as well as other health care professionals, into rounds requires a higher degree of coordination and planning than traditional rounding approaches. Although the optimal combination process may differ from hospital to hospital (or even from unit to unit in the same hospital), there are key procedures and actions that will ensure success. In this section, we combine information from a search of the medical literature, our experience, and the experience of others to compile a list of recommended procedures and actions. These procedures and actions are summarized in Table 1 at the end of this section and in checklist format in Appendix C.

The process begins as soon as a patient is admitted to an inpatient unit. Prior to the next rounding time, the bedside nurse will provide the patient and family with the FCR brochure (Appendix A), and briefly explain the FCR process as well as the patient’s and family’s role in rounds. The following paragraph provides an example of what the nurse might say (note that the amount of time set aside or other specifics may vary depending on the service, setting, patient complexity, family needs, or other factors):

“Here is a handout discussing family-centered rounds. Rounds are the time of day where the medical team comes together to discuss your child’s hospitalization and make a plan for the day. This typically happens in the morning. Rounds are ‘family-centered’ because you are a part of our team, as you know your child best. We invite you to provide input you think may be helpful to the care of your child and ask questions. Rounds typically last about 10 minutes per patient so the medical team can see all of their patients in a timely fashion. Please write down questions that you have. Any questions that can’t be answered on rounds can be answered later in the day by the medical team.”

Prior to the first time rounding with the patient/family, a physician or advanced practice provider (APP) from the primary team will explain FCR, invite patient/family to participate, and elicit patient/family preference for participation. This should occur in advance of the first rounds when possible, either on admission or during pre-rounds, as well as after any changes in patient disposition, such as transfer from general floor to ICU or vice versa., In addition to explaining FCR, the provider should obtain the following preferences:

- Would the patient/family like to participate in FCR?
 - ○ If yes, do they prefer bedside or hallway? Note that hallway rounds may be preferred or even required in certain settings, for example, when isolation precautions are in place or a patient’s clinical status does not permit bedside rounds.
 - ○ If no, the team will round in the hallway and provide updates after.
- Does the patient/family want to be woken up for rounds if they are sleeping? Note that there are scenarios where patients/families must be woken up for rounds, such as medically unstable patients, when time-sensitive medical decisions must be made or procedures performed.

Of course, the preferences may be updated any time throughout hospitalization. The following paragraph provides an example of what the provider might say:

“What is your understanding of family-centered rounds?” [Depending on answer, can clarify what FCR means and invite involvement]

“One of our team members will review medical updates and discuss a plan for the day. Because this is a teaching hospital, there is often back and forth group discussion while we make a care plan, as there are many people working together to provide the best possible care for your child. We will do our best to limit the medical terms that we use. When we do need to review a lot of numbers or other medical terms, we will be sure to explain what they mean at the end. Feel free to provide any clarification or corrections to the story as we go. In order for us to see all of our patients in a timely fashion, we’ll spend about 10 minutes rounding on each patient. Please write down any questions you have. We will do our best to answer at least some of these questions on rounds, and any questions we can’t address during rounds can be answered later in the day.

Would you like to be a part of rounds? Do you like to hear the “whole discussion” or updates only? Do you prefer the team rounding inside of the room or out in the hallway? If you are sleeping, would you like us to wake you up for rounds?”

It can be difficult for patients and families to remember the names and roles of each member of the primary medical team. Therefore, we recommend using specific strategies to assist patients with this, such as providing patients/families with a “face sheet” of their medical team, and/or writing down team member names and roles on the white board in the patient’s room.

Prior to rounds, the senior resident or fellow will assign team member roles (e.g., who will greet family and perform introductions, who will give the presentation, who will summarize plan, who will write down plan for family, etc.–). Table 2 outlines possible team members and their roles.

The presence of the bedside nurse is critical to successful FCR.,– Since nursing workflow is often different than provider workflow, a robust system of communication is necessary to ensure that the nurse is present for rounds. Best practices for ensuring nursing involvement in bedside rounds will continue to evolve based on available technologies, workflow logistics, and process improvements. Every effort should be made to coordinate rounds timing with nursing via page, secure messaging, and/or phone call. This communication should contain specific information about the room number and timing of rounds. For patients on the general care wards, for example, resident assistants (RA) may provide this action on weekdays. Residents or APPs may provide this action on weekends and whenever an RA is not available.

One strategy for ensuring nursing presence during rounds is to have an RA (or other team member) call the bedside nurse when the team is one patient ahead to give the nurse enough lead time to join bedside rounds for their patient. Nurses should ensure that their phone numbers are updated under the MiChart treatment team to allow for more efficient contact. In addition, team “rounding flags” can be displayed outside of the patient rooms as a visual tool to allow nursing and other team members to easily identify where team is currently rounding. Every effort should be made to include the nurse in rounds. However, if the bedside nurse or nursing team leader is unable to be present for rounds, the nurse should be called after rounds to review the plan.

Bedside rounds should incorporate the following key FCR elements:–,

Pre-rounds huddle. Although not required for every patient, any member of the team should be able to initiate a pre-rounds huddle. A pre-rounds huddle is simply a quick discussion that occurs outside the patient’s room, often used to discuss diagnostic uncertainty, sensitive issues that cannot or should not be discussed in front of the patient/family, or other considerations based on team discretion.

Greeting and introduction. The assigned team member should address the patient and family by name and confirm rounds participation. If not already done, an explanation of FCR should be provided. The next step is to introduce the team members. This can be kept brief by introducing only the “key” players by name, such as the presenter, senior resident, attending, bedside nurse, and pointing out the rest of the team by titles, such as medical students, residents, pharmacist, and dietitian.

Invite participation and engage in shared decision-making. Invite the patient/family to ask questions, provide clarification, and share information. The patient/family can be engaged by asking for input about specific parts of the plan (e.g., the daily care plan, goals for discharge).

Physical inclusion of patient/family into rounds. Another way to engage the patient and family in the rounds is to arrange the members of the rounding team into a circle that includes the patient and the family members. It is helpful to strategically position the senior resident and/or fellow next to patient/family to more easily allow for presenter to make eye contact and engage both with family and senior team member during the presentation. It is also important to minimize distractions, by silencing pagers and obtaining the patient’s permission to turn off the TV.

Presentation with discussion of assessment and plan for the day. Whenever possible, the assigned presenter should minimize medical jargon during the presentation, recognizing that the use of medical terminology is necessary in certain settings. It is helpful to preface any use of medical terminology by letting families know that the team will summarize the information without medical jargon at the end of the discussion, and provide an opportunity for questions. Back and forth discussion among team members and with families is appropriate and expected in the teaching setting and does not decrease patient/family trust in the medical team. These discussions should engage the patient/family in shared decision-making, and regularly review discharge goals.

Promote efficiency by utilizing a computer to complete work, such as placing orders or sending prescriptions during rounds. A non-presenting team member may utilize the computer on rounds to place patient care orders as they are being discussed so as to avoid delay in care and improve workflow efficiency.

Summarize the plan. One key goal of FCR is to summarize the actionable plan for the day. Whenever possible, the plan should be written down, along with the discharge goals on the whiteboard or on a communication sheet (see Appendix B) to promote shared understanding.

Check-back and closure. FCR should conclude with a check-back and closure, providing an opportunity for patients, family members, and other members of the medical team to ask questions or express concerns. Make a specific plan to return to answer any questions that cannot be addressed during the rounding episode.

Although the procedures and actions that we discuss here are important, they need not be applied rigidly. FCR should be flexible, allowing rounds to be tailored to the individual patient and family’s needs. Patients and families may have unique styles of communication, varying levels of medical literacy, or differing learning styles. Adjustments in provider communication styles to promote patient/family participation may be necessary.

There are special circumstances (see section on “Special Circumstances” below) where bedside rounding may not be appropriate. Providers should use discretion in these scenarios and should still strive to ensure that nursing is still able to participate in rounds. Even when bedside rounding is not appropriate, the team should strive to provide the patient and family with information and employ shared decision-making.

Given the number of participants, unique dynamics of each rounding encounter, and interplay of competing priorities on rounds, there are common barriers and challenges that may contribute to difficulties in performing successful FCR (see section on “Addressing Common Challenges” below).

### Special Circumstances

Some circumstances may not be conducive to FCR. In these situations, FCR may be modified, or an alternative rounding strategy may be required.

Sensitive information or potentially difficult interactions. There are times when the rounding team may feel that bedside rounds are not the most appropriate format for the necessary discussion. Examples of such situations include the following:

- Discussion of difficult news or a new diagnosis (e.g., cancer, new chronic illness, etc.).
- Discussion of information that may be triggering to the patient, such as an eating disorder (e.g., discussing calories and weight) or certain psychiatric conditions (e.g., acute psychosis).
- Difficult social situations (e.g., consideration of filing with Child Protection Services) that warrant discussion in a more private setting.

In these situations, the team may opt to perform hallway rounds without the patient or family present. Alternatively, the team may be able to utilize a pre-rounds huddle to discuss the sensitive information and then complete the rounding encounter with the patient and family present. Regardless of the rounding method, it is important to ensure that the medical information and plan of care are thoroughly communicated to the patient and family. Consider inviting social work to join rounds for any encounters where challenging social situations or discharge circumstances are expected.

Prolonged hospitalization. During prolonged hospitalizations, the patient and family will likely become accustomed to the FCR method and some of the recurring medical details (e.g., the same labs are discussed each day on rounds). In these cases, the presentation can be adjusted accordingly. Full introduction of team members does not need to occur daily, but any new team members should be introduced. In addition, patient and family preferences for rounds participation may change throughout the hospitalization.

Patient age and ability level. A patient’s age and ability level will determine their role in FCR. This should be accounted for early in the hospitalization through discussion of preferences for participation. When ability level allows, teenage/adult patients should decide upon the rounding format, ensuring sensitivity to their preferences regarding timing, location, group size, and family member inclusion. For adult patients, the team should obtain permission prior to rounding in the presence of family or visitors. It is important to ensure that confidentiality regarding sensitive subjects is maintained if rounding in the presence of a parent or family member.

Speak to the patient, rather than to the parent, when it is developmentally appropriate to do so. Consider that adolescents and young adult patients may have confidential matters to discuss with the medical team outside of rounds and without a parent present. When considering the age at which this might be appropriate, note that after age 11, parent proxy access to the MyUofMHealth patient portal becomes limited.

Persons other than parents or legal guardians at bedside. If a visitor at the bedside is not a parent or legal guardian, consider asking the visitor to step out of the room during rounds unless the patient, parent, or guardian specifically requests that they remain at the bedside. Parents, guardians, or adult patients may give verbal consent or may provide written consent via the Friends and Family form to allow for other adult persons to receive medical information. Information should not be shared with a visitor at the bedside unless permission has been granted by patient’s parent/guardian. The Friends and Family form can be found in patient’s chart under the “Media” tab and can be updated by any member of the medical team.

Families unable to be present. If the team is aware, in advance, of a family’s inability to be present, they should consider coordinating a time with the family to be able to call in remotely for rounds. This can be done via calling the family member and placing the phone on speaker phone or via use of HIPAA-compliant video conferencing technology, such as bringing an iPad on rounds and having the family member join via Zoom at a designated time. Alternatively, the family can be updated by phone after rounds. Minimize distractions and avoid using jargon when updating the family over the phone.

Patients/families with limited English proficiency (LEP) or hearing impairment. For LEP patients/families or those who are hard-of-hearing or deaf, utilization of interpreter services is imperative. In-person interpreters are preferred and should be arranged prior to rounds. Telephone or video interpreters are acceptable when in-person services are unavailable.,– In these circumstances, a thorough explanation of FCR is crucial. The team should use discretion to determine whether or not a pre-rounds huddle is beneficial to discuss a care plan prior to rounding, especially when in-person interpreters are not available. It is important to ensure that information and questions are directed to the patient/family, and not the interpreter, maintaining appropriate eye contact with the family.

To arrange an in-person interpreter, place an order in MiChart for “Consult to Interpreter Services.” Then call 734-936-7021 (or dial 6-7021 from in-house line) to confirm availability for the requested time. If no interpreters are available for the requested time, a phone interpreter can be used. Call 1-800-264-1552 and utilize access code 839875.

Isolation/contact precautions. When patients are in isolation/contact precautions, the rounding team should consider minimizing the number of team members entering room. This will help to conserve PPE and minimize exposure. For patients on Contact Precautions, Contact Precautions- Diarrheal, or Droplet Precautions, the team may consider asking the parent to come to the door for rounds while the medical team remains in the hallway. The door may remain opened, if desired. For patients on Airborne Precautions/Respiratory Isolation or Special Pathogens Precautions, the parent/visitor should remain at the bedside and the door must remain closed. In these cases, the team should consider using alternative rounding strategies to ensure the patient and family remain involved in rounds. Options to consider may include small group rounds inside of the patient room, hallway rounds with patient/family on the phone (can utilize room phone for this) or walkie talkie, or “pre-rounds huddle” with the medical team outside of the patient room prior to the minimal number of necessary team members entering the room to complete rounds.

Changes to rounding procedures due to pandemic precautions. Flexibility in rounding procedures is necessary to adhere to institutional infection prevention guidelines. In the event of needing to maintain strict social distance, the number of persons on walk rounds should be kept to a minimum. This can be achieved via “small group FCR” at patient bedside or in the hallway. The hallway setting may be preferred to limit the amount of time spent inside of a patient room. Small group rounding may consist of an attending physician, fellow, if applicable, senior resident, intern, medical student, if applicable, and bedside nurse. Consider utilization of HIPAA-compliant video conferencing technology, such as Zoom, to have the remainder of the team, such as other interns/medical students, pharmacy, or dietitian, virtually present for rounds. A laptop or tablet would need to be taken on rounds to host the video call. When video conferencing technology is utilized, it may be possible to decrease group size even further (for instance, it only requires one team member to take a tablet from patient room to patient room). If video conferencing technology is not utilized, coordination is necessary to have interns and medical students “trade out” when it is time to round on their patient.

When possible, full group walk rounds are preferred to allow for in-person learning, communication with patients/families, and multidisciplinary input. Even on full group walk rounds, the minimum number of necessary participants should physically enter the patient room, with the remainder of the team staying in the hallway. Team members should try to maintain social distance between each other and patients/families at all times, including when in patient rooms. When limiting the number of team members entering a patient room for rounds, discretion should be used to determine how best to involve the rest of the team members in patient care. Consider leaving the patient room door open, if appropriate, so the team in the hallway can listen in to rounds. FCR principles should still be followed, such as ensuring nursing presence, obtaining family rounding preferences or following FCR checklist elements.

### Addressing Common Challenges

Diagnostic dilemma or uncertainty. FCR can be tricky if the members of the rounding team are not sure about how to proceed. In these cases, a “pre-rounds huddle” with the team outside of the patient room (or in the team room) can allow the team to discuss possible ways forward, prior to discussion with the patient and family. It is important to note, however, that discussion of diagnostic dilemma may be appropriate during bedside rounds, is typically expected in a teaching institution, and does not decrease family trust in the medical team.

Time constraints. Completing timely rounds is crucial to patient care. Equitable distribution of time should be attempted during FCR, understanding that on some days some patients/families may require more time than others. Set expectations with families ahead of time regarding rounds timing. If a family has extensive questions, set a specific time to sit down for a discussion with the family instead of attempting to answer all questions on rounds. Attempt to identify ahead of time patients/families that will likely require more time on rounds and prioritize rounding order accordingly.

Distractions. Attempt to minimize distractions by closing the door to the room or turning off the TV. A team member not involved in the presentation should try to answer calls or pages for actively presenting teammates.

Large team sizes. Large team sizes are intrinsic to multidisciplinary rounds in a teaching institution. Introduction of team members is crucial, as large team sizes may feel overwhelming for patients/families, especially those that have never been hospitalized before. Ensure that patient preferences are respected, as some patients may prefer that only a small group enter their room for rounds.

Disagreements between team member, including patients/families. All team members’ viewpoints and preferences should be considered and mutually acceptable plans should be negotiated. At times, this may require further discussions to be held outside of rounds and/or other experts may need to be consulted (i.e., specialists, risk management or social work). As needed, care conferences can be scheduled.

Utilize existing frameworks to actively respond to patients and their families during rounds, including the following examples.

- TWIST framework28: Tune in; What is the emotion?; Insight; Shared mental model; Taking action
- LEARN framework15: Listen; Explain; Acknowledge there may be differences; Recommend (treatment and care); Negotiate: How can we (doctor and patient/family) work together?

Consider inviting social work to rounding encounters where difficulties are anticipated.

Teaching on rounds. Set expectations with the team in advance of rounds of how/when teaching will take place. Ask the patient/family permission prior to teaching in patient room. Involve the patient/family in bedside teaching, keeping teaching pertinent to them and using lay language as much as possible. Focus on real-time and real-life teaching opportunities, such as modeling communication and showing physical exam findings. Focus trainee presentations on clinical reasoning.

#### Strategy for Literature Search

A single search for “family centered rounds” was conducted in Medline (Ovid) database in addition to a Cochrane search. The search was limited to English, pediatrics, and humans. The search retrieved 247 references. The search was performed by the Taubman Health Sciences Library.
