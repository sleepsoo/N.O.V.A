# How Plague Got Rats

## Dedication

## PREFACE

## Introduction

Since the mid-1990s, human relations with animals have been radically transformed. What for decades had been a relation defined by a secure, if anthropocentric, sense of mastery has taken an unexpected turn toward anxiety, uncertainty, and fear. At the heart of this transformation is apprehension about emerging infectious diseases. Anticipation of the next pandemic has placed unprecedented emphasis on zoonoses, or diseases transmissible from nonhuman animals to humans, as a global challenge and indeed as an existential risk to humanity.1 Thirty years and three pandemics later, we live in a world where the epistemic, biopolitical, and symbolic nexus called “zoonosis” is no longer just tightrope-walking between specialized scientific papers and Hollywood blockbusters—it has become a key structuring principle of the way we inhabit, act in, and experience the world.2

Yet zoonosis’s ability to shape the modern world does not simply rely on the epidemiological impact of diseases transmissible from animals to humans; it also—and perhaps more profoundly—relies on the complex sociological and anthropological effects of the idea of zoonosis on our societies. Social scientists have explored this more-than-medical impact of zoonosis on local and global scales so as to highlight the political, symbolic, economic, and ethical implications of approaching nonhuman animals as potential sources of global catastrophe or even human extinction.3 Consumed as we are by present epidemic crises, which seem to abound in the twenty-first century, it is easy to forget that this is not the first time in history the idea of zoonosis has come to transform the world. While zoonosis-induced historical pandemics like the “Black Death” and the “Spanish Flu” have been more often portrayed in the media, and hence rendered recognizable by the general public, the first pandemic that was understood, experienced, and responded to as zoonotic was one that is much less known today: the Third Plague Pandemic. Marking the third time in recorded history when bubonic plague (caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis) erupted as a pandemic, this global epidemiological event broke out in 1894 colonial Hong Kong, after percolating for several decades in the southwest Chinese province of Yunnan. From the British Crown Colony, the pandemic spread across the globe through maritime trade, and by 1900, it affected all inhabited continents for the first time in the history of the disease.4 The pandemic reached its global peak between 1900 and 1910, but it continued to produce significant, tremor-like outbreaks for many decades to come, officially being declared resolved by the World Health Organization (WHO) in 1959. In total the pandemic killed between twelve and fifteen million people.5 While this may sound like a small number in light of the six decades of its course, the impact of the Third Plague Pandemic is recognized by historians as immense, complex, and long-lasting. From significant shifts to public health governance, to the transformation of medicine, architecture, urban planning, and shipping, the pandemic shaped the modern world in ways that continue to reverberate today.6 Most importantly, the Third Plague Pandemic instituted for the first time a link between two relatively new notions at the time: zoonosis and pandemic. Though the idea that some diseases are transmissible from animals to humans predated 1894 by several decades, and while the notion of the “pandemic” had been applied briefly by medical scientists in the discussion of the so-called Russian Flu of 1889–1893, the two notions had never come together before.7 The Third Plague Pandemic was the first epidemiological event to be examined and controlled, as well as experienced by the lay public, as a “pandemic” and as one caused by a zoonotic disease. At the center of this new epistemic as well as biopolitical constellation, so familiar to us today, stood a ubiquitous animal, considered the mammal living in closest proximity to humans across different urban and rural environments worldwide: the rat.

This book examines this pandemic-zoonotic crossroads through the question of “how plague got rats,” or how did a disease that had been experienced and come under intense scientific scrutiny for centuries become associated with an animal that had been equally the subject of widespread experience and dread but not previously linked to plague? The ready-made answer found in TV shows, podcasts, schoolbooks, and institutional histories of science is through scientific discovery. Technoscientific myths, like all mythology, draw much of their symbolic efficacy from establishing origins. Considering Michel Foucault’s critique of historiography, it is not surprising, then, to also have a “miraculous origin” (Wunderursprung) at the basis of the myth of “the conquest of plague.”8 Rats, this story goes, were first identified as hosts of plague in 1894 in Hong Kong by the discoverer of the plague bacterium, the Pasteurian doctor Alexandre Yersin. The story then goes that four years later, in 1898, another Pasteurian doctor, Paul-Louis Simond, discovered in British India that plague was transmitted from rats to humans (by way of fleas). In some alternative, Anglocentric narratives, the plague–rat flea connection was established by the Plague Research Commission in India (1905–1914).9 Singing paeans to colonial science, this technoscientific wonder-origin-myth also contributes to a broader doxa, or belief in, bacteriology as the science that came to cast away confusion and resolve once and for all questions torturing humanity for centuries. It is, in other words, a story that legitimizes colonialism by way of its supposed scientific achievements, and, at the same time, operates as an “epistemological tranquilizer” in so far as it purports plague to have been “Pasteurized” and transformed once and for all in the bacteriological laboratory.10

This book critically unsettles this story, and thus our broader epidemic histories, by examining the emergence and development of scientific approaches to rats as the first animals to be understood as the zoonotic hosts and spreaders of a global pandemic. Taking a critical step away from historical approaches to zoonosis as a process revealed and resolved by bacteriology, the book allows the archive to tell a different story. This is the story not of the discovery of the rat as the sine qua non of plague—a discovery that provided epidemiological clarity and rendered other foggy or confused or simply mistaken framings of plague transmission redundant—but rather it is the story of the ever-shifting entanglements of rats with plague in epidemiological reasoning in the context of a global pandemic. Whereas the traditional story is one of teleological identification, elucidation, and discovery, this is one of situated and contingent relating. In this book, I argue that the idea that plague was related to rats preceded scientific inquiry, and it was subsequently taken up and reworked by scientists, leading to distinct and often competing modes of epidemiological reasoning in response to the Third Plague Pandemic. This book shows that transformations in scientific approaches to the relation between plague and rats reflected not only shifting frameworks of medical knowledge but also distinct political contexts and agendas, as well as broader social imaginaries about human-animal relations. Most importantly, as they were undertaken primarily within colonial contexts, these transformations were entangled with shifting modes of imperial power, bringing together the governance of human and nonhuman “Others” under the bane of the pandemic. In the process of being related, de-related, and re-related in different ways over the course of the Third Plague Pandemic, both plague and rats became reconfigured time and again as scientifically knowable and actionable epidemiological agents. It is only by examining these complex configurations and transformations of “how plague got rats,” and by paying attention to the emic categories of epidemic and pandemic agency applied to and arising therein, that we can understand the development of zoonosis from a specialized notion at the crossroads of medical and veterinary science into a key framework for modern epidemiology. But to start telling this new story, I first need to situate the reader within the broader context of approaches to rats and plague at the time their association began to emerge.

### Rats in the Mid-Nineteenth Century

Whereas rats had long been considered to be detrimental to property and food resources, by the middle decades of the nineteenth century they were still believed to have a singular redeeming characteristic: their supposed disease-free nature.11 Indeed, in England, the rat was considered uniquely able “to ‘clean’ and preserve itself from contamination by the filth and miasma of the sewer.”12 In his groundbreaking study of perceptions of rats at that time, Neil Pemberton notes that, in the opinion of James Rodwell, whose book The Rat (1858) was the first definitive work on the subject, “rather than being correlated with plague, the sewer rat’s appetite for putrefying matter saved human inhabitants from ‘periodical plagues.’”13 Yet the association of rats with non-disease-related destructive traits is not without interest as regards the ways in which it later came to be associated with plague. Indeed it could be argued that preexisting strata of associations between rats and destruction or catastrophe played a significant role in their later medicalization.

#### Vermin

Narrativized, dramatized, and problematized in various ways across the globe since antiquity, by the mid-seventeenth century, rats in Europe and its colonies had come to be included within, and indeed foster, a new way of thinking about certain animals as “vermin.” In Mary Fissell’s now standard definition, these included “animals whom it is largely acceptable to kill,” not because of some inherent characteristic they were supposed to possess but because “they called into question some of the social relations which humans had built around themselves and animals.”14 The relational approach developed by Fissell rhymes with the way in which I approach scientific framings of the association between rats and plague. If we consider “vermin” to form a significant ontological stratum applied to a number of nonhuman animals in early modern European societies, this is because this animal category instituted and problematized a new human-nonhuman relation. On the one hand, it was a relation underlined by destruction or destructiveness, as Fissell notes, of the products of human labor and the means of human subsistence. On the other hand, this relation was underlined by a particular type of agency or intentionality on the part of these animals; one that was seen as prone to confounding human efforts to control them in domestic, commercial, and agricultural settings.15

If Fissell’s approach is useful, it is because it departs from both the structuralist straightjacket of liminality and “matter out of place,” which dominated histories and anthropologies of animals through the 1980s (most famously, perhaps, was Robert Darnton’s work on the great cat massacre in 1730 France), and from Keith Thomas’s functionalist paradigm in reading vermin as animals that were of no use in an increasingly utilitarian modern world.16 Through this analytical perspective, we can begin to understand both the longue durée of necropolitical transformations of specific nonhuman animals (cats and dogs, as well as rats), most famously expressed in “great animal massacres,” and historically perplexing phenomena such as the infamous “vermin trials” of the Renaissance.17 However, more recent studies have opposed Fissell’s idea that what made vermin a threat to “human civility” was their perceived “greed and cunning,” or in more anthropological terms, their “trickster” qualities.18 Lucinda Cole’s Imperfect Creatures argues that “what made vermin dangerous was less their breed-specific cleverness or greed than their prodigious powers of reproduction through which individual appetites took on new, collective power, especially in relation to uncertain food supplies.”19 The two approaches are not mutually exclusive. Indeed, if approached anthropologically, they point to a potential reading of vermin if not as total social facts then at least as what we may call total social threats—something further supported by the association of vermin with vagrancy, the poor, and, as Cole notes in the case of rats, witchcraft.20

More than simply being “pests” or “nuisances,” by the eighteenth century, animals framed as vermin had become associated with embodiments of disgust, revulsion, and even nausea, “indicating the changing attitudes towards body and environment that characterize modern society.”21 In Europe in particular such ideas were further entangled with an increasing association of vermin with ideas of invasiveness.22 Rats became particularly linked to invasiveness through narratives developed by natural historians on the “disappearance” of black rats (Rattus rattus) in Europe as a result of the mass invasion of the continent by brown, or so-called Norwegian, rats (Rattus norvegicus) across the Volga in the 1720s, setting the ground for what Paul Cowdell has identified as xenophobic configurations of rats as immigrants and vice versa.23 This clustering of negative and disconcerting agencies of rats as vermin is well reflected in the entry on the animal (Mus) in the second edition of the Encyclopaedia Britannica from 1781: “The rattus, or common rat, is the most pernicious of any of our smaller quadrupeds. Meat, corn, paper, cloaths, furniture, in short every conveniency of life, is a prey to this destructive creature. Nor are its devastations confined to these; for it will make equal havock among poultry, rabbits, or young game; nay, it hath been known to gnaw the extremities of infants when asleep.”24 The entry further denotes rats as “licentious” and “mischievous,” citing their great reproductive capacity in spite of poisons, cats, and traps used against them, and their propensity to turn cannibalistic when lacking other food. This verminous narrative reached its apex in the entry’s discussion of the replacement of the black rat by the brown, where it lamented that “it is to be feared we shall reap little benefit by the exchange; for the Norway rat hath the same disposition, with greater abilities for doing mischief than the common kind.”

#### Gothified Rats

The beginning of the nineteenth century saw another layer of significance added to rats, as Romanticism and in particular its gothic variants came to endow these animals with renewed mischievous and nefarious agencies. In his monograph dedicated to the cultural history of the rat, Jonathan Burt notes that the gothification of the animal involved what we may call a dialectical transformation of its destructiveness where quantitative scaling up quickly assumes a qualitative impact: the “rapacity, appetites, breeding abilities and adaptability” of rats rendering them “world-devouring.”25 Gothic configurations of animals more broadly often relied upon the metonymic transformation into forces of nature, seen in Romantic imaginations as in a constant, to-the-death struggle with humanity.26 At the same time, as Ruth Heholt and Melissa Edmundson have noted, gothic narratives heavily invested in and fanned anxieties about “‘animal nature’ residing deep in the human,” often in a dormant state, as both an Other and the essence of “the self in all of us.”27 In this sense, what Burt coins the “dark vitality” of rats should not be psychologically reduced to an aspect of their universal qua ahistorical uncanniness or abjection, but it should rather be seen as a historically situated approach to a specific crisis of subjectivation and human-nonhuman mastery and their interrelation.28 As Pemberton evocatively puts it, rats were rendered into “a species co-existing and co-emerging with civilization, devouring it from within,” thus assuming for the first time what we may call a world-historical agency.29

At the same time, in the course of the nineteenth century, gothified rats played important roles as ways of commenting on life in rapidly industrialized cities. Historians and literary studies scholars have examined the role of rats in English and North American literature in great detail.30 Matthew Crofts and Janine Hatter have argued that the gothification of rats involved their transformation into indexes of horror, neglect, and destitution.31 Mid-nineteenth-century headlines on rats devouring babies in their cradles were tales that became increasingly entangled with imperatives of social, economic, and moral “improvement.”32 “Victorian fiction,” in particular, “was steeped in rhetoric that associated rats and the working classes together as the strata of society that needed most improving.”33 Gothification, Crofts and Hatter argue, rendered rats into “signifiers of truth, bringing things to the surface that others would have kept buried.”34 In stories and headlines of rat-related horrors, rats functioned as a “device” that revealed and exposed underlying social evils with the purpose of their reform and correction, usually in ways that involved class, racial, and gender discrimination and stigmatization.35 Rats, in other words, were canaries in the proverbial coal mines of urban industrial class warfare. Hence, while rats remained universal animals, infesting both urban and rural settings, their moralization in the course of the nineteenth century forged significant links with cities as the milieu par excellence of transgression, moral decay, and social trouble.

#### Convivial Rats

Were rats then, by the mid-nineteenth century, unilaterally approached in Euro-American contexts as enemies of humanity or as tropes for revealing and debating social problems? New literature on these animals suggests otherwise. At the same time they were being verminized, gothified, and moralized, rats were also involved in more ambivalent relations with humans, which often retained a relative autonomy from the above frameworks. Studies of English reformer and journalist Henry Mayhew’s London Labour and the London Poor (1851) have demonstrated the complex character of the rat in mid-nineteenth-century English settings. In Mayhew’s narrative, brown rats are shown to have been an important source of income for large numbers of ratcatchers and manufacturers and sellers of poisons, and also for the blood sport known as “rat baiting.”36 Pemberton’s magisterial analysis of Mayhew’s narrative on rats has shown how practices like rat-catching and rat-baiting involved complex multispecies relations (also involving dogs and ferrets), forms of labor, subjectivities, sensibilities, and moralities. “Attention to rat-human entanglements in Mayhew’s London Labour,” Pemberton notes, “reveals the ill-defined and hybrid character of urban nature/culture and the attendant politics of multispecies urban life, demonstrating how humans, rats and the urban environments of the London poor were bound in complex relations and mutual becomings.”37

Kaori Nagai’s recent studies of human-rat relations have come to further underline ambivalent approaches to rats across the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. Nagai has shown that prior to them being connected to plague, “rats were something of a ship’s institution.” Nagai adds that “though the number of rats had to be kept in check by the ship’s cats and other means, such efforts were part of tolerating, and living with, rats on board ship.”38 This does not mean that maritime spaces constituted an exception to the vermin-oriented configuration of rats. Rather, the problem of operating in a closed system, such as a cargo boat, which was infested by these animals posed a problem of an order that required a different approach to human-nonhuman mastery. Whereas fumigation, poisoning, and rat-catching were regularly used to stem rat populations when these were considered to be out of control, on day-to-day bases, Nagai argues, a different human-rat relation prevailed. Approached as sui generis seafarers, with an intimate, even sagacious “knowledge of the ship’s condition” (manifested in them being the first to abandon ship when it was about to sink), rats were “accepted as fellow travelers, and the troubles they caused were part of the package that humans gracefully had to endure and deal with in exchange for having their company.”39 Nagai notes that James Rodwell also pointed at this nonverminous agency when he spoke of “the reasoning powers of the rat,” whereupon the animals “were trusted not to do any damage to the ship that would compromise their own safety at sea.”40 Rather than being ontologically pitched against one another as enemies, rats and humans formed complex, if also ambiguous, forms of intimacy and even “companionship” and “care” on board ships.41 Nagai’s studies hence confirm and enrich long-standing anthropological approaches, which have argued that “conflict between humans and animals does not come without simultaneous recognition of continuity and commonality.”42 Indeed, following Brian Morris’s approach to animals, Birgitta Edelman has argued that “human-rat relations might be one of the most extreme examples of such ambiguity.”43 While this approach is well established in historical and anthropological examinations of rats in non-Western contexts, it is important to keep in mind that such complexities and ambivalences were also in place in the mid-nineteenth-century industrialized West.44

#### How Plague Did Not Get Rats

It is within the timeframe of the abovementioned transformations of what we may call the broader ontology of the rat that the first observations linking ratfalls—as the mass death or epizootic of rats is known—to plague were made. Rather than being a discovery by medical scientists, this was an association made by Indigenous villagers in the Indian Himalayas. Yet instead of leading colonial medical doctors operating in the region to perceive the relation between rats and plague, it rather involved them in a complex epistemic process aimed at explaining how even rats could be affected by the disease. The way Indigenous observations of a consistent relation between plague and rats were approached by mid-nineteenth-century British colonial doctors is the best indication we have of the epistemic difficulty in connecting rats with plague inherent to Western medicine at the time.

As I have discussed elsewhere in detail, by the 1840s British colonial doctors in the Indian Himalayan provinces of Kumaon and Garhwal had noted that Indigenous communities abandoned their villages and ran to the hills once ratfalls were observed, as they associated these with outbreaks of “Mahamari,” a disease that British doctors identified as bubonic plague.45 At first, colonial doctors ridiculed Indigenous communities, accusing them of superstition. Following colonial racialized framings of the time, the colonial doctors also used this story to illustrate beliefs in the contagious nature of plague as being inherently irrational—a popular approach at the time, following decades of bitter debate about whether plague was contagious or not.46 However, by the 1850s, a new team of colonial doctors came to recognize the value of Indigenous knowledge and accepted that ratfalls did indeed precede outbreaks of Mahamari.47 C. R. Francis and Frank Pearson, the two physicians pioneering this approach, even went so far as to undertake the first-known necropsy of a rat in association with plague. Finding the lungs of the animal punctuated by “several black carbonaceous-looking patches,” Francis and Pearson noted the similarity of these symptoms with the human pathology observed in ten autopsied cases.48 Rather than concluding from this that plague was transmissible from rats to humans, they maintained miasmatic frameworks that saw the disease as being “endemic” in the region and emanating from the soil.49 If thousands of rats managed to “breed and thrive” in places as filthy as the London and Paris sewers, Francis and Pearson reasoned, it was evident “that the so-called poison of ‘Mahamurree’ must be a peculiarly virulent one, to destroy a class of animals which select the foulest atmosphere in nature for their abode.”50 What rats dying of plague proved to these colonial doctors was the extreme concentration of the disease in the specific location, which led even such animals as rats, those habituated to the “foulest atmosphere,” to succumb to it. Thus ratfalls became an emblem, and at the same time an index, of the exceptional and indeed anomalous pestigenic conditions prevailing in the affected locality. These were explained by Francis and Pearson as a result of Indigenous ways of living, which in their colonial medical imagination led to an extreme “vitiation of the air,” so much so that Indigenous houses were compared to “a brewer’s vat” saturated by carbonic acid—a miniature of the Black Hole of Calcutta or the Neapolitan Grotta del Cane, where CO2 rendered the blood of the inhabitants “vitiated” in turn.51

In my detailed examination of this Indigenous-colonial encounter, I stressed how the recognition of Indigenous knowledge by colonial doctors led to more intrusive and violent measures of epidemic containment than previously put in place on the basis of an assumed Indigenous ignorance.52 Rather than focusing on the biopolitical aspects of this case, here I want to underline its epistemic import. This is because the way in which colonial doctors framed the relation between plague and rats took place at a time when medical frameworks of zoonosis were in full bloom across Europe. Rather than being an exotic case at the margins of medical science, Mahamari poses a key question: Why, when a series of diseases were becoming approached as transmissible from animals to humans and a broader framework of zoonosis, as a new class of diseases, was emerging in Europe, did the association between rats and plague in the Indian Himalayas not lead plague to be approached as a zoonosis?

### Plague in the Mid-Nineteenth Century

The years between the conventional end of the Second Plague Pandemic (the 1720 epidemic in Marseille or, for some authors, the 1771 epidemic in Moscow) and the start of the Third Plague Pandemic is the period least studied by historians of the disease. Although there exist excellent studies of individual plague outbreaks in this period, no study has attempted a global history of plague between the end of the eighteenth century and the end of the nineteenth century, or of scientific approaches to the disease in this period. Such study is beyond the scope of this book. What I would like to briefly offer instead is a hypothesis regarding why plague was not included in the class of zoonotic diseases during the decades when this medical category emerged and became established in European medical literature (1840s–1860s). To do this I will first draw the outlines of the formation of this new medical framework, initially known as “diseases transmissible from animals to humans” and then coined “zoonosis,” and then I will discuss what I propose as the obstacles involved in plague being included therein, even when an association with rats had been shown, in the case of Mahamari, by the end of the 1840s.

#### Zoonosis

By the end of the 1830s, developments in medical and veterinary science had led to questions arising regarding the links between an increasing number of diseases in animals and humans.53 Most important among these was glanders, a disease affecting horses that can be transmitted to humans with horrifying and fatal results.54 In France, in particular, by 1837 questions around the “contagiousness” of glanders between horses and from horses to humans had assumed the dimensions of a full-blown scientific and governmental debate under the auspices of the July Monarchy’s Ministry of War, which was becoming increasingly concerned over the impact of the disease on the cavalry.55

By the mid-1850s doctoral theses in medicine discussed not just the “contagiousness” of rabies, anthrax, and glanders from animals to humans but also a new category of diseases “transmissible from animals to humans” (les maladies transmissibles des animaux à l’homme).56 In most cases, for a disease to be classed under this new concept, it had to meet two fundamental conditions: First, it had to be identical in animals and humans, being caused by the same “contagium.” Second, animals had to be the source of human infection, something that excluded a third (i.e., environmental) source affecting both animals and humans. Moreover this class of diseases was set apart from “poisons” (venin) in terms of two criteria. First, that diseases caused the same symptoms in the animals where they originated and in humans, whereas poisons caused no symptoms in their hosts.57 Second, that whereas poisons only caused immediate, and hence limited, damage to their recipient, diseases derived from animals were able to “undergo important modifications” in the course of transmission and to “regenerate themselves indefinitely,” hence causing a chain of contagion.58 Rather than marking a “discovery,” Rudolf Virchow’s introduction of the term “zoonosis” in 1855 thus came to consolidate more than two decades of developments around this new idea, which by that time had become widespread in medical and veterinary literature across continental Europe.59

In the second half of the 1860s, among the many diseases whose inclusion within this new category was hotly debated by medical and veterinary experts, trichinosis became the first to indict rats as potential carriers of an infectious disease.60 Plague, by contrast, was absolutely absent from all discussion of zoonosis, in German, French, Italian, or English publications at the time, including up to 1894 when the first monograph on this class of diseases was published.61 Discussing in detail the conditions that were sufficient for the inclusion of other diseases but not plague in this category is beyond the scope of this introduction. Instead I will briefly propose a hypothesis for future elaboration and testing, keeping in mind that its acceptance or not is not a condition for the history of “how plague got rats” proposed in this book.

#### Conditions of Improbability

In the midst of the nineteenth century, most of the diseases included in the class of zoonosis were believed to be miasmatic, or to emanate from the soil or other putrefying material, with some medical scientists also maintaining that certain diseases could be generated spontaneously inside animal bodies.62 Thus the fact that, by the 1840s (following half a century of bitter debates), plague had become stabilized as a miasmatic disease cannot be said to be the reason it was not included alongside glanders or anthrax in this new disease ontology that saw no contradiction between the miasmatic source of zoonoses and their contagious spread between species. Instead, my hypothesis is that what rendered plague a bad fit for this medical framework was that “zoonosis” emerged in response to specific needs for and conditions of mastery that were not applicable to plague at the time. If, by the 1850s, diseases as different as glanders, rabies, or anthrax could be grouped together as zoonoses, this was because the reinterpretation of “contagion” as an interspecies phenomenon had become a field that allowed bringing together the governance of humans and animals by way of new forms of mastering human-nonhuman relations. Indeed rendering rabies, glanders, and anthrax scientifically knowable and actionable under the shared question of zoonosis permitted not only an epistemic but also a biopolitical exchange and entanglement between questions of urban governance (rabies), meat and wool production (anthrax), and military power and colonial expansion (glanders). As a new disease category that became particularly prevalent in the political context following the eruption of the 1848 revolutions across Europe, zoonosis thus allowed bringing together distinct spheres of governance, testing and sharing techniques, and technologies of mastery. Most importantly, it allowed for a reconceptualization of human-animal relations through the triple axis of public order, industrialization, and military might. Following this hypothesis, while diseases like trichinosis could come under the mid-nineteenth-century prism of zoonosis to the extent that they contributed to this epistemic-biopolitical nexus, plague, as it was understood at the time, did not meet the necessary criteria of inclusion.

This was because, at the time, plague as a governmental problem for mid-century European powers was synonymous with the question of quarantine—a largely autonomous epistemic and biopolitical sphere with well-developed technologies and techniques of governance, which did not require its reacticulation within a new class of diseases.63 Plague did not concern mid-century Europe as a “native” problem, with works studying the disease in manners other than quarantine (very limited in the 1840s–1870s) being focused on plague “foyers,” or areas of endemicity, in faraway regions such as Mesopotamia.64 With the exception of the Indian Himalayas, none of these regions were under European colonial control, rendering the governance of plague insignificant by comparison to problems like rabies, anthrax, or glanders, which struck at the heart of daily life, production, and governance in Europe and which required or fostered in-depth biopolitical innovation and management.65 As for the Himalayas, or other colonial regions, long-established ideas of plague as a disease of “filth” formed adequate grounds for the purposes of governing plague, more often than not by burning down Indigenous houses.66

At the same time, by the 1850s, the development of the concepts of the “Black Death” and “Oriental Plague” had led to an exoticization and racialization of the disease, which set it apart from the more mundane and, at the same time universal, image of the diseases making up the class of zoonosis. Plague was more like cholera in this way.67 While there is no space to discuss the long process of the Orientalization of plague in its complexity, it is important to follow Nükhet Varlık in noting that this rhymed with broader approaches of the Ottoman Empire as “the sick man of Europe” and its increasingly epidemiological inflections, which reached their apex in the mid-century International Sanitary Conferences.68 If “modern epidemiology, in its inception, was closely entwined with the study of the Orient” and the Ottoman Empire in particular, plague became the topos par excellence for forging together pathologizations of “Oriental” spaces and races.69 At the same time, what Lori Jones has called “historically plagued geographies” were not simply what anthropologists call geographies of epidemic blame but also ones that overdetermined the ontology of plague.70 By the mid-1830s, adopted by Justus Hecker in his best-selling The Black Death in the Fourteenth Century (1832), “Oriental plague” came to be intricately woven together with the gothification of the disease.71 Emerging by the beginning of the nineteenth century to find its most developed form in Hecker’s book, the idea of the Black Death (der schwarze Tod, referring to the first, brutal years of the Second Plague Pandemic, 1347–1351), signaled a turning point in the historiography of plague, as it transformed the disease into a world-historical and world-catastrophic agent. The Black Death forged a new epidemiological imagination where humanity’s encounter with diseases assumed the role of an epic existential battle. In her examination of the emergence of this epidemiological, literary, and historical trope, Faye Marie Getz has argued that, for Hecker, “The Black Death was a demonstration of the power and glory of Nature, so overwhelming in its universality and its terror as to defeat the best efforts of mere science to define it.”72 This vision was key to the transformation of plague under the sign of “gothic epidemiology,” which in combination with the Orientalization of the disease rendered it into a sui generis register—a disease with no par or sibling, which formed a category on its own, hence making its inclusion with diseases that were primarily seen from a veterinary lens, like glanders or anthrax, both symbolically and epistemically improbable.73

#### A New Epidemic History

In examining how rats came to be associated with plague, this book provides a vital new history of how epidemiological reasoning about zoonosis developed as a scientifically and politically contested ground in the context of a global pandemic. Insofar as this is a history of epidemiological epistemology, I follow Hans-Jörg Rheinberger in taking the latter to mean an investigation whose question is not “how knowing subjects might attain an undisguised view of their objects” (in this case the relation between rats and plague) but rather “what conditions had to be created for objects to be made into objects for empirical knowledge under historically variable conditions.”74 However, what I, as an anthropologist, take to be the field of scientific—in this case epidemiological—investigation, configuration, and practice is not so much about “objects” as relations. Acknowledging the “diffuseness” and ever-slippery nature of the term “relations,” the leading anthropological theorist, Marilyn Strathern, notes that these “are ubiquitous in the accounts people give of their world, and no less in the observations or theories by which any kind of knowledge is made.”75 Functioning as a much-needed counterweight to profoundly ethnocentric Latourian approaches, which tend to reduce and attenuate everything into “networks” and “actants,” Strathern’s anthropology has encouraged generations of social scientists to develop ethnography as a practice of “knowledge-making that does not simply seek out associations and dissociations across phenomena but imagines and describes them as relations.”76 And at the same time, it has inspired us to pay close attention to the ways such knowledge-making is undertaken by our ethnographic (including historical-ethnographic) participants.

My approach to “how plague got rats” is rooted in this anthropological tradition, in that it approaches epidemiological reasoning as a practice of imagining, investigating, and acting on the plague as an epidemiological relation. Rather than being a process of elucidating the link between plague and rats, epidemiological reasoning is here approached as a theory and practice that forged different, often more-than-medical, relations between them (and not only them), and in the process of doing so transformed both plague and rats as epistemic, biopolitical, and symbolic fields, and epidemiology as a science. In my investigation of these transformations, I follow Ludwik Fleck in assuming that “different and incommensurable views of a given disease can exist not only diachronically in different cultures, but also synchronically within the same culture” or system of medical thought.77 But whereas Fleck attributed these differences to scientific “thought-style,” or an “intellectual preparedness or readiness for one particular way of seeing and acting and no other,” in this work I see them as agonistic forms of epidemiological reasoning that are articulated within, and are in turn the conditions of, broader projects for mastery.78 For readers unfamiliar with my work, I here need to briefly explain what I mean by “epidemiological reasoning” and “mastery.”

Lukas Engelmann has taken the Third Plague Pandemic as a paradigmatic historical ground to develop his notion of “epidemiological reasoning,” noting that the latter “set the groundwork for the development of epidemiology as a unique scientific practice, at a remove from the clinic and the laboratory, but dedicated to colligating an endless array of material, social and biological aspects.”79 Over several groundbreaking studies, Engelmann has shown how epidemiological reasoning transformed ideas of infectious diseases, and public health approaches to them, by instituting new epidemic and pandemic ontologies, chronotopes, causalities, and responsibilities.80 Rather than being reducible to a “theory” or a “narrative,” epidemiological reasoning must accordingly be seen as a dynamic process of rendering infectious diseases scientifically knowable and actionable. In my own work, which has been in productive dialogue and collaboration with that of Engelmann, I have stressed the importance of the dialectics of visibility and invisibility in the formation of the agonistic field of epidemiological reasoning, and also the need to consider reasoning in tandem with, first, epidemiological imagination, or what I have called epidemic/pandemic imaginaries, and, second, epidemic governance.81 By “pandemic imaginaries,” I mean not simply the ways in which epidemics or pandemics are imagined, or what older anthropological approaches would have called “epidemic cultures,” but rather how they are configured in relation to questions, anxieties, and aspirations relating to the human condition, civilization, society, and history. In this sense, pandemic imaginaries need to be seen as not simply instituted within and by specific societies or cultures but also as instituting these.82 By “epidemic governance,” I do not simply mean the ways in which states or empires controlled epidemics but primarily the governmentalities and technologies of power (biopolitical, necropolitical, or other) developed during epidemics, and how these brought together and in tension the governance of humans and nonhumans. Rather than epidemiological reasoning, pandemic imaginaries, and epidemic governance forming three distinct nodes—epistemological, cultural, and political—what I argue in this book is that they develop and work indivisibly together in instituting the epidemic and the pandemic as more-than-medical categories and experiences. And in doing so, they interinstitute the conditions of mastery of specific diseases and epidemics as well as epidemics/pandemics as overarching social and interspecies phenomena.

Throughout my anthropological and historical investigations of plague, zoonosis, and epidemics/pandemics more broadly, I have adopted Walter Benjamin’s approach to mastery as a key technoscientific project of modernity, in which framings of zoonosis have played an important, if often historically and anthropologically neglected, role. For Benjamin, this was a project not so much about what Theodor W. Adorno and Max Horkheimer more famously problematized as a “mastery of nature” (Naturbeherrschung) as it was about the mastery of the relation between nature and humankind.83 How is this notion helpful in drawing a new history of epidemiology, and in particular of scientific approaches to plague as a zoonotic disease? First, by not being nonreducible to a teleological relationship, Benjamin’s nuanced notion of mastery allows us to appreciate historically instituted, agonistic intercompositions and intertransformations of human and nonhuman agents in their indivisible relating. The lens of mastery thus allows us to approach “how plague got rats” from an angle that is critically distanced from recent historical and anthropological turns pivoted upon the notion of “animal agency” while at the same time taking emic notions of rat agency historically and ethnographically seriously. For while it may be tempting to approach “animal agency” as a driving epistemological principle in the formation of scientific knowledge about rats’ relation to plague, such focus risks internalizing colonial and racialized narratives around the rat. As we will see in the course of the book, these narratives imputed agency onto rats precisely so that these animals could become actionable under ever-tighter regimes of technoscientific mastery. To uncritically adopt “animal agency” as an analytical pivot would thus be akin to thinking with the grain of mastery, like the architect of San Francisco’s war against plague and rats, Rupert Blue, who saw human-rat relations as “a battle between the intelligence of the one [humans] and the instinct of the other [rats] with the advantage not always on the side of the former.”84 By contrast, a critical focus on mastery allows us to approach rats’ agency as a contested, emic category at the heart of the colonial project, which fostered ever-newer studies, technologies, policies, and interventions aimed at controlling an animal that was configured as a sui generis uncontrollable menace.

Second, as projects for mastery are not reducible to a relation of domination or control, we are encouraged to consider that the epidemiological approaches under examination in this book worked only insofar as they “constantly came apart, broke down, and got jammed by conflicting political outlooks, financial interests, and epistemic investments and aporias.”85 This means that we can examine the iterations and transformations of the ways in which rats became related to plague without assuming that these somehow progressed toward a fuller, more comprehensive or totalizing “control” of rats or of human-rat relations, let alone toward a more complete or perfected scientific knowledge of plague as a zoonotic disease.

By examining how the project for mastering plague as a zoonotic disease unfolded around the interinstituted nodes of epidemiological reasoning, pandemic imaginaries, and epidemic governance, in this book I hope to trouble the history of epidemics so as to showcase a long process of epistemological emergence and transformation. Unfolding principally in colonial contexts of intense imperial antagonism, the process of plague becoming configured as a zoonotic disease forces us to critically reflect on received concepts and methods of approaching epidemic and pandemic diseases as well as nonhuman animals as epidemic/pandemic villains. And at the same time, by showcasing the ambivalent and aporetic ways in which zoonosis became entangled, for the first time, with a pandemic, this book highlights that the history of modern scientific thinking and practice cannot be reduced to the “bacteriological revolution” or any other master narrative of discovery but needs to be studied instead as a dynamic field of multispecies relations.

#### Methodological Note

In researching and writing this book I have adopted a method of examining sources that brings history and anthropology together in what we may call a synergetic tension. Being an anthropologist, I conceive of my research into relations past in terms of historical ethnography. The aim of my investigation into “how plague got rats” has hence been to explore the ways in which my historical “participants” (in this case, primarily medical scientists) understood and acted upon the relation between plague and rats, and how understandings of both plague and rats were in turn shaped by their relating, de-relating, and re-relating. In my research more broadly, I have opted to go beyond the dichotomy of reading archives “along” and “against” the grain. As both approaches are essential for the work of historical anthropology, giving priority to one over the other may have produced an interesting debate more than fifteen years ago, but it feels moot today.86 I thus implicitly adopt an approach that follows Ann Laura Stoler in reading the archive “along the grain,” by taking my “informants” ethnographically seriously and investigating the “epistemic anxieties” in place.87 Yet, at the same time, I follow Walter Benjamin in “brush[ing] history against the grain,” in the sense that, while taking note of the doxa of discovery inherent to medical narratives, I am interested in underlining other processes in place, particularly ones that do not tally with, or tell a tale of, scientific revelation.88

I furthermore need to note a methodological heuristic employed in this study: For the most part, I have treated archival sources and published sources differently in terms of their epistemic value as evidence. I have taken archival sources (by which, for the purposes of this study, I mean manuscripts, notebooks, logs, letters, diaries, etc.) as reflecting more closely ideas, experiences, or concerns of their authors, whereas published sources are taken as evidence of self-presentation or of what the author may have wanted to express about a subject to a particular audience at a particular point in time. This interior-exterior distinction is not of course absolute as, for example, I fully recognize that unpublished letters may indeed be sources reflecting exterior motive rather than internal reflection. Still, when it comes to comparing a published source that describes an experiment and the unpublished notes of that experiment, my method is to accept the latter as reflecting more closely what the author experienced as taking place (if not what actually did take place) or the experimenter’s thoughts about the experiment (what Ludwick Fleck called the “living practice” of science), while the former as a public-facing rendition of these facts (what Fleck called “the official paper form” of science), which, as we will see in the case of Paul-Louis Simond in chapter 3, may go as far as fabrication.89 Rather than using archives to debunk publications, or publications to complete archives, my interest here is to bring different sources in tension with one another, so as, first, “to demarcate the logic of research from the logic of representation” and, second, to approximate more closely what we could call a historical ethnography.90 In the case of Simond, this allows us to understand what the Pasteurian doctor decided to do with his failed rat flea experiment in light of pressure from his superiors.

This method is, however, faced with a practical limitation. Due to the state of archival holdings, it is not always possible to have access to both published and archival sources on a single event or process. More often than not, only published accounts survive. In such cases, it is customary in histories of science and medicine to treat these as evidence of actual events or experiments. To an extent, this is a valid approach when it comes to published accounts of events or practices (for example, rat-catching operations in a particular location) that were witnessed by people other than the author of the publication in question, who could (as is indeed the case sometimes) contest the details as described by the author. It is less so the case when there are few witnesses, or when these witnesses are dependent on the author of the account or have no access that would permit them to publicly correct or contradict the author. I have examined such a case elsewhere, where a false account of a plague outbreak in 1898 Nha Trang was published by Alexandre Yersin, the discover of the plague bacillus, in order to protect himself both from local accusations of bearing responsibility for the spread of the disease and from his own bad conscience.91 The acceptance of published accounts as accurate representations is particularly problematic when it comes to experiments that were conducted by a single individual or by a very small group of people who would be institutionally bound to agree with the official account provided by the lead scientist. Archives, such as experimental notes, diaries, or letters, which may be able to enrich or trouble such representations, do not survive in many cases. This is particularly true for plague studies and experiments by British medical scientists, for which such sources are extremely scarce. Whereas the Institut Pasteur (a key institution in the study of plague) affords historians with rich and complex personal archives of plague researchers, like Simond or Yersin, the archival holdings of British plague experts, like William J. Simpson or William Glen Liston, are mostly limited to official documents. This results in an asymmetry in the quality of archives available for a global history of the Third Plague Pandemic and for the history of “how plague got rats.” While in the case of Pasteurian doctors we can perform in-depth research down to their private concerns, aspirations, and doubts, following their epidemiological reasoning week by week, and in some cases even day by day, in the case of British doctors researching plague, we mostly have access to their publicly expressed opinions, and in some cases those expressed to their superiors with whom they corresponded or to whom they reported. Only in extremely rare cases can we locate a trace or what, following Hans-Jörg Rheinberger, we may call a “splinter” of private opinion or thinking, but never the day-to-day development of their reasoning about plague.92 This marks an important methodological, narrative, and analytical challenge regarding the story of “how plague got rats” if one does not want to reproduce a spectacle of smooth, uninterrupted global-historical knowledge. As it is not always possible to mark the shift of register from “archive and publication” to “only publication,” patient readers will be rewarded by referring back to this book’s copious endnotes where the lack of, for example, experimental notes, is noted and limitations of interpretation are highlighted.

#### Organization and Structure

The book’s first chapter examines the emergence of the first way of connecting rats with plague in the course of the Third Plague Pandemic. Discussing 1870s Yunnan, at the time a remote province of the Qing Empire, the chapter shows how this connection was developed in French missionary works during a devastating plague epidemic that worked together with a massive Islamic rebellion in challenging the Catholic mission in the region. The chapter focuses on the figure of the “staggering rat,” or the notion that rats affected by plague come out into the open to perform a danse macabre before dying. I argue that the “staggering rat” emerged as an apocalyptic trope aimed at making sense of what missionaries experienced as an all-consuming catastrophe and at proposing colonization and conversion as a remedy. The chapter further examines how this trope was received and adapted into subsequent medical works discussing rats as plague copatients (early 1880s to early 1890s) in South China and in the Indian Himalayas, and how bacteriological frameworks became applied to and transformed the figure of the “staggering rat.”

I then proceed by examining the development of scientific approaches to the relation between plague and rats in the context the devastating epidemics of the disease in Hong Kong (1894, 1896) and in British India (1896–1899), which chronologically followed the discovery of plague’s causative bacterium (1894). Challenging established histories that assume that the bacteriological discovery of plague led to an exclusive focus on rats as its host, in chapter 2, I argue that the rat only emerged as an object of colonial medical interest in its indivisible relation to other “plague agents,” such as humans, clothes, grain, and the soil. The chapter follows research by, and debates between, international plague experts, commissions, and public health officers in colonial Hong Kong and India, as well as the lay public’s ideas about the relation between rats and plague. I use anthropological theory to argue that, between 1894 and 1899, the rat was transformed from a copatient of plague into an “epidemiological dividual.” In other words, a plague agent that could be scientifically understood and acted upon only in relation to other plague agents. The chapter explores the impact of this configuration on antiplague measures, and on the pathologization of the ways of life of Indigenous, colonized communities.

Chapter 3 examines one of the most famous and celebrated experiments in the history of epidemiology: the experiment conducted in India by the Pasteurian doctor Paul-Louis Simond (June 1898) showing that plague is transmitted by rat fleas. Using archival material hitherto overlooked by historians, the chapter shows that the experiment was in fact botched and the publication claiming the discovery of the transmission pathway was a product of scientific falsification. Examining Simond’s rat and flea experiments within their colonial context, I investigate his reasons for undertaking these experiments and for falsifying their results. Through an examination of Simond’s manuscripts, I show that he was immersed within colonial frameworks of the rat as an epidemiological dividual. I argue that Simond’s attempt to identify rat fleas as the true pathway of plague was the result of institutional pressure. In particular, I show that deciding to claim the discovery of the rat flea as plague’s vector was a result of Simond’s failure to achieve the real aim of his “India Mission”: to prove the efficacy of the Institut Pasteur’s plague serum in the context of intense imperial antagonism over the production and marketing of a plague prophylactic.

I then proceed by examining the reception of Simond’s experiment by the international scientific community, showing that acceptance was far from unanimous, with the majority of plague experts at the time rejecting Simond’s research. Chapter 4 follows debates between scientists, paying particular attention to attempts to replicate Simond’s experiment in laboratories in France, Australia, Italy, and Austria. I argue that these attempts at experimental replication deeply troubled Pasteurian approaches to plague, instituting rat fleas as liminal epistemic objects situated at the threshold of bacteriology and parasitology. I examine the undecidable role of rat fleas in the transmission of plague between 1899 and 1905, arguing that suspending the insects between the status of a vehicle and that of a true vector or intermediary host of the disease led to a productive epistemological limbo. Paying attention to the heated debates and messy experiments surrounding the replication of Simond’s 1898 study allows us to appreciate the dynamic role of rat fleas in the transformation of scientific approaches to the relation between plague and rats, which cannot be reduced to a teleological tale of discovery.

In chapter 5, I examine the development of the “no rats, no plague” doctrine between 1900 and 1910, or the idea that there can be no plague without rats, asking how this doctrine became universally accepted by 1905 in the absence of scientific evidence. I argue that the third iteration of “how plague got rats” took place as a result of this doctrine’s economic, political, and symbolic efficacy when applied to the control and containment of plague. Drawing on examples from across the globe, I show that this related to the development and application of rat-control technologies. Some of these (maritime fumigation) promised the abolition of costly quarantines, while others (rat-poisoning, rat-proofing, and rat-catching) brought together the governance of humans and animals in new ways, particularly in colonial contexts. At the same time, I show that the transformation of the rat into a pandemic villain relied upon histories that proclaimed the plague-rat relation to have been known to humans since ancient times. I thus argue that the rat emerged as a plague protagonist not due to any scientific breakthrough but as a result of the synergy between acute economic, political, and symbolic needs arising at the peak of the Third Plague Pandemic.

I then return to British India to examine scientific research on rats and fleas under the Plague Research Commission (1905–1915). I show that, on the one hand, the commission’s research retroactively validated the already universally accepted role of rats in the spread of the disease, while, on the other hand, also came to resolve the question of the role of rat fleas in plague transmission. Chapter 6 examines debates between the commission and its detractors, investigating the casuistic epidemiological reasoning of William Glen Liston and the impact of his and Major Bannerman’s godown and house experiments with fleas and guinea pigs in colonial Bombay (Mumbai). The chapter then examines experiments by Bacot and Martin, which led to the identification of the “blocked flea” as an epidemiological mechanism that resolved the suspension of rat fleas between a status of vehicle/carrier and vector/intermediary host of plague. I argue that this breakthrough (1914) relied on examining plague by means of novel visualization techniques and, in particular, what I call diagrammatic montage. Bringing together different images of a plague-carrying flea, this visual method reinvested the flea’s anatomy as the locus of a new epidemiological spatialization of plague, rendering it into a veritable factory of plague infection.

The last chapter of the book explores how wild rodents came to be understood as involved in the spread and maintenance of plague and discusses the complex ways in which scientists across the globe tried to connect enzootic plague (among wild rodents) and epizootic plague (among rats). I examine the development of notions of chronic plague and plague reservoirs in Inner Asia, the United States, Hong Kong, and South Africa, and the international development of the notion of sylvatic plague and its impact on the previously established protagonistic role of rats in relation to plague. The chapter examines how ecological approaches developed by Charles Elton came to be applied to plague by K. F. Meyer in California, and how such approaches further assumed different forms in other plague contexts, such as Argentina and Tunisia. My focus turns toward epidemiological questions surrounding relations between wild rodents and rats. I thus explore the final iteration of “how plague got rats” in the context of the Third Plague Pandemic and how this was impacted by debates on species invasiveness and the seasonality of plague.

The book’s conclusion reflects on the development of scientific approaches to the relation between rats and plague between the 1870s and the 1950s with a focus on the entanglements between a pandemic emergency and the scientific investigation of its zoonotic causes. I examine what I call the “conditions of mastery” that allowed human-rat relations to become scientifically intelligible and actionable in each of the iterations of “how plague got rats.” Focused on the transformation between these iterations and the shifts in mastery these entailed, I consider the dynamic and agonistic nature of zoonosis-related science in the context of the first pandemic to be attributed during its course to a disease derived from an animal.

At the crossroads of the anthropology and history of medicine, this book is ultimately an effort at what the great Zapotec Isthmus poet Victor Terán has called biting our “understanding’s feet” in order “to wake it from its stupor.”93 I hope that readers will take that journey not seeking a definitive conclusion but an opening to new possibilities of telling the story of the coming together of zoonotic diseases and pandemics, of the meeting between humans, plague, and rats, and of epidemiological reasoning, pandemic imaginaries, and epidemic governance.

## Staggering Rats at the End of the World

The story of “how plague got rats” begins in Yunnan, China, a remote province on the southwestern frontier of the Qing Empire. It was there that plague witnessed a significant upsurge by the end of the 1850s, leading to a devastating epidemic that lasted for nearly two decades.1 The Yunnan plague epidemic is today universally recognized as the springboard of the Third Plague Pandemic.2 As part of this historical narrative, accounts of dead rats in mid-nineteenth-century sources on plague in Yunnan are habitually taken at face value and treated as indexes of rat epizootics preceding outbreaks of the disease among humans.3 This “virus hunting” approach to history resonates all too well with what appears to be an insatiable desire for discovering pandemic origins in the first quarter of the twenty-first century.4 But there is a different story, free from the passion of epidemic and pandemic origins. In this chapter I adopt a methodological approach that reads contemporary sources on plague in 1870s Yunnan against the grain of “virus hunting” approaches to history, or what we may call the passion for pandemic origins. My aim is not to extract epidemiological data but to take these stories ethnographically seriously. In other words, I want to understand what their intended meaning was as well as how they became themselves tangled in more-than-medical webs of meaning and practice. To do this, we need to immerse ourselves in the worlds of the people articulating these accounts, and to approach the accounts as part of “outbreak narratives” or, more precisely, epidemic and pandemic imaginaries.5 This will allow us to see the complex and nondeterministic ways in which rats and plague became forged together in Yunnan as part of distinct, historically situated forms and processes of thinking, acting, and being with epidemics.

Accounts of ratfalls and plague in Yunnan were not originally developed or delivered by scientists. Instead, at the center of these narratives stood an unlikely assortment of colonial figures, including Catholic missionaries on enemy territory, a French gunrunner with geographic pretentions, and a British punitive expedition. Key to the development of the new ways in which “plague got rats” in Yunnan was what I will call the figure of the “staggering rat”—an image so evocative that it came to find its way many decades later in what is without a doubt the most influential modern novel on an epidemic, Albert Camus’s 1947 La peste (The Plague).6 Early stories of staggering rats featured accounts of rats infected with plague coming out of their nests, abandoning their supposedly secretive character, and swirling around for all to see before dramatically dying out in the open. In this chapter, I will explore the emergence and development of this figure in the writings of Western agents in Yunnan in the 1870s and 1880s. Interrogating the place of this figure within the longue durée of pandemic imaginaries, I will argue that the staggering rat became a pivotal trope for missionary, eschatological thinking about and with epidemics, which brought together imperial agendas of conversion and colonization. By the mid-1880s, scientists eventually came to adopt and adapt the figure of the staggering rat to fit their own epidemiological reasoning and imagination, without, however, any firsthand experience of the epidemic or the regions affected by it. I will deal with these reconfigurations of the staggering rat in the final section of the chapter, where we will see how scientists tried to bring Mahamari (see the introduction) and the Yunnan plague together in a unified, epidemiological reasoning about rats and plague at the cusp of sanitary-bacteriological transformation and synthesis.

Given that this chapter deals with accounts of epidemics by Western agents in East Asia, the question immediately arises as to Indigenous knowledge about plague and its impact on Western associations between rats and the disease. Carol Benedict has shown that Chinese records point at the potential existence of plague in Southwest China as early as the late eighteenth century, with contemporary Chinese commentary on ratfalls more recently examined in detail by Wilt L. Idema.7 And yet, there is no indication that the Western agents initiating and developing the new association between plague and rats in 1870s Yunnan were aware of or interested in Chinese sources. The scant mentions of Chinese approaches to plague and rats, as we will see below, are extremely vague, appearing to be no more than an imposition of Western ideas onto Indigenous culture. As a result, this chapter will not venture to guess how Indigenous approaches to rats or plague may have impacted the development of the colonial framings of their interrelation, as, lacking primary evidence on the matter, this risks simply reproducing later Chinese nationalist histories of science and, as a result, colonial standards of what counts as “knowledge,” which were adopted and applied by the authors of these histories.8

### Missions Étrangères de Paris

Plague was mentioned alongside other disasters in the region in missionary documents from Yunnan as early as 1866.9 Beginning in the early 1870s, missionary reports and letters began associating outbreaks of a devastating epidemic in Yunnan with the end of the Panthay Rebellion (1856–1873), an Islamic insurrection that challenged Qing sovereignty and led to the establishment of a short-lived Sultanate in the region.10 The death toll of the rebellion was enormous (ca. two million), and missionaries as well as explorers wove together an image of war- and epidemic-driven ruination in the province, with the discussion of the epidemic being generally used as an appendix to or support for narratives around the ravages of the insurrection, especially as these affected local communities that had converted to Christianity. The first extensive record of plague was in the missionary correspondence of the Missions Étrangères de Paris (MEP), which had maintained an active presence in the region since the 1850s, and which was greatly affected by and preoccupied with the Islamic insurrection.11

On July 27, 1874, Joseph Ponsot (1803–1880), bishop of Philomélie for the Apostolic Vicariate of Yunnan, informed the members of the central council of the Society of the Propagation of the Faith (Œuvre de la Propagation de la Foi)—a Pontifical Mission Society founded in Lyon in 1822, which supported Catholic missions across the world—that following the end of the hostilities, a devastating epidemic had struck the province. This appears to be the first document in a European language discussing at length what would come to be known as the Yunnan plague. Strikingly, the report did not directly associate the epidemic with bubonic plague. Instead, Ponsot stressed that this was “an epidemic unknown in Europe.”12 The only mention of “plague” in Ponsot’s letter was a generic one, where la peste was used in the general sense of an epidemic or pestilence rather than that of bubonic plague, as understood at the time. Ponsot wrote that the “terrible epidemic” (terrible peste) called “Cang-tsy-tchén” was in fact first noted by another MEP missionary, Jean Marie Le Guilcher:

The people, he wrote, call it the disease of rats. The rats start by being affected by an epidemic, dying in heaps. I saw one of my catechists remove from his old hovel two bushels of rotten rats. The infection is strong, and it is it that undoubtedly that gives the contagion to man in the plains of Tsou-hiong-fou. There are villages where the disease returns every year. As soon as the rats die, the inhabitants emigrate to the mountains, where they wait two or three months, and once the bad odor has disappeared from their home, they can return there with impunity. It should be noted that the mountains are much more salubrious than the valleys. From the beginning of August last year, the Minjia father, Paul Tchao, has occasion to visit me. He told me that rats died in immense quantities in Pienkio (his district). We did not pay much attention to it at first, as the disease had never appeared in this country.13

Ponsot’s letter gained widespread attention, as it was published, with edits, in the 1875 volume of the Annales de la Propagation de la Foi, the main publication of the Society for the Propagation of the Faith.14 Significantly, where the two versions differ most extensively is their account of Indigenous responses to ratfalls. The manuscript and published versions of Ponsot’s rendition of Le Guilcher’s account of the Yunnan epidemic differ in the testimony about villagers fleeing to the mountains. The manuscript contains an etiological narrative that identifies the cause of the epidemic as miasmatic (odors) and endorses the choice of the Indigenous population to flee to the mountains (where the air was supposedly free from miasma) until the odor disappeared from the villages; the published version of the letter makes no mention of the movement of Indigenous peoples. To the extent that Ponsot’s manuscript accurately reproduced the original letter, it is clear that, according to Le Guilcher, locals fled not ratfalls or a disease spread by rats but the miasma that killed these animals. By contrast, consider a key passage in the publication—“The infection, which they spread, is undoubtedly the case of the contagion which attacks man in the plains of Tsou-hiong-fou”—which unambiguously states that rats did not simply suffer from but also spread the disease; this passage was not part of the manuscript.15

Who edited Ponsot’s letter and to what purpose is not known, as no archival sources survive on the matter. What is clear is that in its published form, which came to be read by future authorities on the disease, the miasmatic etiology of the original letter was transformed into a “contagious” one, involving the transmission of the disease from rats to humans. Given the complex debate on plague’s infectiousness and contagiousness in the course of nineteenth-century French medical thinking, this did not deny the miasmatic origin of the disease but added a new trait to it in terms of its transmission, thus transforming the disease’s ontology and introducing for the first time the idea of rat-to-human plague infection.16

### Rats, Missionaries, and Gunrunners

In the following years, the epidemic in Yunnan continued to be discussed in French missionary letters and publications.17 Yet it was another, unexpected source that elicited international medical interest in the Yunnan plague: the report of a British-led murder investigation/punitive expedition in 1876 led by Thomas Grosvenor following the murder of Augustus Raymond Margary, a British consular official in Yunnan. This “affair” had important consequences for British-Chinese relations, and on the extension of knowledge about plague.18 The account of the investigation/expedition, written by Edward Colborne Baber, contained information gathered by another key MEP missionary in Yunnan and important player in the “Margary affair,” Jean-Joseph Fenouil (1821–1907).19 In Baber’s report, Fenouil was quoted as claiming that the Yunnan plague affected not only humans but also domestic animals and rats. This is the first mention of the figure of the staggering rat: “[Plague’s] approach may often be known from the extraordinary movements of the rats, who leave their holes and crevices, and issue on to the floors without a trace of their accustomed timidity, springing continually upwards from their hind legs as if they were trying to jump out of something. The rats fall dead, and then comes the turn of the poultry; after the poultry have succumbed, pigs, goats, ponies, and oxen successively die off.”20

By contrast to Ponsot’s published letter, in Fenouil’s account, there was no mention of rats or other animals transmitting the disease to one another or to humans. Instead he stated that the epidemic was caused by a “pestilential emanation slowly rising in an equable stratum from the ground, and as it increases in depth, all animals are as it were drowned in its poisonous flood; the smaller creatures being first engulfed, and man, the tallest of Yunnan animals, suffering last.”21

Baber’s report of Fenouil’s testimony, published in 1878, is strikingly similar to another account of plague in Yunnan published the same year by Patrick Manson, who in the following decades became the most celebrated proponent of tropical medicine.22 In his Report on the Health of Amoy for the Half-Year Ended 31st March 1878 for the Chinese-controlled but internationally led Imperial Maritime Customs Service, Manson translated and reproduced notes on the Yunnan plague authored by Émile Rocher, adventurer and future French diplomat to China, who had taken part in a mission to the region in 1871–1873.23 In “Notes on the Plague in Yunnan”—contained in a two-volume work titled La province chinoise de Yün-nan—Rocher related that the disease was locally called Yang-tzŭ, which historian Marta Hanson has explained as “meaning severe itching of the skin or pruritus,” but which in the context of colonial writings on plague in the region was understood as relating to “boils.”24 Following prevalent etiologies of plague at the time, Rocher saw the epidemic as being caused by telluric gases: “There is a fact that inclines one to think that the epidemic is owing to exhalations from the soil and it is this, those animals that live in the ground, in drains or in holes, that are the first to be attacked. This is particularly noticeable with the rats.”25 In Manson’s translation, Rocher then proceeded to give an evocative account of ratfalls, which, carrying as they did the figure of the staggering rat in an amplified form, came to have an important impact on framing rat epizootics in relation to plague outbreaks for decades to come: “As soon as these animals are ill, they leave their holes in troops, and after staggering about and falling over each other, drop down dead. The same phenomenon occurs in the case of other animals, such as buffaloes, oxen, sheep, deer, pigs, and dogs. All are attacked, but the dog less severely than the others.”26

As Jean Michaud has shown in his history of French Catholic missions in the region, what historians usually describe as a “diplomatic” mission led by the explorer Jean Dupuis (to which Rocher was attached) was in reality a gunrunning expedition “launched in the wake of the recent agreement made with the Yunnan authorities aimed at providing local rulers with the Western weaponry they needed to crush the Muslim uprising.”27 Rocher, also an apt cartographer, may be seen as a quintessential colonial agent, as he found the time to combine gunrunning with geoprospecting, “investigating the mineral resources of the region.”28 Rocher’s book, Michaud argues, was “designed for administrative consumption, with the aim of informing on the topography, the layout of roads, the communication systems, the regional history, the current rebellions, production and commence, and, in particular, indigenous metallurgy.”29 Rocher’s notes on plague should be accordingly scrutinized as part of this colonial project: as information collected and relayed not for the benefit of some detached scientific knowledge but for immediate imperialist exploitation and resource extraction.

Historians and life scientists writing on the Yunnan plague have overlooked the aim and context of the information collected by Rocher, taking his account at face value and not submitting it to the scrutiny usually reserved for a report collected in the process of running guns and whose aim was to prepare the colonial takeover of the region.30 Equally overlooked are the different evidentiary registers in Rocher’s account. Maintaining a critical distance from the information related in Rocher’s account of plague as a product of French imperialism is as important as taking ethnographically seriously its tropes and tones of witnessing.

Rocher’s book is composed by diary-like entries, general descriptions of the expedition in nondiary form, and analytical passages on the history of the region, the Panthay Rebellion, and the Yunnan plague. Different discussions of plague in the book, both in its main body and in the “Notes on the Plague in Yunnan” appendix, reflect these narrative differences and their evidentiary claims. The first mention of plague in Rocher’s book (vol. 1) is in his entry for January 26, 1871, where he noted that the expedition entered a rice-cultivation area whose inhabitants “had fled before the plague, which over several years, has made appalling ravages in the country.”31 It is in this passage that rats also make their first appearance:

This illness, which is no other we think than a plague in the form of the buttons of Aleppo, breaks out most frequently in the month of May and continues to strike until the month of November, but changing location. What makes us think that this epidemic is due to soil exhalations is that rats are the first victims of the scourge; once they are ill, they come out of their holes, take refuge inside habitations, make a few turns on themselves and die. Buffaloes, oxen, sheep and goats are then struck, but among these the evil has a lesser hold.32

Whereas medical authors and historians alike have assumed Rocher provided an eyewitness account of ratfalls, no such claim is made in the passage quoted above, major parts of which (on soil exhalations and rats leaving their nests and making a gyration before dying in the open) were repeated in his “Notes on the Plague in Yunnan” (appended to the second volume of the book). Was Rocher simply reproducing Fenouil’s discourse, either through conversations with him or by exposure to Baber’s published account, without making mention of him as his source?33 This possibility is reinforced by fact that the way evidence is presented in the passage differs from how Rocher marked eyewitness accounts elsewhere in his narrative. Let us take the example of the second passage in the book where ratfalls are mentioned. Here Rocher’s description of the devastation encountered employed two testimonial registers: an eyewitness account, followed by a statement of a generally known fact (which I highlight below in italics), followed by an eyewitness account:

At the moment of our passage, the village was entirely deserted, the inhabitants having fled their abodes in order to camp on the heights, abandoning their growing crops, so as to escape the attack of an enemy more ruthless than the rebels, the plague. That year, the terrible scourge had stricken with such furry on the unlucky district, that almost all horned beasts, following the rats, perished. So it was only with great difficulty that we managed to find an old temple to spend the night there, and our servants were forced to go to the heights to stock up on the essentials. This small place had the aspect of a desert: no one in the fields or in the houses, wreaths of rice lay in places, half made and as if someone would come to finish them. No domestic animals, no more smoke rising from the chimneys, no noise, no movement; life seemed suspended.34

Whereas the abandonment of the village at the beginning and end of the paragraph is accounted as a direct, firsthand experience, by contrast the death of rats and other animals from plague is simply reported as a generally known fact. If Rocher encountered dead rats or ratfalls on January 26, he never made such a claim. Indeed, the entry on that date instead stated that the disease struck “in the month of May and continued to strike until the month of November.” In late January his account was thus one of the aftermath of the epidemic and not of its course, as is often assumed by historians, with information about phenomena such as ratfalls and other epizootics being secondhand and not the result of direct observation.

As plague spread from Yunnan to other parts of southwest and southern China, Rocher’s account played an important role in fostering the figure of the staggering rat as a sine qua non of outbreak narratives about plague in China. In some cases, this involved no more than directly reproducing (often without acknowledgment) Rocher’s narrative to describe outbreaks in other regions of the country.35 In other accounts, it involved weaving together more information about ratfalls, often entangled with plague etiologies involving human corpses and contaminated soil.36 From there, the figure of the staggering rat entered the realm of medical literature engaged with the Third Plague Pandemic, as it proceeded from Hong Kong (1894) to spread across the globe. By the start of the twentieth century, accounts of plague in different parts of the world came to rehearse the figure of the staggering rat in various permutations, so much so that this figure became both a leitmotif of plague narratives and an index of authenticity in observations about ratfalls preceding human epidemics. By the early twentieth century, the staggering rat was thus enmeshed in origin stories of plague.

Following this genealogy, and keeping the evidentiary ecology of the development of outbreak narratives involving the staggering rat in mind, it becomes clear that approaching accounts of ratfalls in the context of the Yunnan plague as a source of epidemiological evidence is enormously problematic. This is not only because we do not have any unambiguously firsthand account of this phenomenon but also due to the epistemological violence and obfuscation that are involved in reducing the outbreak narratives in question to repositories of data about the zoonotic aspects of plague in the region. Instead of poaching in the archive for evidence of zoonosis, what is needed here is to understand the affordances of the accounts of ratfalls within their immediate ontological and political environment. In other words, we need to take these accounts ethnographically seriously as part of outbreak narratives that formed part of broader epidemic imaginaries and as ways of negotiating the human condition and human-nonhuman relations by way of thinking with epidemics.

### The Great Chain of Infection and the Staggering Rat

Reading accounts of ratfalls from 1870s Yunnan, it becomes immediately clear that these were embedded in a much broader outbreak narrative regarding the death of nonhuman animals as a result of the epidemic. This outbreak narrative revolves around what we may call the “great chain of infection.” This is a story absent from the Ponsot/Le Guilcher account, first appearing in Baber, where Fenouil presents the death of poultry following that of rats, itself being followed by the successive death of “pigs, goats, ponies, and oxen”—a chain of infection attributed to “pestilential emanation slowly rising in an equable stratum from the ground” engulfing different creatures in succession of height.37 In Rocher’s narrative, by contrast, we do not find a chain of infection determined by the height of each animal, but simply a collection of susceptible beasts. In one passage these are said to include “buffaloes, oxen, sheep, deer, pigs and dogs,” while in another passage only “buffaloes, oxen, sheep and goats.”38

We can thus say that accounts of the Yunnan plague contain two mythic variants linking the disease to animals. The weak variant, seen in Rocher, simply presented animals as dying at the same time as humans, stricken with plague in no particular sequence or order. This is an iteration of a well-known classical mytheme (in Claude Lévi-Strauss’s sense of the term), which first appears in Latin accounts of pestis—a term not reserved for plague, as it would come to be known from the sixth century, but more generally to epidemics. The image of an epidemic striking both humans and animals appears in Virgil’s Georgics III, in the famous description of the Noric plague, which kills calves, pigs, and horses. So miasmatic was the atmosphere of Noricum said to be, that it caused even birds to fall dead in mid-flight: “Here once arose a season pitiable for the diseased atmosphere, and through-out all the seething of autumn it glowed white hot and gave over every kind of beast to death, and every [race of] wild animals.”39 Hunter Gardner has shown how Virgil’s narrative on the Noric plague relied on Lucretius’s earlier configuration of the so-called plague of Athens (not plague in biological terms) in terms of civil war, as well as, on a more ontological level, a “process of decay and the collapse of boundaries within corpora.”40 The disease striking across the human-nonhuman ontological barrier marked not simply an all-engulfing epidemic but one that threatened the order of things with discordia.41

This was a theme aptly carried over in Christian plague narratives during the Second Plague Pandemic, and in particular in visual representations, which often relied on classical images of plague, such as Marcantonio Raimondi’s 1515 engraving of Raphael’s The Plague of Phrygia, known as Il Morbetto (fig. 1.1). As Sheila Barker has noted, the engraving represents the plague striking the city of Pergamon in Virgil’s Aeneid in a way that brings into tension religious and naturalist etiologies of the disease but above all stresses ruination, conflict, and all-encompassing catastrophe: “ominous formations in the sky, dead livestock, patches of barren earth, a desolate town, and buildings toppled by earthquakes.”42

To find this mytheme reflected in Rocher’s account of plague in Yunnan is not surprising, as it had been reproduced by the physician and historian of medicine Justus Hecker in his 1832 book on the Black Death (Der schwarze Tod im vierzehnten Jahrhundert). This was an immensely popular and widely translated work that introduced a new “historical and nosological” framework of plague, transformed understandings of the epidemics of 1347–1351, and invested plague with catastrophic, world-historical consequences.43 Hecker argued that during the Black Death, “multitudes of dogs, cats, fowls and other animals, fell victims to the contagion; and it is to be presumed that other epizootes among animals likewise took place, although the ignorant writers of the fourteenth century are silent on this point.”44 Rather than simply reproducing the Latin mytheme of the “plague of animals,” however, Hecker integrated it into what Feye Marie Getz has shown to be his decisive turn to “gothic epidemiology.”45 This approach was unique and unprecedented in that it attributed world-historical properties of ontological proportions to an epidemic. For Hecker, the Black Death marked not simply a catastrophic event but also one that revealed humanity’s true relation to “Nature.” As Getz argues, for Hecker, “the Black Death was a demonstration of the power and glory of Nature, so overwhelming in its universality and its terror as to defeat the best efforts of mere science to define it.”46 The mytheme of the “plague of animals” functioned by underlining, on the one hand, the all-encompassing power of plague over living beings and, on the other hand, the power of “Nature” to abolish any distinction between humans and nonhuman animals through plague as an ecumenical equalizer.

The second, stronger mythic variant involved not simply the concurrent infection and death of humans and animals but what we can properly call “a great chain of infection”: the gradual and sequential infection of “lower” animals (exemplified in rats), cattle, and then humans. We have already seen how Baber’s account of Fenouil’s testimony formed the prototype of this trope as regards the Yunnan plague. However, this was not a story invented by Baber or Fenouil but a variant on the classical mytheme of the “plague of animals.” The most developed version of this is to be found in Ovid’s Metamorphoses and, in particular, in his narrative about the plague of Aegina: “At first, in a heap of dogs and birds and sheep and oxen and among the wild beasts, the power of the sudden disease took hold.”47 Here the “plague of animals” is configured as taking place in advance of the human plague, borrowing from the dramaturgical trope used by Homer in the Iliad (1.50–51) and by Sophocles in his narrative on the plague of Thebes in Oedipus Rex (25–30).48

Where the two mythic variants significantly differ in the Yunnan plague narrative is in their emphasis on plague as a disease of disorder. In the weak variant, the mytheme emphasizes the manner in which plague collapses ontological barriers and, being a disease of decay or disarticulation, fosters a discordant state. The employment of the weak variant thus reiterates the ontological catastrophe that is supposedly inherent in epidemics while at the same time it allows its authors to rhyme the ecumenical ravages of plague with those of the Panthay Rebellion, creating an onto-political metonymy of ruination, disaster, and decay. The strong variant, too, relies on the same metonymic operation, but with a twist. Here nature itself provides a glimpse of order within the prevailing disorder: Animals and humans do not get infected or die helter-skelter but in a hierarchical manner, according to their height, which also happens to be, roughly speaking, a hierarchy on the scale of animality: rats, poultry, pigs, goats, ponies, oxen, and finally humans. Therefore, the strong mythic variant provides a kind of hope, a glimpse of the reordering capacity of nature led by “man” (white, male, bourgeois, Christian, colonialist), while also allowing the terrible catastrophe to highlight divine providence, as the sequential death of animals could be argued to be a divine sign or portent of the coming plague.49 If, as Florence Bretelle-Establet has argued, Chinese approaches to plague in the late Qing Empire combined telluric and demonological readings of epidemics through politically inflected prisms, missionary approaches to the disease similarly relied upon a political theological tension between naturalist and, in this case, eschatological framings of plague.50

It is as part of the employment of the strong mythic variant that we need to examine the symbolic efficacy of the figure of the staggering rat. For whereas in Ponsot/Le Guilcher we read about rats “dying in heaps” or “en masse,” in Baber/Fenouil a new image emerges, describing how rats “leave their holes and crevices, and issue on to the floors without a trace of their accustomed timidity, springing continually upwards from their hind legs as if they were trying to jump out of something.” The image is repeated in Rocher, whose phrasing in the original is more evocative than Manson’s translation: “Once they fall ill, they come out in groups, burst into the interior of the houses, run in a panic, and after a few turns on themselves, fall dead,” and “once they are ill, they come out of their holes, take refuge inside habitations, make a few turns on themselves and die.”51

This proved to be a very popular image, with publications following Baber/Fenouil and Manson/Rocher not failing to further embroider it. In an 1886 article on the Yunnan plague published in Les missions catholiques, for example, we read an intricate weaving together of Baber/Fenouil’s “timidity” and Manson/Rocher’s “staggering” images: “Forgetting their natural shyness, [rats] come out of their hole in broad daylight, and run hither and tither with no fixed purpose (courir çà et là à l’aventure). They make a few steps in painful trotting, then they stop, breathless, and they soon end up succumbing.”52

The source of this evocative image has in fact nothing to do with rats and is no other than the well-known passage from Boccaccio’s Decameron: The Yunnan plague narrative borrowed the image of the swirling hogs and transposed it onto rats.54 Boccaccio’s “frictional” image would have been readily available to authors at the time both directly from Decameron and from Hecker’s naturalization of the scene as evidence of the epizootic nature of plague: “Thus Boccaccio himself saw two hogs on the rags of a person who had died of plague, after staggering about for a short time, fall down dead, as if they had taken poison.”55 However, in transposing the image of the staggering hogs onto rats, the Yunnan plague narrative added an important ontological layer that may be easily overlooked were we to ignore the intertextual nature of outbreak narratives. For the image of the rat abandoning its natural ways, coming out in the open, and swirling around itself for all to see before dying is consistent with a particularly Latin configuration of pestis. This, as Gardner has shown, depicted affected animals as transgressing the boundaries of their nature (a metaphor of civil strife in Virgil).56 David West has evocatively described this image as mobilizing anthropomorphism so as to deliver a powerful poetics of “pathos and paradox.”57 Gardner explains how this transformation/transgression is brought about by means of “the internal conflicts that riddle the bodies of plague victims gradually play[ing] themselves out”: “As birds and sea creatures abandon their natural habitats (541–3; 546–7), predatory animals (wolves and dogs, 537, 540) no longer stalk their accustomed prey (sheep and deer, 537, 539). An eerie peace ensues, though it is a tranquility achieved through death rather than concord.”58

Of this mine own eyes (as hath a little before been said) had one day, among others, experience on this wise; to wit, that the rags of a poor man, who had died of the plague, being cast out into the public way, two hogs came up to them and having first, after their wont, rooted amain among them with their snouts, took them in their mouths and tossed them about their jaws; then, in a little while, after turning round and round, they both, as if they had taken poison, fell down dead upon the rags with which they had in an ill hour intermeddled.53

The figure of the staggering rat in 1870s narratives about plague in Yunnan may thus be said to be emblematic of the ontological and political affordances of the great chain of infection mythic variant within which it was embedded. A “vermin” increasingly invested in the course of the nineteenth century with gothic meanings and agency, the rat was an ideal protagonist in an outbreak narrative that, on the one hand, incorporated the onto-politics of “gothic epidemiology,” while, on the other hand, was about much more than simply a disease.59 For these were not so much plague-infected rats, in the sense we understand this term today, but plague-afflicted ones: animals struck by a disease that did not simply affect them biologically but transformed their very being, forcing them in a most cruel way to abandon their natural character as elusive, secretive animals so as to voluntarily reveal themselves to their enemies (humans), exposed in the enemy’s habitat (the home) and, having thus betrayed their nature, perform a danse macabre and perish. The figure of the staggering rat, though later recuperated bacteriologically, was one of primarily ontological and cosmological import. Situated within long-standing tropes of what Jouhaud et al. have identified as spectacles-indices of scourge (fléau), through its kinetic dramaturgy the staggering rat embodied the end-time inhabited and awaited by the French Catholic missionaries.60 In this way, the staggering rat rhymed more closely with early-modern epidemic horrors—such as the one described by the Jesuit father Jean Grillot who, during the 1628 plague of Lyon, told of an enormous dog digging out the corpses of the dead while wolves from across the Rhine filled the air with their terrifying howls—than with its bacteriological reiterations undertaken by medical doctors in years to come.61 Both in missionary accounts and in Rocher’s narrative, the staggering rat functioned as a sign of panolethry (from the Greek pan for all or all-encompassing, and olethros for disaster) imagined to be taking place in Yunnan at the time: “a nexus of calamities” where all disasters seem to be working “in tandem” in a manner “involving simultaneous political, climatological, seismological, and pandemic crises”—or in the case of Yunnan, famine, the Panthay Rebellion, and plague.62 We need to see the staggering rat not as being simply symbolic of this process but as embodying it by way of its kinetic dramaturgy. Performing a literarily cata-strophic movement, in the Greek sense of the term (katastrephein, overturning or turning upon/against oneself), the staggering rat is metonymic of a revealing catastrophe and at the same time of a catastrophic revelation.

Considering the figure of the staggering rat as the pivot of the new ways in which “plague got rats” in 1870s–1880s Yunnan makes evident a process long overlooked by historians: The emergence of the association between plague and rats during what is assumed to be the original epidemic of the Third Plague Pandemic took place within an eschatological framework that had no bearings in positivist approaches to plague and was only loosely attached to what, following Philippe Descola’s heuristic model, we would call a naturalist ontology.63 Instead of proceeding from scientific debates about infection and contagion, “plague got rats” within colonial iterations of Christian apocalyptic imagination. Thinking with rat’s relation to plague, in this case, was linked to an epidemic imaginary that had its roots in both classical approaches to nature and social order and to Catholic eschatology, but which was also part of a situated, catastrophic experience of the world. In Yunnan, “plague got rats” not so as to make sense of empirical observations, or broader questions regarding the epidemiology of the disease, but so that MEP missionaries could make sense of a time that felt out of joint, and so that both they and other colonial agents could mobilize this experience to advocate conversion and colonization as the interlinked solutions to what they saw as a double (Indigenous and natural) challenge to Empire’s and God’s order.

### Linking Plague in Yunnan and the Indian Himalayas

In the fifteen years between its publication and the outbreak of plague in Hong Kong (1894), Rocher’s book on Yunnan (1879–1880) and his account on plague in particular reached an international audience that was unusually large for an epidemic in a remote province of China. A plausible reason for this unusual reception was the now-forgotten panic that gripped Europe at the end of the 1870s as a result of a plague epidemic that occurred much closer to home. This was the violent outbreak of (probably pneumonic) plague in the Russian village of Vetlyanka (1878) that revived international interest in the disease and mobilized British, French, German, and Romanian medical teams for on-site investigation in the Volga.64 Hence, when Louis Dunoyer de Segonzac presented Rocher’s work on plague at the Geographic Society of France on March 7, 1878, he did so explicitly in relation to the “considerable emotion caused in Europe by the epidemic that seems to be making ravages in the Russian province of Astrakhan.”65 A year later, Max Joseph von Pettenkofer, a leading medical authority in Germany committed to miasmatic theory, speculated that the epidemics in Yunnan and the Volga were caused by the same zymotic disease process.66 The Vetlyanka outbreak functioned as a catalyst for a return of scientific attention to plague. By challenging the notion that plague had “gone away,” it helped bring information on contemporary plague outbreaks from North Africa, Eurasia, South Asia, and China together. At a time when European scientists were debating infectious diseases and epidemics through a series of International Sanitary Conferences, the Vetlyanka outbreak pushed authorities to further draw together patterns between outbreaks in an effort to develop new understandings of the etiology and transmission pathway of plague.

Within this comparative epidemiological framework, it did not take long for medical authors to try to connect accounts of ratfalls in Yunnan with those in Kumaon and Garhwal, India (see the introduction).67 By 1894, China-based European doctors like J. L. Michoud believed the fast-increasing outbreaks of plague in southwest and southern China to have originated in the Mahamari outbreaks in British India and to have been carried into Yunnan by means of caravans via Tibet.68 The first systematic comparison between Mahamari and the Yunnan plague appeared in a British report of a new outbreak of the former in Kumaon and Garhwal (1876–1877). Challenging the opinion that Mahamari was a form of endemic plague uniquely prevalent in the Indian Himalayas, and drawing on ratfall-related similarities between it and the Yunnan plague, British epidemiologist John Netten Radcliffe reasoned that “it [was] possible also that the disease may exist in an endemic form in other parts of the same vast mountain system.”69

An inspector at the Medical Department of the Local Government Board charged with monitoring “foreign epidemics” for the British colonial office, Netten Radcliffe reasoned that what connected Yunnan and the Indian Himalayas was their supposed belonging to the same mountain system where a particular form of endemic, rat-killing plague prevailed. Still, for others, comparing the two outbreaks led to generalizations on a larger scale. When, in the autumn of 1881, “a remarkable mortality among field-rats” was observed in Nanjing, China, the medical report of the Imperial Maritime Customs Service made sense of the epizootic by directly connecting it to accounts from Yunnan and the Indian Himalayas, and making use of the figure of the staggering rat: “The animals emerged from holes in dwellings, jumped up, turned round, and fell dead. Here was evidently a subsoil poison which affected the animals precisely in the same way as the malaria of the Yunnan pest, which extended to higher animals and to man.”70

The connection of Mahamari to the Yunnan plague by means of the precedence of human outbreaks by rat epizootics had a profound scalar effect. No longer particular to Yunnan or the Indian Himalayas, the relation between plague and rats began its journey as a global epidemiological question. Rather than leading to a stable outbreak narrative or etiology, however, the comparison lying at the basis of the globalization of this plague-rat relation fostered a heated debate regarding the epidemiology of the disease. This was a debate that, unfolding at the historical crossroads of miasmatic and germ-theory understandings of disease, soon came to involve a new epidemiological agent that was able to accommodate both frameworks: grain.

In a short but influential note on the 1876 Mahamari outbreak, Superintendent of Vaccination W. Watson, in Allahabad, Jhansi, and Benares, India, used the supposed Himalayan-Yunnan connection to bolster his theory that the cause of Mahamari was “decayed grain.”71 Whereas, as David Barnes has shown, decaying grain was framed as a source of spontaneous generation of miasma in other contexts around the middle of the nineteenth century, in this case the agency involved was closer to germ than miasmatic theory.72 Although there is no direct evidence, it is almost certain that by 1876 Watson was aware of Pasteur’s yeast experiments and ensuing debates about the bacteriological causation of diseases—for example, through Claude Barnard’s discussion of germ theory and the role of fungi in public health, or from John Simon’s fungal theory in relation to typhoid fever.73 Watson believed that the cause of plague was a fungus infesting mandua, or finger millet. He based this belief in his observations from the field, where he noted that when a quantity of mandua from the infected village of Balt was sent to the watermill, the hitherto healthy rats that were infesting the mill all died. For Watson, the fact that, according to his information, the death of rats was followed by that of snakes and jackals could be explained through a theory of chain-infection: Rats consumed the damaged, fungus-infested grain first, with snakes then becoming infected by eating “dead or dying rats.”74

Here we have an example of how the trope of the “great chain of infection” was transformed from a cosmopolitical element of outbreak narratives on the Yunnan plague into a datum for epidemiological reasoning in relation to renewed cases of Mahamari in the Indian Himalayas. This transformation of the great chain of infection required the introduction of grain as a new epidemiological agent, in line with epidemiological thinking about disease “mediums” at the time that marked a shift from miasmatic to germ theories of disease causation. No longer the result of filth or vitiated air, plague was seen by Watson as a fungal disease, marking the first microbiological configuration of the plague as a disease that affected both humans and nonhuman animals, who then became directly infectious to other animals, like jackals, who consumed their flesh. For Watson, designating decayed, fungus-infested grain as the cause of plague was a momentous discovery, as it elucidated not only the death of rats and the subsequent deaths of other species across the two geographical regions but also some of the long-standing questions surrounding Mahamari. This approach explained, for example, why cattle, sheep, and goats were not infected by the disease (due to their grass-eating diet), why southern regions were less affected by Mahamari (people there did not keep grain in storage for long periods due to a greater demand), why a large number of pilgrims passed through the area without being affected (as they supposedly did not eat locally stored mandua or other grains), and why the disease was on the decline in Garhwal (increased pilgrimage traffic had led to an improvement of the grain market in the region). Watson’s grain theory further purportedly explained why some Mahamari outbreaks were confined to a single household, whereas others spread across the community. In the first case (which Watson designated as an imported infection), Mahamari was caused by persons returning home having contracted the disease from grain they had eaten in another location. In the second case (coined by Watson a “spontaneous outbreak”), the pestigenic grain was to be found in the affected village, with the only escape from plague being for the community to flee without taking the grain with them.

This transformation of the relation between ratfalls and Mahamari, by means of the introduction of germ theory and fungus-infected grain in particular as a new component in epidemiological reasoning about plague, did not find support among veterans of Mahamari research. An important scientific observer of Mahamari, Surgeon-Colonel Charles Planck, opposed the idea of grain-related transmission, arguing that if that were the case, rats would have been found dead in their nest; he furthermore negated the fact that snakes died of the disease, witnessing a healthy-looking snake emerging from an affected household.75 In turn, in his June 2, 1880, address on “Endemic Plague in India” to the Epidemiological Society in London, Charles R. Francis did not dismiss either the importance of ratfalls or reported similarities between plague outbreaks in the British Himalayas and in Yunnan, applying across them the figure of the staggering rat: “The manner of a rat’s death is remarkable: the animal would emerge from its hole on to the floor, stagger, perform an involuntary gyration or two, bring up blood, and die.”76 In the British Himalayas as in Yunnan, Francis noted “a similar preliminary mortality amongst the rats” was observed, leading people to flee their homes.77 And yet, for Francis, this similarity, striking as it was, could not be explained through, or indeed be used to legitimate, Watson’s fungus-infested grain theory.

In 1852, Francis and Pearson had already noted that the rat they dissected as part of their Mahamari investigations was found with a stomach “full of sound grain of the Mundooa (a small millet) and of Chova (an amaranth).”78 This, they stressed, was “gradually forming into a proper pultaceous mass.”79 In his 1880 London address, Francis added that when they inspected stored grain, it too bore no “trace of fungus or disease whatever.”80 Moreover, he argued, the observations of a great chain of infection, as reported by Watson, were fallacious in terms of their epidemiological reasoning. First, such theory could not account for the fact that cat epizootics were never observed in the course of Mahamari. Second, with the exception of rats and more infrequently snakes, no other species had been observed to be affected during outbreaks.

Francis ridiculed the whole idea of a chain-infection originating in defective grain as based on “the principle of the house that Jack built.”81 To press the point, he presented a vivid counterexample. If this chain-infection theory were true, then bears that devoured human plague corpses would also succumb to the disease: “I myself saw, one moonlight night, what appeared to be a small family of bears pass the bamboo scaffolding on which I was seated—they were too far for a shot—and the plague stricken corpses rapidly disappeared. No dead bears were ever seen.”82 Instead of fungi or germs, Francis insisted on earlier, miasmatic framings of Mahamari, and as a result plague, albeit modernizing his view by referencing “electrical or volcanic changes in the rocks” leading to “the development of sulphuretted [sic] hydrogen.”83 Denying the “great chain of infection” but amplifying the figure of the staggering rat as a phenomenon applicable across Yunnan and the Indian Himalayas, Francis mobilized this figure to confirm his earlier, racialized etiology of plague as a “poison” to be found “in great force, in the soil, which becomes at every visitation more and more impregnated with it, owing to the filthy habits of the people.”84

### Conclusion

In this chapter I have examined “how plague got rats” in the context of outbreaks of the disease in late nineteenth-century Yunnan. Reading the sources against the grain of “virus hunting” approaches so as to take the former ethnographically seriously, I have argued that accounts of ratfalls in this context need to be examined as part of broader processes of epidemic imagination and governance involved in colonial projects for mastery. This historical-anthropological examination of missionary, expeditionary, and other nonmedical colonial sources on the Yunnan plague has shown that Western narratives on the epidemics relied on long-established mythemes regarding plague-related epizootics, the most important of which was the “great chain of infection” and its staggering rat variant. Exploring these epidemic imaginaries allows us to understand their role within broader eschatological ways of thinking prevalent at the time among Yunnan missionaries. I have argued that the ways in which “plague got rats” during what is often considered to be the origin of the Third Plague Pandemic need to be understood not as innovations in epidemiological reasoning but as contributing to Western efforts to make sense of and master a catastrophic situation qua an apocalyptic one. The new configuration of plague’s relation to rats emerged not within medical or epidemiological reasoning but rather within a more-than-medical, colonial political theology in a time of acute political and (from the perspective of its authors) spiritual adversity that required major innovations as regards the mastery of human-nonhuman relations.

Yet the fact that this configuration emerged as an eschatological one did not prevent it from being soon adopted and adapted by scientists. By 1880, connecting the Mahamari and the Yunnan plague narratives by way of their accounts of the relation between ratfalls and plague forged together not simply a hybrid narrative about rat epizootics preceding human outbreaks of plague but also a radical epistemological unsettlement of ratfalls as epidemiological phenomena. Problematizing the relation between rat epizootics and human plague for the first time through a lens informed by germ theory (fungus infection) marked a radical departure from both sanitarian/miasmatic and eschatological framings of this relation. It is tempting here to hail this as a breakthrough in understanding the etiology of plague, which, however mistaken in content (as plague is not transmitted by fungi), was correct in form (as plague is transmitted by microorganisms). However, an approach less infatuated by scientific discovery and the doxa of the bacteriological revolution calls for a more cautious reading. Watson’s fungal/grain theory was immediately refuted by leading authorities on plague, such as Francis, and appears to have left no mark in broader medical receptions of the Yunnan plague (e.g., Manson). Rather than germs, what appeared to emerge triumphant out of debates about the relation between plague and rats both in the case of Yunnan and in the case of the Yunnan-Mahamari comparison was the figure of the staggering rat. Freed from its eschatological or broader cosmopolitical origins, in the minds of medical doctors, by the 1880s, this came to function as a sine qua non of true plague, an apocalyptic symbol turned into an epidemiological index.

## An Epidemiological Dividual

While the prevalent historiographical notion that the nineteenth century was free from plague before the inaugural outbreak of the Third Plague Pandemic in Hong Kong in 1894 is fallacious, it is safe to assume that precisely because there had been so many localized outbreaks of the disease (in the Punjab, Kumaon and Garhwal, as well as Mesopotamia, Egypt, Russia, Yunnan, Corfu, Benghazi, and Palestine) without any of them leading to an international spread of the disease, the Third Plague Pandemic caught medical scientists, public health authorities, and governments around the world by surprise. What was unprecedented was the combination of two recent developments: one political, the other epistemological. On the one hand, by 1894, the greatest part of Africa, Asia, and the Pacific had come under direct or indirect colonial rule, with Britain, France, Germany, the Netherlands, and Portugal claiming the greatest part of these imperialist exploits. This meant that vast regions of the world had come under newly connected colonial medical surveillance and intervention. It also meant that both older and newly acquired colonies were transformed into what historians Wolfgang Eckart and Helen Tilley have called laboratories for imperial science, including medicine and epidemiology—to which we may add, vast laboratories for colonial mastery.1

One the other hand, by 1894 the medical sciences, including epidemiology, were well versed in a new, bacteriological paradigm. In full bloom by the end of the 1870s, bacteriology pitched rival medical schools in a competition for the reinterpretation of infectious diseases by way of germ theory and the identification of pathogens and transmission pathways, as well as cures and prophylactics. By 1892, bacteriology had also proved its relevance to both confirming epidemiological theories and to public health intervention in the course of the cholera epidemic in Hamburg, Germany.2 This was the start of the golden age of discovery for bacteriologists and parasitologists, who began identifying the causative pathogens of infectious diseases and their vectors to great international acclaim.3

At the same time as they were unsettling older and fostering new knowledge and practices on infectious diseases and epidemics, the proponents of bacteriology also generated a triumphant narrative about the transformative nature of their science and its ability to master diseases. This was especially so in the colonies, where these processes were portrayed as cornerstones of Western civilization and its supposed supremacy over both nature and Indigenous Others.4 In other words, both bacteriology and its presentation became powerful tools of empire.5 Nonetheless, the narrative of discovery and conquest of infectious diseases by means of bacteriology has since been reproduced—not only by historians allied to imperialist agendas or worldviews but also by scholars celebrated for their theoretical innovation. As relates to plague, this has led to a historiography that has relied on positing the bacteriological identification of the disease’s pathogen (summer 1894, independently by Alexandre Yersin and Kitasato Shibasaburō) as an event marking a paradigm shift in both knowing and controlling the disease.6 This approach has since come under sustained critique, both for its lack of empirical evidence and for its reductionism to a bacteriological doxa.7 Studies have shown that, in reality, doctors continued to rely on methods other than bacteriology in order to know plague epidemiologically and diagnostically.8 Indeed some of the most important theories about plague, and interventions on outbreaks across the globe, relied on prioritizing nonbacteriological over bacteriological evidence and methods of inquiry.9 Historical studies based on empirical data rather than philosophical speculation have thus come to show the limitations of the bacteriological “transformation of plague” paradigm and highlight new ways in which we should approach the political and epistemological entanglements of plague science in both colonial and metropolitan contexts.

This chapter deepens this critique and enriches the new historiography of the Third Plague Pandemic by delivering for the first time an examination of “how plague got rats” in the years immediately following the discovery of the disease’s causative bacterium, between 1894 and 1899, in British India and Hong Kong, the areas mostly affected by the disease in this period. This is easy to mistake as a confused period when medical experts grappled for explanations of the spread of plague in a supposedly inevitable pathway toward scientific, bacteriologically based clarity, which reached its pinnacle in the discovery of plague’s single and true vector (the rat flea). In fact, this was an epistemologically intense, nuanced, and complex era of plague research that produced vital epidemiological debates without necessarily concluding on or revealing the transmission pathways of the disease. Against the current of histories reducing science to a linear process of discovery, I will show that, during the 1890s, the scientific focus was not on the identity of plague hosts or vectors but rather on the pestigenic relations between potential so-called agents of the disease. We will then see how, within this epistemological context, the rat was rendered an epidemiological dividual. Tacitly borrowing the term from anthropological theory, I mean by this that medical works and debates instituted the rat as an epidemiologically knowable and actionable propagator of plague through investigating the complex ways in which this animal related to other potential hosts or vectors of the disease.10 As a heuristic, the dividual thus allows us to consider the relational institution and transformations of the rat as a propagator of plague and to take colonial medical configurations of pestigenic agency ethnographically seriously, without reducing them to the metaphysics of agency implied in Latourian notions of the “network” and the “assemblage.” Whereas the Actor Network Theory paradigm assumes agency to actually reside in relating or connecting or networking, in my approach to “how plague got rats” this relational emergence and maintenance of agency, as far as plague is concerned, is taken to be not an ontological fact but rather a historical-ethnographic one: a way in which colonial doctors, faced with an acute epidemiological crisis, sought to render plague intelligible and actionable.

The potential “agencies” in relation to which “plague got rats” in colonial medical theory and practice in Hong Kong and India between 1894 and 1899, included grain, insects, excrement, the soil, cloths, and clothes, as well as the capacious category of the “infected house.” Taking as a common ground the bacteriological nature of plague (identified in Hong Kong in 1894) not everyone agreed on how these “agents” of plague interacted or what conditions were necessary for this interaction to produce plague epidemics. It was, however, always by means of interrogating plague’s complex relation to such potential propagators, and the relations of these “agents” with one another, that colonial medical experts came to frame the rat and attempted to master human-rat relations. So much so that, as a propagator of plague, the rat became inseparable from the pestigenic relations, real or imaginary, in which it became entangled. We can thus say that the rat was instituted in medical discourse and practice as an “agent” of plague through its indivisible, dividual relations with other plague “agents.”

It is crucial to take this dividualist focus of epidemiological reasoning ethnographically seriously and not reduce it to a prelude of the discovery of a single host or vector. For only thus can we grasp colonial medicine as an epistemological and biopolitical process that incorporated, integrated, and often mutually unsettled bacteriological and sanitarian approaches to plague as a disease that remained consistently outside the framework of tropical medicine. Both the epistemic and the “moral force” of this approach was derived not so much from identifying the supposed “agents” of plague as from interrelating them and mastering their pathogenic relations.11

### Plague in Canton and Hong Kong (1894)

Although it is usually Hong Kong that is considered to be the springboard of plague’s development into a pandemic, the 1894 outbreak in the British Crown Colony was immediately preceded by an equally devastating epidemic in the nearby city of Canton (Guangzhou), where a plague epidemic between January and June 1894 cost at least 80,000 lives.12 The key medical report on the outbreak in the Chinese metropolis was written by Alexander Rennie for the Imperial Maritime Customs Service.13 Referring to Émile Rocher’s observations on plague in Yunnan (see chapter 1), Rennie stated that, in the case of Canton, “from districts of the city where the disease had lasted for some time rats entirely disappeared, but kept on dying in other parts to which it subsequently extended.”14 Rennie invoked the figure of the staggering rat and revealed that some local officials in Canton put a bounty on rats captured in the course of the outbreak (collecting twenty-two thousand of them).15 This was the first time an extensive antirat policy was put into place in relation to a plague epidemic, its application being also attested by Mary Niles, a missionary doctor at the time in the South Chinese city. “One of the officials from his private funds I am told,” Niles reported, “offered 10 cash for every dead rat brought to him. He had collected 35,252 in one month. 2,000 were brought in one day.”16 Rennie performed postmortem examinations on rat cadavers with the aim—as in the case of Francis and Pearson thirty years earlier (see the introduction)—to establish a comparative pathology between the animals and humans.17 The results remained, however, inconclusive, as no human postmortem was made possible due to opposition by Chinese authorities. Rennie was left hoping for an answer from “bacteriological examination” and comparative pathology by colleagues working on the disease in Hong Kong.18

Rennie operated within the long-established miasmatic framework of plague, with ratfalls serving as confirmation to this theory. He observed that people living in boats were unaffected by the disease. Adding to “the fact that rats living in the ground and drains are the first animals to fall victims,” he concluded that “the specific poison emanated from the soil” with the unsanitary conditions attributed to Chinese dwellings seen as “provid[ing] a specially suitable nidus for its growth and dissemination.”19

When plague struck Hong Kong in March 1894, William Robinson, governor of Hong Kong, entertained no doubts: The disease had been imported from Canton, with the supposedly insalubrious habits of the Chinese population and a drought causing a lack of water by which “to keep the densely populated districts of China Town in a satisfactory sanitary condition” being framed as what contemporaries called the exciting cause.20 However, this was not an opinion shared across colonial medical authorities. James Alfred Lowson, acting superintendent of the Government Civil Hospital and a key player in the management of the plague epidemic in Hong Kong, maintained that the “poison is probably developed from atmospheric conditions underneath the houses in a certain district, and that it is caused simply by poverty and dirt.”21 In his influential report on the epidemic, Lowson quoted earlier opinions about the great chain of infection as related to Yunnan (see chapter 1), cautioning that “the question of the infection of rats, previous to the epidemic being noticed in humans [had] been made too much of”: In his report, Lowson mentioned that he had proceeded to inoculate with cultures of the bacillus mice, rabbits, and guinea pigs, all of which proved susceptible to the disease, with the postmortem of the succumbing animals showing pathological similarities to humans; in the case of rats, this reportedly involved enlarged lymphatic glands.23 Lowson also reported having examined rats and mice that had died “spontaneously” in Hong Kong, mentioning that “in the internal organs of a mouse [he] discovered the same Bacilli” as found in humans who had succumbed to the disease.24 He, however, drew no conclusions from this discovery other than that it was necessary to remove mice and rats found dead in houses “with proper precautions,” so that handlers did not get infected.25

It is only natural that as rats have their snouts about an inch above the floors of houses they are much more liable to inspire plague-infected dust than people who have their mouths at least two feet high. Inoculation too is easy. It must be remembered that rats usually die two or three days after inoculation; therefore the statement that the deaths of rats generally precede an epidemic—although generally true—is only dependent on the fact that rats and other small animals are peculiarly liable to be infected, and have a very short incubation period of the disease. Their habits and residence also conduce to their early affection after the disease has been introduced.22

For his part, following his discovery of the plague bacillus on June 20, 1894, Alexandre Yersin initially expressed only mild interest in rats.26 In his first report to the General Governor of French Indochina (June 18, 1894), under whose authority he was operating in Hong Kong, Yersin painted an impressionistic portrait of the streets of the British colony, which rhymes with the photographs of the colony included in the notebook containing his handwritten observations of the outbreak.27 The following passage from his report is indeed one of the pinnacles of what historian Andrew Mendelsohn has highlighted as Yersin’s plein air bacteriology: “For those resident in Hong Kong, the aspect of the city is completely changed: the streets are deserted, the harbor contains but a few boats in the place of the numerous vessels that are usually moored there. In taking a walk along the streets of the infected neighborhoods I saw many dead rats lying on the ground; a great number of houses are shut, few people in the streets.”28

In Yersin’s second report, by contrast, written on June 29 (i.e., after his discovery of the plague bacillus), rats assumed a less ambient and more clinical character. Yersin informed the General Governor of Indochina that, as in Yunnan where ratfalls had been observed in the course of human plague outbreaks, in Hong Kong, too, this phenomenon assumed remarkable proportions. Wanting to ascertain whether the rats actually died of plague, Yersin procured a number of rat cadavers. It is here that the Latourian fallacy of asserting a hard opposition between the diagnostic role of clinical examination and of bacteriology becomes clear, as for Yersin (who was Bruno Latour’s model for this evocative but ahistorical and highly misleading reading) it is only the working together of these methods that could produce reliable proof: “Upon performing an autopsy, I found almost always the presence of a bubo identical to that in humans, and inside the bubo I found my bacillus in enormous quantities.”29 By the time of his third report (July 17, 1894), Yersin appeared convinced that “the rats [were] certainly the main propagators of the epidemic.”30 “These animals,” he reasoned, echoing sanitarian framings of the plague-rat relation, “live in the sewers where they maintain the disease in a sporadic state.”31

How Yersin came to this conclusion is not clear as there is no discussion on the subject in his surviving notes or letters, where he failed to further elaborate on this idea of sporadic maintenance of plague in rats. In the 1894 Annales de l’Institute Pasteur article announcing his bacteriological discovery, Yersin paid scant attention to rats. He mentioned that “in the infected neighborhoods many dead rats lie on the ground,” adding that the outbreak had first taken place and most forcefully affected an area of the city where “a new sewer had just been installed.”32 Yersin appeared interested in this, as he believed that the particular form of sewers in Hong Kong made cleaning “almost impossible,” speculating that this “consequently constituted multiple permanent foci of infection.”33 Rather than this observation indicting rats as spreaders of the disease, as is often assumed in historical accounts of Yersin’s research, it formed part of a much broader sanitarian approach to plague etiology, which clearly operated within the contemporary idea of “pythogenic” theory, or filth-focused understanding of plague, which Prashant Kidambi has called “an infection of locality.”34 Yersin echoed this sentiment in language that reflected both broader medical preoccupations with the “insalubrious home” back in France and prevalent colonial tropes of racializing disease: “The lodgings, occupied by the Chinese of the poor classes, are everywhere fetid dives where one hardly dares to enter and where an incredible number of people are crammed together. Many of these hovels do not even have windows and are below ground level. We understand the devastation that an epidemic can cause when it establishes itself in such terrain, and the difficulty that must be experienced in stopping it! The only remedy would have been to set fire to the Chinese city.”35

Yersin mentioned that “dead rats found in houses and on the streets contained almost always the microbe in great abundance in their organs,” something earlier attested by Kitasato.36 The susceptibility of animals was seen as an opportunity to conduct experiments on guinea pigs, mice, rats, and pigeons. As regards rats, Yersin noted that injecting them with plague cultures, or with extractions of liver or spleen from an animal that had succumbed to the disease, almost always killed them, with the bacillus being found in the “blood, liver, spleen and ganglions.”37 He also conducted an experiment with mice, inoculating them with plague bacilli and placing them inside a jar alongside healthy animals of the same species. While the former were the first to die, “on the following days, the healthy mice all succumbed one after the other, with the plague bacillus in their organs.”38

Poorly designed and described as this may seem, it constituted the first effort at a plague experiment suggesting an inter-rodent transmission of the pathogen. “Plague,” Yersin stated after describing the experiment in haste, “is thus a contagious and inoculable disease.”39 As becomes further clear from his third report to the General Governor of Indochina (July 17, 1894), the aim of this experiment was not to test specifically whether rodents carried and spread the disease but to examine whether plague was more generally “contagious,” a much older and contentious epidemiological question.40 As a result, to Yersin, the mice experiment proved primarily not rodent-to-rodent but human-to-human transmission under the supposedly unsanitary conditions attributed to Chinese modes of dwelling: “The Chinese who live crammed into narrow spaces are indeed in the same conditions as my mice, and just the other day we saw five Chinese who slept in a sort of narrow cellar in the post office building contracting the disease the one after the other.”41 Primarily confirming long-standing morally inflected sanitarian framings of plague and epidemics more broadly, which mobilized the notion of “conditions d’existence” to pathologize working-class lifeways, it is only at a secondary level that the experiment suggested to Yersin a possibility of rodents as vectors of the disease.42 And this only within a broader array of potential nonhuman vectors: “It is probable that rats constitute the principal vehicle [of plague] but I have equally noticed that flies catch the disease, and may also serve as agents of transmission.”43

### Flies, Soil, and Virulence

The passage on flies from Yersin’s celebrated publication is one that is often omitted from historical accounts of his bacteriological discovery. Presumably, framing rats as spreaders of plague and then putting them on equal standing with flies, an insect we now know does not transmit the disease, is thought to be embarrassing to the Pasteurian doctor’s fame. We need, however, to approach the flies passage in Yersin’s article not from the “virus hunting” perspective of retrospective epidemiological judgment but in a way that takes it ethnographically seriously. To do this, we must immerse ourselves in Yersin’s epidemiological reasoning: “I have remarked that, in the laboratory where I made my animal autopsies, there were many dead flies. I took one of these flies, and after tearing off its legs, wings and head, I ground it in broth and inoculated it into a guinea pig. The inoculation liquid contained a great quantity of bacilli absolutely similar to those of plague, and the guinea pig died in 48 hours with lesions specific to the disease.”44 Yersin proceeded from this experimental description to state that he was also able “to isolate the plague bacillus from soil collected 4 to 5 centimeters deep in the ground of an infected house” and that the bacillus “was quite similar to that taken from buboes, but it was not virulent.”45

The two experimental instances in Yersin’s plague investigations in Hong Kong (the first on flies and the second on soil) need to be treated not as distinct aberrations from what we might teleologically consider to be the correct path to identifying the rat and the flea as plague’s host and vector. Instead, they need to be seen as indicative of the actual system of epidemiological reasoning about plague articulated by the Pasteurian doctor—that is, of Yersin’s epidemiological bricolage.46 Rather than being aimed at narrowing down his scientific gaze and identifying a single host or vector of the disease, Yersin’s research on plague was an open-ended and profoundly relational one, bringing evidence, indications, and traces in tension with one another as these came at hand. His epidemiological reasoning encompassed various players and factors in plague transmission and maintenance so as to problematize their complex interrelations. Yersin’s interest in the soil as a potential reservoir of the plague bacillus is something I have examined in detail elsewhere.47 Based on experiments involving the extraction of soil from “infected houses” in Hong Kong, Yersin maintained that plague could be maintained in an “attenuated state” therein: “The plague microbe exists in the soil, where it probably undergoes a long evolution through which from being non-virulent, it ends up by becoming virulent again. Why? How? We know nothing about this yet.”48 Though the idea was bitterly contested by British medical officers like Lowson, it rhymed with the pathologization of Chinese working-class neighborhoods, houses and their contents, and modes of dwelling, which formed a colonial medical common ground between the otherwise tense interactions between Yersin, Kitasato, and British colonial doctors.49 Locating plague in the soil and the household was also actionable, a key way for colonial authorities to reify their power. Although it proved unable to directly infect animals, the soil was believed to be able to function as the locus of plague’s maintenance in an attenuated form, where the pathogen would supposedly lie low, waiting an opportunity to regain its virulence and strike back at humanity. This view was endorsed by Colonial Surgeon Philip Ayres in the course of the heated debate over the fate of Taipingshan, Hong Kong’s most affected neighborhood (fig. 2.1).50 Arguing that the entire neighborhood should be “destroyed, as far as possible, by fire,” Ayres indicted the soil as being both the source and medium of infection: “I say that the sub-soil is strongly infected and cannot be thoroughly dealt with as long as the buildings stand over it. If it were not so, you would not see rats coming out of sewers and holes in the ground, full of bacteria, not communicated to them from human patients but from the sewage which is soaking through the soil, and from the gases down below, a sure proof that the sub-soil is in a dangerous state. The rats do not catch the disease from any infection above, but from below.”51

The soil provided a material locus for plague’s legendary elusiveness, as well as a convenient bridge between sanitarian and bacteriological etiologies, while remaining an epistemic object that attracted considerable scientific debate, policy innovation, and public debate.52 On the one hand, configuring the soil as a locus of bacterial attenuation amplified the prevailing ontological frame of plague as a disease whose character was acutely stealthy—a disease that had the ability to transform itself and remain at the edge of science’s sight. This imperative ontology, defining not so much what plague is but what it must be, aligned with what Fey Marie Getz has described as the gothic turn in epidemiology from the 1830s onward—a turn that attributed a nefarious, world-historical, and world-catastrophic agency to diseases as foes of humanity.53

On the other hand, Yersin’s framing of plague as a disease that could become attenuated and then regain its ability to infect humans or nonhuman animals set the disease at the center the distinctively Pasteurian preoccupation with “an old concept given new meaning”: virulence.54 Virulence, Andrew Mendelsohn has shown, was conceived by Louis Pasteur and his collaborators and disciples “to operate continuously in the world”: “Virulence was as much a matter of Nature as of Art. To be exact, the attenuation and return to virulence of bacteria was understood as biological variation.”55 The study of diphtheria had provided Émile Roux “the opportunity to explore a general hypothesis he and Pasteur had articulated in their studies on anthrax in 1881: infectious disease resulted not only from contagion, but also, and perhaps above all, from activation of latent (‘attenuated’) microbes already present in human and animal bodies.”56 Virulence was thus a notion central to the “reorganization of bacteriological knowledge” that “promised a new and unified way of explaining disease.”57 Fostered by Pasteur’s development of vaccines, Mendelsohn convincingly argues, this framing of diseases as phenomena that result from the recrudescence of hitherto attenuated microbes (a “return-to-virulence etiology”) rendered epidemics “not so much invasions as disturbances of a delicate balance between microbes and animal life.”58

The idea that the attenuation and recrudescence of bacteria were linked to the lull and reappearance of infectious disease epidemics not only within human bodies but also in given locations was one that had been promoted by Pasteur himself. Writing in collaboration with Roux and Charles Chamberland in 1881, Pasteur had suggested that “an epidemic that an attenuation of its virus has extinguished can be reborn by the strengthening of this virus under certain influences.”59 It is significant that the historical example given was one relating to plague: “The accounts that I have read of the spontaneous appearance of the plague seem to me to offer examples of this; witness the plague of Benghazi, in 1856–1858, the outbreak of which could not be linked to a contagious origin. Plague is a virulent disease specific to certain countries. In all these countries, its attenuated virus must exist, ready to regain its active form when conditions of climate, famine, misery, once again manifest themselves.”60

I will elaborate on Pasteur’s and Yersin’s ideas about virulence below. For the moment, let us return to flies. The question of flies never became embroiled in a heated debate about attenuation and recrudescence (as was the case with the question of soil). It also did not have the biopolitical consequences of the medicalization of the soil: demolition, deroofing, and incineration of Indigenous houses, or the removal and baking of the soil forming their floors. And yet, Yersin’s short passage on flies formed part of the same system of epistemological reasoning as the question of the soil, and it resulted in framing the ubiquitous insects as relational “agents” of the disease.61 If the soil seemed able to conserve plague in an attenuated form, flies appeared to Yersin to be able to carry it in a virulent one.62 In his third report to the General Governor of Indochina, Yersin noted: “We easily understand how a fly that has stood on the cadaver of a plague-infected rat and which has subsequently taken a walk on the leg of a Chinese man, where there are often wounds and ulcers, risks very much transmitting the disease to him.”63 In this racialized narrative, it is the interrelation between flies and rats (at least their cadavers) that rendered them into “agents” of plague. Rather than being an embarrassing mistake, Yersin’s identification of flies as propagators of plague was equally important to his view on rats, and it should be understood as an early example of the dividual framing of plague “agency,” which reached its apex during the first years of the pandemic in India.

### Debating Plague and Rats in India (1896–1899)

The press in India, where “beastly encounters” had long preoccupied both colonial biopolitics and public opinion, extensively covered the outbreak of plague in Canton and Hong Kong in 1894.64 The Times of India marveled at the phenomenon of animals being reportedly implicated in it: “Many of the lower animals were affected, thousands of rats especially being found dead at the commencement of the epidemic.”65 Yet within two years of the Hong Kong outbreak, by September 1896, plague also arrived in India with devastating consequences.66 Although Antoinette Burton and Renisa Mawani have described the “British Empire as a multispecies enterprise,” little attention has been paid to the question of rats in this context.67

When were ratfalls first sighted in the context of the arrival of plague in India? During his testimony to the Indian Plague Commission (February 18, 1899), Dr. C. H. Freeman Underwood, a private physician in Bombay, claimed to have been the first to inform the authorities about the sighting of dead rats in Mandvi (a neighborhood in the port of Bombay) in September 1896, though the commission did not appear to be wholly convinced by his testimony.68 What is certain is that the idea that rats were involved in the outbreak quickly attracted interest from a broad audience and gave rise to a rich array of vernacular epidemiological reasoning. A letter published in The Times of India on January 12, 1897, highlights the complex approach to the subject on the ground. The anonymous author readily admitted rats to be victims of plague but wondered why crows that “feast” on the rats were impervious to the disease: “I have seen the crows feasting delightfully on dead rats during the early weeks of the plague, and have only seen two dead crows during the whole of that time, and these were caught in one of my wire American decapitation guillotines. The crows therefore not only feast on dead rats but digest them splendidly.” “Why,” the author asked, “should the crow be immune from plague poison? What is the difference between the digestive secretions of the crow and of man?”69

On the one hand, the letter reveals the diffusion of the idea that plague was a disease contracted through the alimentary tract. The theory of alimentary infection, already part of medical understandings of plague by the end of the 1870s (see Watson’s fungoid grain theory in chapter 1), received particular support in a report published in 1897 by Staff-Surgeon Wilm of the Imperial German Navy on plague in Hong Kong in the preceding year.70 Also available in three installments and as a single digest article in the Indian Medical Gazette, Wilm’s thesis was widely quoted and discussed in medical publications.71 The observation that plague was “most frequently” contracted “by way of the alimentary tract” was, in his opinion, “substantiated by the results of experiment on animals,” as well as by postmortems of human victims showing significant impact on the stomach and mesenteric glands.72

While reflecting ways in which the alimentary transmission pathway came to be intertwined in vernacular epidemiological reasoning, The Times of India letter is also revealing of the perception that lay members of the public had of scientists trying to ascertain the role of rats in the spread of the disease: The anonymous author’s tongue-in-cheek challenge to scientists shows us the extent to which the discussion of the rat’s relation to plague was not limited to medical or sanitarian cycles but rather galvanized public opinion.

The plague has killed off the rats, and they are now a very minue quantity, so minue that I have seen an aspiring savant microscopist offering as much as eight annas a head for a dead rat, and, with no result. He was tearing his hair in despair at a lost opportunity of handing his name down to prosperity. The propounder of the theory of rat communicability of plague should offer prices for cases proved to have been infected by “touching” or “going near” dead rats. Until this proof is established it is wise to count the rat theory of communication as “not proven.”73

The first authoritative opinion about the role of rats in the spread of plague in the context of the epidemic in India was expressed by the prolific physician and adviser to the India Office on matters of plague, James Cantlie.74 Cantlie’s influential “Lecture on the Spread of Plague” to the Epidemiological Society of London (December 18, 1896) reflected on his experience with the disease in Hong Kong in 1894 and on available data on plague from medical and colonial reports and research at the time.75 Cantlie attempted a global synthesis on the disease, which included the question of the susceptibility of rats and their role in transmitting plague to humans. Commenting on the Yunnan plague narrative and the idea of “plague being a soil-bred—a miasmatic—disease” that spread in a “scale of infection” from those animals living closer to the ground all the way up to humans, he reasoned that this great chain of infection should “not to be taken seriously but the infection of some of these animals is undoubted.”76 At the same time, he reproduced almost verbatim the Yunnan-derived figure of the staggering rat (see chapter 1) found in accounts he otherwise advised his audience to not take seriously: “Before, or it may be during, an epidemic of plague, or before the individuals in any particular house in an infected locality are stricken, the rats leave their haunts and seek the interior of the house. They seem careless of the presence of man and run about in a dazed way with a peculiar limping jerk or spasm of their hind legs.”77

The figure of the staggering rat became immensely popular in the first years of the pandemic in India, and was further embellished with new attributes. In a report by Captain C. H. James on the 1897–1898 outbreak of plague in the Jullundur and Hoshiarpur districts of the Punjab, we read of plague-stricken rats “wander[ing] about in an aimless, tottering way … ‘as if it were drunk or mad.’”78 The rat’s only desire before dying was said to be water, with plague-infected rats described as possessing the same craving for it as humans: “If it can get near water it goes for it at once and drinks greedily as if it were suffering from unquenchable thirst. In its eagerness to do this, it often falls in, and then makes very little attempt to get out again and is consequently drowned.”79 Delirium (a supposed telltale symptom of human plague, according to many physicians at the time) took the place of thirst in another variant of the figure of the staggering rat: “Within the villages, diseased rats were found in the stage of delirium staggering about in unwonted places, and living diseased rats were found prior to the stage of delirium in search of water outside the houses. There is nothing strange in any of these facts. They are what we would expect.”80 Here the staggering rat appears to have completed its transformation from an eschatological into an epidemiological figure, through a further act of gothification. The addition of thirst and delirium as symptoms of plague-stricken rats rhymed with the attribution of these symptoms to human patients at the time, creating a seamless pathological continuity that fostered the idea of rats and humans as copatients of plague as a disease that revealed the horrifying power of “Nature” and put humanity’s sanity and survival to the test.81

Cantlie’s examination of the relation between rats and plague also revealed scientific uncertainty surrounding the disease. Asking how the rat becomes infected, Cantlie dismissed inoculation through wounds or scratches as a sufficient means of contamination and endorsed the alimentary mode of transmission, which involved not only consuming infected food but, in yet another turn of gothification, also cannibalism: “It must not be forgotten that rats are cannibal in their habits, and direct contagion must play a large part, if not the major part, in the general infection of their tribe.”82 And yet, while he professed, with a nod to Watson (see chapter 1), that snakes and jackals could get infected with plague by eating rats, as regards the manner in which rats may infect humans, Cantlie admitted this was yet to be known.83

Reluctance to take the death of rats as proof that the animals spread the disease to humans was not unusual in the initial years of the pandemic. Surgeon-Captain W. W. O. Beveridge, a member of Poona Plague Committee (est. March 1897), expressed this succinctly when he noted that while “undoubtedly a frequent concomitant of plague,” rats could not be considered to be “as precursors of the disease”; the only reason why they died before humans was that they were “inhabitants of the ground and also ground feeders.”84 Something that, nonetheless, caught the attention of early observers of the epidemic was that, in the course of the Bombay outbreak of 1896–1897, rats appeared suddenly reduced in numbers even in locations or structures, like prisons, where they usually thrived. For some, like Surgeon-Lieutenant-Colonel G. Waters, this was taken as proof “against the validity of the theory that plague is disseminated by rats.”85 Waters argued that rats were the first to die of plague, not because their snouts were closer to the ground, as Lowson and Beveridge had argued (mirroring Fenouil’s earlier narrative, see chapter 1), but due to them “being nearer the damp surface than human beings.”86

Rehearsing well-established Victorian sanitarian concerns about humidity, in a lecture to the Anthropological Society of Bombay, Waters used site plans of the Common Jail in Bombay in order to demonstrate dampness as the “chief determining factor” of plague infection and to show that rats were not the source of infection.87 This was a rare investment of tropical medical concerns on the question of plague, a disease that systematically fell outside the realm of medicalized tropicality. “It will be seen in the plan,” Waters argued, that “whilst rats died chiefly in one place, the attacks among the convict occurred all over the rotunds [sic] with wonderful correspondence to the existence of dampness, not otherwise.” Water asked, “Now, are we to suppose that the rats by some wonderful intentions selected their subjects for infection among those alone with moisture?”88 More than simply focusing on dampness or humidity, Waters’s epidemiological reasoning operated within the broader epistemic milieu of associating plague with location. As shown by Mary Sutphen in her study of antiplague measures in colonial Hong Kong and Calcutta, focusing on “where the bacillus lurked” fostered synergies and accommodations between bacteriological and sanitarian approaches to infectious diseases, expanding on what David Barnes has called a sanitary-bacteriological synthesis.89 In his examination of the epistemological and biopolitical iterations and implications of this synthesis in relation to plague in India, Kidambi has further showed that “localist ideas appeared to supply the missing link in the etiology of the plague by suggesting that the germs that caused the disease were either a product or a constituent element of localized sanitary disorder”—in this case, prison-located dampness.90

### The Indian Plague Commission

The explosive range of opinions and ideas about plague, including its relation to rats, developed and debated in the context of the epidemic in 1890s India, is best exemplified in the testimonies provided to the Indian Plague Commission. Forming part of “a huge proliferation of commissions in late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century India,” the Indian Plague Commission was brought together in 1898. In many ways, it resembled the earlier Cholera Commission (1861–1862), particularly in that “both were expeditionary in character, touring the subcontinent and reconstructing narratives of the disease under investigation.” The former commission, however, would also include investigations into plague’s “aetiology, pathology, and treatment.”91

In his insightful examination of the Indian Plague Commission, anthropologist Nicholas Evans has shown that, over seventy sittings undertaken between November 1898 and March 1899 across India, “the questioning of witnesses was the major technique through which the commission sought to produce truth.”92 While most witnesses were directly invited, advertisements for potential witnesses were also distributed, attracting a wide range of experts, including British and Indian medical doctors, civil servants, army officers, hakims, and sanitary inspectors. Evans has shown how the interviews with 260 witnesses involved a total of 27,415 questions, all under the strict guidance of those “call[ing] and examin[ing]” the witnesses: “There was no sense that anything was guided by the witnesses, even those who were presented as experts. Instead, the commission extracted evidence, one numbered question at a time, and in the process they transformed plague into a thing that could be known through a particular form of speech.”93

A number of witnesses to the commission professed only an indirect knowledge of rats’ involvement in the plague outbreaks, while others confessed to having no evidence linking the animals to plague. Several respondents confirmed observations of ratfalls before human epidemics. Captain Leumann of the Indian Medical Service (IMS), for example, related that in a shed containing goods in Hubli, “coolies” employed in unloading manifested a great mortality between late April and early May 1898: “Then suddenly they reported a frightful stench; the floor was taken up and hundreds of rats were found dead, some who had recently died” and were found to contain “bacilli in the blood like plague bacilli.”94 Some witnesses confidently denied that rat deaths preceded human cases or that rats imported plague, attributing the spread to humans. Others gave mixed reports, stating that deaths among rats were observed in some but not all cases. But even in those cases, the idea that dead rats preceded infection in humans was not always supported. The international plague expert Frank G. Clemow maintained that the infection was concurrent in humans and rats, while also claiming that in some cases it was humans that caused plague in rats.95 While recognizing that rats died before humans, Deputy Plague Commissioner A. H. Stevens, in the princely state of Hyderabad, stated that he had “seen a good deal about rats in the newspapers” but that he was personally “rather sceptical whether rats convey it or not,” as in several houses where dead rats were found no plague in humans occurred.96

Rather than signaling an effort to identify rats as the sole agent of plague, what emerged out of the testimonies and debates collected by the commission were approaches to the ways in which a range of agents of the disease became interrelated within residential and commercial infrastructures in India. It was this complex and dynamic interrelation between situated plague agents, rather than any one of the supposed agents in and of themselves, that attracted evidence and fostered new iterations of epidemiological reasoning. Questions of how soil, excreta, humans, rats, clothes, grain, and cloths came together to lead to the spread of the disease dominated medical frameworks of plague before 1900.

Take, for example, the report by Dr. McCabe Dallas on his work at the Grant Road Hospital, one of the most important plague hospitals in Bombay at the time.97 McCabe Dallas’s interest was not with identifying a single agent of plague but in configuring a system of pestigenic interrelations. This importantly involved the city’s drains and sewers, which were both the home of rats and the receptacle of “human excreta, urine, and other contaminated matter allowed to escape from plague-stricken patients owing to their habit of converting their bath-room space into latrine accommodation, or through the washing of soiled clothes containing microbes from excreta or pneumonic sputa,” and further became the highways of plague transmission by contaminating the soil.98 There were several ways in which rats were imagined to be involved in fostering pestigenic interrelations. For example, McCabe Dallas argued that plague bacteria from rat cadavers or from the soil could turn airborne, “become released, ascend with the warmer atmosphere from below, and, reaching an upper current, travel with it and [be] ultimately deposited in a building or a locality.”99

Another way in which “plague got rats” within this dividualist framework was in relation to grain, an “agent” whose role in the spread of plague was directly connected with governmental concerns—especially colonial ones—over trade and sustenance. As we saw in chapter 1, framings of grain as a potential source of plague already formed part of early, germ-theory-informed framings of the disease by the late 1870s. Between 1896 and 1899, other than in specialized publications revisiting the question of Mahamari, the theory of plague being caused by “fungoid grain” was not revisited in a systematic manner.100 Grain in this case was seen not as the generator but as a spreader of plague. It was often assumed that infected rats introduced the disease into grain depots, where they spread it to healthy rats and in turn contaminated the grain and grain bags, which subsequently became “sources of conveyance of the disease to human beings.”101

It was especially in relation to mastering the spread of plague across colonial locations that rats assumed an important role within this dividualist epidemiological reasoning. For, with the exception of humans, rats were the only plague agents able to move within and between locations out of their own volition. Both within and outside the confines of the Indian Plague Commission, a focus on the mobility patterns of rats led to the formation of a new figure that came to play an important role in “how plague got rats”: the migratory rat. As I have examined in detail elsewhere, this figure marked a radical break from the previously established figure of the staggering rat, insofar as it unfolded and configured epidemic space and time not in terms of a cosmopolitical eschaton but in terms of epidemiological progress.102 Ideas regarding rat movement across domestic and urban space were fostered by long-standing approaches to the animal as vermin (see the introduction)—an animal category imbued with traits of transgressiveness and pollution.103 This supposedly continuous movement of rats was a key factor in the spread of the disease in space and in perpetuity. In 1897, in the context of investigations of the spread of plague in Thana (a city just outside Bombay), the term the “rat’s progress” was coined to describe this movement.104 According to Collector of Thana A. C. Logan and other supporters of this theory, the “rat’s progress” involved not only spreading plague between different rat populations but also bringing rats in touch with other, nonrodent, agents of plague, and thus establishing new dividual networks of the disease.105 Different hypotheses regarding what caused the migration of rats, and whether rats migrated only within or also between localities, were soon developed, with their proponents, both British and Indian, clashing with one another as well as with detractors of the migratory rat theory.106 In some cases, rat migration was configured as a flight from plague-infected locations as a result of rats taking fright from the resulting epizootics therein.107 In others, it was simply attributed to what the best-selling English naturalist Edward Hamilton Aitken, known by the pen name of Eha, castigated as their propensity for vagrancy—a moral and juridical term that historians of medicine have shown to be tightly tied with contemporary ideas of human immigrants and the wandering poor as pathogenic agents.108 So “vagrant” a life did such rats lead, according to Aitken, that “any individual among them, catching the infection of plague at one end of the village, may die at the other end, after visiting every cranny of half the houses between.”109 Verminous, transgressive agency formed a key aspect of this framing of rats, and of the urgency to master human-rat relations. For, in this scenario, the propensity to foster networks of plague infection between humans, floors, clothes, bedding, food, and so on was attributed not simply to supposed migratory habits but also to the fact that “rats pry into everything.”110

### Experimenting with Rats

During the first half of 1897 a series of experiments was undertaken with rats, some of which had important consequences for dividualist framings of the animals as agents of plague. These experiments were carried out by British scientists as well as by members of foreign plague commissions, which had in the meantime arrived in India to study the epidemic. This was not the first time that international commissions or teams had converged on a location to study plague—the often-forgotten precedent was in response to the Vetlyanka plague in Russia (1878–1879), where German, French, British, and Romanian commissions were involved.111 Yet British India provided the first instance where, aided by the common ground of the bacteriological identification of the disease, international commissions competed and cooperated on experimental studies of the disease. With medical eyes turned to India, this, like the competition over the development of prophylactics (vaccines and sera), fostered what Pratik Chakrabarti has called an agonistic “experimental theatre” for deciphering the propagation of plague.112 Under the direction of Georg Theodor August Gaffky (Koch’s assistant who excelled first in identifying Salmonella typhi and later as a cholera researcher during the Hamburg epidemic of 1892–1893), the German Plague Commission (1897) placed particular emphasis on animal experiments.113 The German Commission conducted experiments involving rat inoculation and attempted to infect rats through the alimentary tract and their mucous membranes. The experiments proved the susceptibility of rats to plague, leading the German Commission to conclude that “since it is known that these animals in a state of freedom are accustomed to gnaw the bodies of their companions dead of plague, it is easy to understand that the pestilence must spread very quickly among them and destroy the whole rat-population of a place, and that by means of rats the plague germs can be introduced from one home into another and conveyed to men.”114 The findings were endorsed by the German pioneer of bacteriology, Robert Koch, who in his diary from his own plague expedition to India (1897) commented both on rat cannibalism as a mode of inter-rat transmission and on the infectivity of rats “from the uninjured mucous membranes and digestive tracts.”115

Mark Harrison has argued that infection through inoculation formed the focus of other foreign commissions, including the Russian and the Egyptian plague commissions, with the former prioritizing experiments with monkeys and the latter largely dismissing the role of rats in the spread of the disease.116 In fact, the position of the Egyptian Plague Commission was much more nuanced. Writing on his findings in 1897, the director of the Institute of Hygiene in Cairo, Dr. H. Bitter, argued that during his research in Bombay, in spite of everyone purporting to have witnessed ratfalls, he was only able to procure two dead rats, neither of which had plague.117 Bitter expressed his skepticism toward automatically associating rat epizootics with plague, as the former were frequently observed in contexts unrelated to plague outbreaks. On the one hand, he did not deny the fact that “plague could be contracted by rats by natural infection” leading to epizootics among the animals.118 On the other hand, he cautioned against both “attributing all extraordinary mortality among rats during an epidemic of plague to that cause” and exaggerating the extent of ratfalls as a result of “public rumour.”119 If Bitter was ready to admit that rats may play a role in the propagation of plague, particularly through their excreta and, to a lesser extent, through their cadavers, he decreed this to be insignificant by comparison to human-to-human infection. Whereas the Egyptian Plague Commission bypassed the question of how rats became infected, the Austrian Plague Commission claimed to have observed cervical buboes in a few animals, and as a result, it highlighted the possibility of rats becoming infected through the nose and mouth.120 Finally, on the part of the Italian plague delegation to India, Gino Galeotti performed “experiments on hundreds of rats of different races, and living in the most different conditions,” concluding that they were all susceptible to plague. Galeotti maintained that infected rats would always end up infecting healthy ones cohabiting the same cage, speculating that urine was the mode of transmission, with grain being another potential medium.121

Experiments on rats and plague did not, however, only take place in British India. In fact, some of the most important experiments were undertaken by Yersin and his Pasteurian colleagues in Paris. These experiments asked vital questions about how the virulence of plague bacteria was affected by the passage of the bacteria through rats. During his 1894 Hong Kong experiments, Yersin had observed that “in cultures derived from the blood or from a bubo of a plague patient, one could isolate many varieties of the bacilli differing among themselves in their virulence towards animals, and certain cultures have even lost all virulence for the guinea pig.”122 Even more strikingly, in the conclusion of his 1894 paper on the discovery of the bacillus, Yersin remarked, “By inoculating the pulp of a ganglion that had been extricated from a convalescent patient for three weeks, I was able to obtain a few cultures absolutely devoid of any virulence, even for the mouse.”123 By contrast, the bacillus isolated from a recovered patient proved to be virulent in both mice and rats. This led Yersin to speculate that “the inoculation of certain more or less virulent races or varieties of the bacillus would be without a doubt capable of conferring a certain immunity against plague to animals.”124

Yersin undertook further experiments in order to ascertain the impact of plague’s variation in virulence. Key to these was the notion, already discussed by Pasteur in 1881, that the passage of microbes between animals affected plague’s virulence. In his discussion of epidemics, Pasteur had speculated that such successive passage through animals of the same species reinforced virulence, rendering microbes otherwise incapable of “attacking” humans able to do so: “By this method, new virulences and contagions can be created. I am very inclined to believe that this is how smallpox, syphilis, plague, yellow fever, etc., have appeared through the ages, and that it is also by phenomena of this kind that from time to time certain great epidemics appear.”125 The experiments conducted in 1895 by Yersin, Roux, Amédée Borrel, and Albert Calmette in Paris were published in the Annales de l’Institut Pasteur in a paper coauthored by Yersin, Calmette, and Borrel (but curiously not Roux); this was a publication that came to play an important role in the development of the Institut Pasteur’s plague serum (see chapter 3).126 The “second note” on the bubonic plague, as the article was subtitled, argued that conducting a “series of passages” led to a “fixed virulence for the animal species” involved, leading to bacilli capable of killing mice and rabbits.127

Two years later, in 1897, Yersin published what was widely considered his second trailblazing article on plague, focusing on the development of a plague serum. There, Yersin stated, “A rat still takes the disease if it is made to ingest a culture of the plague bacillus[;] it can then contaminate other healthy rats placed in the same cage. This is how, starting from a pure culture, we give birth to an epidemic which does not differ from spontaneous epidemics other than that it remains limited to a cage instead of extending to an entire city.”128 This striking rhetorical turn where the cage comes to stand for the city may sound like an excellent illustration of “Pasteurization” to ears long-attuned to Latour’s interpretation of Yersin’s research.129 If we are, however, to elude philosophical reductionism and try to understand Yersin’s intended argument, we need to pay close attention to his epidemiological reasoning. Yersin used the cage-city metonymy not in order to render the laboratory into an epistemic model but to stress the irreducibly relational character of pestigenic agents: “At the time of plague epidemics, and even after the disease has disappeared, one finds, in the soil of infected localities, a microbe exactly similar to that of the plague, but less virulent than that removed from buboes.”130

Yersin was back to his soil theory, as first developed in Hong Kong, but with a twist: “This microbe,” he wrote, “is preserved in the earth, and it is conceivable that the rats can become contaminated if the circumstances are favorable. This is how epidemics awaken.”131 While imbuing it with unmistakably gothic properties of “awakening” the slumbering monster of “Nature,” Yersin provided no new evidence to support this claim on recrudescence. Rather he returned to a key Pasteurian moment of epidemiological reasoning, already examined above: Pasteur’s “amazing prescience,” as Yersin called it, of identifying plague’s attenuation and recrudescence mechanism in relation to the Benghazi outbreak of 1856–1858—“Experiment has entirely confirmed M. Pasteur’s ideas.”132 What we have here, in fact, is no mere confirmation of Pasteur’s genius but the emergence of a new concept within Pasteurian thinking: that of the bacterial passage from one plague agent (the soil) to another (rats) being a catalyst of recrudescence—an interagent jump that, as it were, reawakens plague.133 We need to note the mutually entangled epistemological and political impact of this new forging together of plague and rats. First, Yersin configured rats as epidemiological dividuals, in so far as they became agents of plague (more precisely, of its recrudescence) only in reference to other agents of plague (more precisely, of its attenuation), such as the soil.134 Second, this epidemiological concept carried with it an acutely racialized sting. “This etiology,” Yersin argued, “explains to us why the plague rages with such intensity in countries like China, where families live piled up, on soil soiled with rubbish of all kinds, visited by rats. The plague, which was initially a disease of rats, soon became a disease of man.”135

On the one hand, this formed a common ground between the Pasteurian vanguard and competing theories of virulence and recrudescence, which targeted immigrants and their dwellings as foyers of infectious diseases, fitting well within broader pathologizations of Indigenous and working-class lifeways.136 On the other hand, Yersin’s return to the epidemiological topos of the racialized, Orientalized dwelling should be examined closer in terms of its epistemological specificity. To fully understand the way in which dividualist epistemology and colonial politics became mutually entangled, we need to pay attention to a tension between two registers of causality that underline Yersin’s conclusion. On the one hand, read etiologically, it suggests that Chinese dwellings were no longer treated—like working-class, immigrant, and Indigenous homes had been for decades, and as Yersin’s earlier notes had reflected—as the “conditions” of infection. Rather they were seen as the catalysts of bacteriological interrelations between soil, rubbish, and rats, which eventually allowed plague to infect humans through an increase in virulence (recrudescence). In this way, previously hegemonic pythogenic framings of Chinese dwelling became unsettled. They were no longer problematized in terms of filth per se, but in terms of specific attributes that were believed to attract rats or to facilitate their ability to interrelate a series of plague agents in a complex bacteriological process of attenuation, recrudescence, and, eventually, infection. On the other hand, read ontologically, Yersin’s statement “the plague, which was initially a disease of rats, soon became a disease of man” alluded to an idea of cause as origin. It thus fostered an epidemiological imagination of Chinese modes of dwelling as the locus of the original transformation of plague from a disease of animals to a disease of humans. In other words, it suggested that so-called Oriental Plague was not “Oriental” just because it arrived from the East, but also because it emerged there in the first place as a result of Chinese dwelling practices, which fostered rat-catalyzed dividual relations of plague infection.137 At once transforming the long-held medical-juridical construct of the “insalubrious home,” on an epistemological level, and maintaining its moral-ontological efficacy, Yersin strove for a new sanitary-bacteriological synthesis via the figure of the all-connecting, epidemiologically dividual rat.

### Conclusion

In this chapter I have examined “how plague got rats” during the first five years of the Third Plague Pandemic, with a particular focus on the two most affected areas at the time: Hong Kong and India. What the examination of forging together rats and plague over this period demonstrates is that following the bacteriological discovery of the plague bacterium, rats came under sustained observation and experimentation as agents of plague. Rather than, however, this being a process of trying to identify or isolate rats as hosts or vectors of the disease, it was a process aimed at relating the rat to other suspected plague agents. The broader scope of these investigations was to master sets of situated, pestigenic relations between humans, rats, clothes, the soil, excreta, and grain. Whether by means of ideas about animal migration or through elaborate experiments on and theories about virulence, attenuation, and recrudescence, rats assumed important roles in these frameworks of plague agency. At all times, however, rats remained what I have called epidemiological dividuals. Their pestigenic agency was, in other words, made sense of and acted upon as instituted through their complex and dynamic relations with other agents of plague. This indivisible, relational, or dividual agency formed the productive core of plague science between 1894 and 1899, as it was these pestigenic relations rather than rats in and of themselves as plague agents that colonial medicine attempted to problematize and master.

Epidemiological dividualism allowed competing theories about how plague is spread or maintained to emerge and contend in dynamic ways. At the same time, it retained and reworked colonial, racialized understandings of the disease, which fostered broader forms of imperial governance. These invested on earlier configurations of plague as an infection of locality, imagined to arise from non-Western, Indigenous modes of life and dwelling, while always maintaining “multiple and ambiguous identifications of the local.”138 However, rather than basing this approach on previously hegemonic ideas about filth, or simply on a synthesis between bacteriology and sanitarianism, dividualist framings of plague unsettled established colonial medical doctrines. What were now being increasingly seen as leading to plague epidemics were not Indigenous dwelling and lifeways as medico-juridical conditions of infection but the ways in which they supposedly allowed potential plague agents to relate and interact with one another.

As a result, this framework marked a shift of epidemiological blame of racialized Others from an ontological to an etiological field. Rather than signaling a softening of antiplague policy, this led to more complex and intrusive interventions on Indigenous lives and lifeways. While the hitherto hegemonic destruction or burning down of Indigenous houses continued during and after this period, new methods of controlling plague were also introduced.139 These added new sovereign layers of intervention, such as the evacuation of towns and cities and the segregation of their populations in plague camps.140 But they also introduced new disciplinary layers of what, following Graham Mooney, we may call “intrusive intervention.”141 In many cases these required the participation of Indigenous subjects, the adoption of antiplague practices from below, and an internalization of epidemiological principles and norms. Rat-catching and rat-poisoning began to feature centrally in plague control.142 The disinfection of clothes, household items, and entire houses became widespread in Hong Kong and India.143 Deroofing houses in order to desiccate plague bacteria in the soil forming the floor of houses, removing the soil and baking it in ovens, and using mobile soil-desiccating stoves inside houses were methods particularly focused on the soil as another dividual agent of plague.144

These methods and practices propelled official careers and economic and industrial interests (of chemical companies in particular), as well as scientific ambitions. They also marked the emergence of epidemic response as an integrated field of mastery, where the control of plague could assume biopolitical proportions both in the sense of fostering new forms of instituting and managing colonized or national populations, and in the sense of bringing together the governance of humans and nonhumans, no longer as isolatable epidemic-control targets but as systems of life, disease, and death. The rat as an epidemiological dividual sat at the center of this project for mastery, being able to mobilize epidemiological reasoning, pandemic imaginaries, and epidemic governance and finally tune their interrelations in situated and context-adaptive ways.

## In Search of Lost Fleas

A rat captured in a plague-infested district of colonial Karachi is placed inside a large glass jar. To add to the preexisting number of fleas hosted by the animal, twenty more (captured on a cat) are thrown into the jar. Twenty-four hours later, the rat is already close to dying. At that point, a small iron cage containing a second rat is lowered into the jar. One side of the cage is covered in wire mesh. Soon after, the first rat dies but is not removed until thirty-six hours later. The necropsy certifies it died of plague. Left inside the cage in the jar, the second rat dies on the fifth day of the experiment. Having been originally healthy, its necropsy certifies plague.1 The conclusion: The second rat could have only been infected by means of the fleas carried by the first rat, “not only because a wire mesh separated the animal in the experiment from the plague rat, but above all because we have never succeeded in infecting a rat or a mouse by placing them in contact with animals inoculated in the laboratory and free from parasites.”2

Few experiments are better known in epidemiology than the one published in the Annales de l’Institut Pasteur in October 1898 by the Pasteurian doctor Paul-Louis Simond, proving that rat fleas are vectors of the plague-causing bacterium known today as Yersinia pestis. Coupled with clinical observations of a blister found in human plague patients, which Simond attributed to fleabites, it is an experiment that is today widely recognized as marking a pivotal step toward the “conquest” of plague.3 Indeed, the Institut Pasteur celebrates the accomplishment annually, on June 2.4 The combination of utter simplicity and the experiment’s ability to finally identify the transmission pathway of one of the most dreaded diseases in human history has made Simond’s experiment one of the most iconic moments of a simple experimental design and a great idea coming together to produce a true scientific breakthrough.

Or that is what we have been led to believe by accounts based on Simond’s original description of the experiment (1898) and its longer recollection nearly forty years later (1936).5 If the 1898 article was not immediately recognized as bringing about the breakthrough that Simond and the Institut Pasteur had been undoubtedly hoping for (see chapter 4), the experiment became gradually solidified in medicine’s experimental and hero-scientist pantheon following the Second World War.6 This eureka moment has been enshrined not only in institutional histories of the Institut Pasteur but also in scholarly historical works, which have unreservedly lauded Simond’s discovery: “Doctor Paul-Louis Simond casts a giant shadow on the destiny of mankind. He lifted the veil that until then had covered the mysterious means of plague transmission,” wrote Edward Crawford Jr. in the definitive article on Simond’s plague research.7

And yet, surprisingly, to date no historical account of this iconic discovery has gone back to the archive to examine Simond’s experimental notes and ask: What led Simond to undertake his famous experiment? What ideas did Simond hold about rats, fleas, and plague transmission at the time? What was the place of Simond’s rat/flea studies within his broader research on plague in British India, and within the dividualist ontology of rat’s relation to plague current at the time? What evidence do we have of his famous experiment or of other experiments with rats or their fleas from his notes and correspondence?

### Simond’s India Mission

Born in 1858, Paul-Louis Simond joined the Institut Pasteur in 1895 after studying medicine at Bordeaux and serving as Médecin de 1ère classe des Colonies in French Guyana and in Guangzhouwan (Kouang-Tchéou-Wan), a French treaty port in South China.8 Returning to Paris in 1895, Simond worked in Ilya Ilyich Metchnikoff’s laboratory on the subject of the “biology of coccidians parasitising intestinal tissue of rabbits and salamanders,” in the process identifying coccidian flagella as associated with the male sex of the organism—something that later contributed to understanding of the “life cycle of the malarial parasite.”9 He thus combined colonial medical experience with a pedigree of laboratory work on parasites in metropolitan France, working alongside one of the most influential scientists of his time. Having been appointed director of the bacteriological laboratory of Saigon in 1897, Simond was soon called to replace the discoverer of the plague pathogen, Alexandre Yersin, at Institut Pasteur’s mission to British India in May 1897.10

[그림/Figure] Figure 3.1 Dr. Paul-Louis Simond injecting a patient with Yersin’s plague serum in Karachi, India, Vishandas Hospital, June 4, 1898

As we saw in chapter 2, a number of foreign commissions conducted scientific research on plague during the first four years of the epidemic in British India (1896–1899). Institut Pasteur’s mission was initially led by Yersin and was primarily intended to test and further develop the plague serum that Yersin had coinvented in Paris in 1895, and which he had originally tested successfully during an outbreak of plague in Canton and Amoy (China, June–July 1896).11 Though designed to cure rather than prevent infection, the serum soon came to stand in direct competition with prophylactic methods of plague control, marking what Pratik Chakrabarti has called the “experimental theatre of vaccines”—most importantly, the serum competed with the plague vaccine developed for the British in Bombay (1897) by the Waldemar Haffkine.12 With Yersin returning to Indochina only three months into his India Mission, his replacement, Simond (fig. 3.1), spent nearly fourteen months in British India (June 1897 to August 1898, with a break between February 10 and March 2, 1898, when he returned briefly to Saigon) conducting research on the serum in, among other places, Bombay, Agra, Karad, Mandvi, and Karachi.

The global history of the Pasteurian plague serum has been recently examined for the first time in detail by Matheus Alves Duarte da Silva.13 For the purposes of understanding Simond’s research on rats and fleas and their role in the transmission of plague, it is important to keep two things in mind. First, that Simond’s posting to India was not primarily aimed at studying plague’s transmission pathway but rather at developing and proving the efficacy of Institut Pasteur’s serum. Second, that his rat/flea research unfolded in a context that was causing embarrassment to and pressure on the Institut Pasteur.14 This was because, by early 1898, it was becoming increasingly clear that the serum faced a range of problems. Duarte da Silva’s meticulous examination of Simond’s research has shown that, by the end of his mission, Simond was unable to prove the efficacy of the serum. In a report on his tests with plague patients in Karachi, he admitted that his tests had left much to be desired: 55 percent of the patients treated with the serum succumbed to the disease, marking a staggering difference from Yersin’s previous tests in Canton and Amoy (1896) where only 6 percent of patients given the serum had died of plague.15

This presented a significant economic problem for Institut Pasteur, as the serum was at the time one of its key global commodities.16 But it was also a problem of imperial prestige. For, having previously claimed the discovery of plague’s pathogen, over the competing claim by Japan’s Kitasato Shibasaburō, Institut Pasteur was now also aiming to claim the discovery of the cure of the disease.17 In short, as “a diplomatic strategy centered on the production and distribution of the anti-plague serum,” by 1898, Institut Pasteur’s imperial ambition stood severely challenged by the actual limitations of its product.18 If the aim of the India Mission was not just to establish and improve the efficacy of the serum but also to showcase it to the world, then, for all Simond’s optimism that progress was in sight, this was a major scientific, economic, and public relations debacle for Institut Pasteur.

### Simond and the Dividual Rat

How did Simond take interest in the subject of rats and their fleas in the context of his ultimately unsatisfactory research on and demonstration of the efficacy of the Pasteurian plague serum? To answer this question, we need to follow rats and their fleas as they appear in Simond’s notebooks and correspondence before and during his experiments with the animals, which commenced in May 1898. Only through this examination of everyday note-taking and its cross-examination with Simond’s published work can we begin to understand how he developed his framing of rat-/flea-borne transmission and propagation of plague. Though both fieldnotes and letters on the subject are often laconic and rarely if ever continuous, they are our best chance to understand which narratives about the relation between rats, fleas, and plague Simond found convincing at the time and what theories of his own he developed over the course of Institut Pasteur’s India Mission. Whereas the 1898 article presented a teleological narrative, where various angles on the rat’s relation to plague were brought together so as to lead to Simond’s “discovery” of the role of rat fleas in the transmission of the disease, in his fieldnotes and letters we encounter a much more complex, syncopated, and ambiguous framing of the rat-plague relation, where fleas appear only fleetingly up until the moment of the experiment that would be the cause of their fame in the context of Simond’s biography and research.

The sources available for reconstituting Simond’s study of and experiments with rats during the India Mission are two notebooks and his correspondence, all held at the Institut Pasteur archives. Of these, Simond’s carnet “Observ. concern. épid. de peste” (1897–1898, henceforth “the carnet”) is the most extensive and richest source and yet also one that has been overlooked in historical studies of Simond. The carnet is a squared, cardboard-bound notebook (17.5 × 22 cm, or 6.9 × 6.7 in.) composed of 129 unnumbered pages. It initially takes the form of a dated diary, starting September 1, 1897, but this is interrupted after November 22, 1897. Following that entry, the carnet contains fieldnotes and analyses as well as longer retrospective discourses and reflections, all intermingled with occasional diary-like notes, some dated and some not. Simond’s day-to-day notes in the carnet stop with his departure for Saigon (February 10, 1898), with Simond providing a summary of his research to that point over fifteen densely written pages, and noting that he spent the twenty days that he had to wait for an Indochina-bound boat by “treating some plague patients and completing my observations on the propagation of the disease.”19

Simond did not resume his day-to-day note-taking after his return to British India in March 1898. Instead, what takes up the rest of the carnet is a long discourse on the propagation of plague. The discourse appears to be a sketch of Simond’s 1898 article (or of a nonsurviving draft leading to it), including detailed notes on the rat experiments discussed in the Annales de l’Institut Pasteur.20 However, the carnet’s long discourse is not identical to the 1898 article. The two differ on several points, including significant passages of epidemiological argument. The nature of this long discourse is moreover strikingly different to the notes proceeding it in the carnet, as—although clearly being a text in progress, punctuated by extensive deletions, ellipses, and corrections—this is a cumulative, synthetic, and nonspontaneous text. It is unclear when the text was written; different writing styles suggest that it was produced gradually rather than at one sitting. A detailed examination of the carnet would be indispensable for an in-depth historical examination of Institut Pasteur’s India Mission.

The carnet opens with a note, which provides the earliest evidence of an interest in rats by Simond during his part in the India Mission: “In Karad plague began in [unreadable] we found […] dead rats inside the gr[ain] shops [unreadable] various houses. Karad is a great grain market.”21 Further down on the same page, Simond wrote (September 16, 1897): “In Karad, in ordinary times, there is a great commerce of grains, which explains, it seems to me, due to the great number of rats that exist in this town, that the epidemic develops with such intensity.”22 These short notes clearly indicate that, already by September 1897, four months into his expedition, Simond was well immersed in colonial framings of rats in relation to the spread of plague that were prevalent during the first year of the pandemic in British India. Contrary to Simond’s 1898 article, which dismisses British colonial scientific opinions and debates on the role of rats in the transmission of plague as “too timid,” his carnet notes indicate that he was well aware of the extent, complexity, and importance of these debates as regards introducing new understandings of the disease.23

Simond’s understanding of the rat’s role in the propagation of plague sat comfortably within prevalent framings of the rat, examined in the preceding chapter, as an epidemiological dividual. That Simond actively partook in this relational ontology of the rat for the greatest part of his India Mission is evident in his fieldnotes and correspondence, where his key concern was the “propagation of plague,” a notion that referred both to the transmission of the disease and to what was generally known at the time as the “progress” of the disease across geographical space. Simond’s 1898 article and the draft contained in his carnet followed various pathways of reasoning about this question, which brought different potential hosts of plague in relation to one another. In his 1898 article, Simond argued that when imported human cases preceded indigenous ones, a period of more than one month intervened between the two, followed by a “period of latency” during which cases were localized or manifested a seemingly sporadic pattern before developing into a full epidemic.24 For Simond, it was during the period linking the latent and the epidemic stage, “a period of rapid growth of the epidemic so that it reaches its acute state,” that “the possibility of the extension of the plague without a human intermediary appears clearly.”25 This argument is not, however, present in the carnet draft, where another argument is developed in its place. There, Simond portrayed what he called the “simultaneous” infection of people inhabiting a household as a significant epidemiological datum that, he argued, excluded the possibility of human-to-human contagion and could be attributed instead to infection through the soil or items in the household, which had been in turn infected by rats.26

For Simond, one of the reasons that rats played a key role in the propagation of plague was their supposed propensity to migrate in response to the disease—a theory that, as we have seen in chapter 2, formed a staple of British colonial approaches to plague in India at the time. In an entry in his carnet dated January 16, 1898, Simond noted that the Bombay Plague Commission had ascertained that the death of rats preceded that of humans, and that the former were observed to “emigrate” from Bombay to surrounding villages, thus creating new foyers of the disease.27 The note directly mentions Thana, which indicates that Simond might have been exposed to the “rat’s progress” theory developed at the time (see chapter 2).28 The idea of migrating rats seems to have had an early and lasting impact on Simond’s reasoning regarding the drivers of the propagation of plague. In the carnet’s entry dated January 16, 1898, he described the inspection of a house in the Bombay quarter of Mandvi, in July 1897, where two young girls had fallen victim to the disease: “We did not see dead rats in the house, but the rats had disappeared 10 or 12 days before.” Simond concluded that this might be due to the outbreak of plague causing “their emigration.”29 Commenting on the subject of rat migration, in an earlier letter to his mentor at the headquarters of the Institut Pasteur in Paris, Émile Roux, Simond had speculated that rats fled infected locations once noticing ratfalls among them and that, in this way, “in short distances (the maximum observed by me is 3km [1.9 mi.]) plague can be transported by rats without the intermediary of humans.”30

This epidemiological reasoning would be clearly formulated in Simond’s later meditations on rats, where he maintained that, in the course of the peak of the epizootic, “panic arises, which determines the emigration of the majority of the rats,” thus reducing their numbers in affected areas.31 The rat’s alleged migratory nature was consequently used as the key to explain another of what Simond called the “mysterious points in the history of the plague”: its disappearance and eventual recrudescence in a given location after a period of time (which Simond called accalmie, or lull).32 That in both the carnet’s draft and the 1898 article Simond dismissed the role of the seasons in this process of attenuation and recrudescence is not surprising, as Pasteurian and British understandings of plague largely excluded it from the frame of tropical diseases. What is significant, however, is that, contrary to what we would expect from a Pasteurian and collaborator of Yersin, Simond did not resort to the hypothesis that the soil acted as a reservoir of the disease.33 Instead, he framed rats as responsible for this phenomenon: The period of “lull,” which Simond saw as a period of a locality’s immunity to plague, was essentially a time when plague could not affect rats.35

In summary we can presume know that a large part of the rats in a town die during the plague epidemic, that a large part run away and others that a certain proportion of them that remain are immunized having experienced a non-fatal form of plague. If the rat is as we think the principal [written above: essential] propagator of plague, there is reason to admit that a new human epidemic would not be able to break out until young generations of rats susceptible to contract the disease have repopulated the town.34

While the argument connecting rats to plague’s recrudescence is mostly identical in the carnet and the 1898 article, in the former we find a passage that is not included in the latter. Simond speculated that one of the reasons for the “lull” and subsequent recrudescence of plague in a given locality was what we would today call ethological: “The natural distrust of the rat may be a reason that the survivors of the epidemic avoid the causes of contamination[;] it is very likely, for example, that they renounce the habit of eating the corpses of their fellow creatures after having witnessed the mortality resulting from such feasts. It would then be expected that later generations would resume this custom favorable to the extension of the epidemic.”36

Cannibalism had been framed as a source of plague infection among rats during the experiments conducted by the German Plague Commission to India (1897), an association endorsed by Robert Koch (see chapter 2) and disseminated in English by Robert Nathan in the first volume of his authoritative report on plague, commissioned by the British India colonial government’s Home Department.37 Simond’s notes on rat cannibalism fall comfortably within this bacteriologically backed framing of rat-to-rat infection and the hypergothic image of vermin cannibalism being a driver of plague. And yet, we need to take a closer look at what is entailed in this idea of plague’s lull as a result of rats’ ethological transformation. For, being here at the mythic heart of epidemiological reasoning as applied by Pasteurian science to plague, we might rightly ask, following Claude Lévi-Strauss: “When a myth attributes to an animal a certain behavior under determined circumstances, does it follow an empirical observation, or an independent but unfounded belief, or does this attribution result from an internal constraint to the myth?”38

The idea that cannibalism drives inter-rat infection and that plague’s recrudescence is the result of rats somehow learning not to cannibalize one another reveals a complex interplay between plague and rat ontologies in Simond’s epidemiological reasoning. On the one hand, plague epizootics were seen to be dependent on rats’ supposedly cannibalistic nature, an idea potentially connected to notions of rat cannibalism as a population self-limitation mechanism going back to the early nineteenth century.39 On the other hand, rather than plague being simply the result of rat cannibalism, it was portrayed as if it also had a transformative agency upon rats. For, as a result of observing plague-induced ratfalls, rats were said to suspend their natural character and abstain from eating one another, thus preserving themselves from further infection and ending the epizootic. This self-limiting property of plague through working directly on the supposed nature of rats needs to be compared here with the self-propagating faculty of the disease implied in the aforementioned notion of rat migration as a key plague-spreading mechanism. In this case, plague-induced ratfalls were said to cause “panic” among rats, leading them to flee, thus spreading the disease to new locations. It is tempting here, if also highly speculative, to wonder whether the idea of “contagion” as a phenomenon characterizing crowds, developed by Gustave Le Bon, had an impact on Simond’s epidemiological reasoning, with contagion qua plague in rats leading to contagion qua panic among them, which in turn further spread contagion qua plague.40 Whatever the case may be, here plague was also portrayed as having a direct ethological impact on rats, which, if perhaps not as profound as that of suspending their supposedly cannibalistic nature, was said to have a significant epidemiological implication. From this perspective, rats were pivotal in plague’s life cycle only insofar as they were transformed by the disease in two distinct ways: The first, behavioral transformation (panic-driven flight), resulted in the propagation of plague into new locations, while the second, ontological transformation (refraining from cannibalism), led to the quiescence of the disease in the affected location.

Taken together, as revealing a tacitly formulated but discernible system that relied on an acute synergy between epidemiological reasoning and epidemic imagination, Simond’s notes on rat migration and cannibalism are very troubling for the teleological reading of his studies of and experiments with rats, as this was constructed in his 1898 article and subsequently accepted by historians. What we can say is that eight months into his India Mission, Simond had not only endorsed the complex, dividualist epidemiological framing of rats and plague already developed by British colonial doctors but had also formed his own framing of the rat’s relation to plague, which innovatively proposed plague’s supposedly transformative impact on rats as a sine qua non of both the propagation and the self-limitation of the disease.

### Plague by the Alimentary Tract

Key to many approaches that scrutinized the rat’s role in the propagation of plague in a relational manner was the idea of transmission through the alimentary tract, which, as we have already seen in chapter 2, found its most coherent form in the 1897 report on plague in Hong Kong by Staff-Surgeon Wilm of the Imperial German Navy.41 In his 1898 article, Simond rejected the theory of alimentary infection, presenting his discovery of the flea vector as the only viable alternative. By contrast, his field notes indicate a far more receptive approach to the idea. Indeed, as we have already seen (the case of Karad), a key entry point for Simond’s interest in rats was their connection to grain—an object that was intricately connected to questions regarding the relation between rats and plague in the course of the first years of the pandemic in the Indian subcontinent. A key concern for colonial authorities was that infected rats might introduce plague into grain depots, where they were attracted for nourishment (see chapter 2). There, it was feared, rats could spread plague to healthy rats, as well as contaminate grain and gunny bags, rendering these into “sources of conveyance of the disease to human beings.”42

Grain had been experimentally tested for plague in the first half of 1897 by the leading British experimental bacteriologist in India, Ernest Hanbury Hankin. In a letter dated February 19, 1897, Hankin stated that he “never succeeded in detecting” plague in grain.43 Nonetheless, framings of grain as implicated in the spread of the disease remained prevalent and were further fueled by the question of how plague was introduced to Karachi during the city’s second epidemic of the disease (1898). Reports and testimonies given to the Indian Plague Commission (1897–1899) maintained that dead rats (which had been conspicuously absent in the first outbreak between December 1896 and July 1897) were found in gunny bags and in grain houses and mills.44 The majority of these grain-containing sacks had reportedly arrived from Bombay, fueling the question of the interrelation between grain and rats in introducing and spreading the disease to new locations.45

Simond seemed to agree with the plausibility of this theory, also allowing for the infection of Karachi through the direct importation of rats from Bombay.46 In a short discussion of the plague outbreak in Haridwar in the spring–summer of 1897, he also speculated on the reasons why at the end of the epidemic in the great pilgrimage city, an epizootic of rats was observed in nearby Kankhal.47 Simond reasoned that the disease spread through infected foodstuff being transported from “infected houses” in Haridwar to Kankhal, where they “were eaten by rats and spread the [epidemic] among them.”48 In a letter to Roux (October 22, 1897), Simond clarified that it was not grain per se that he considered as a possible host of plague but rat excrement soiling it: “In Bombay, grain was accused of being the source of the epidemic and people of importance claimed that it was neither rats nor microbes but wheat and rice that transmitted plague. There is an element of truth in this ignorant reasoning, deriving from the droppings of sick rats, the microbe must frequently use grain as a vehicle, and in humid weather as a growing medium.”49

Furthermore, in a manner that amplified the dividualist entanglement of the rat as propagator of plague, Simond speculated that there may also be an excrement-mediated airborne pathway between rats, grain, and humans: “The person who grinds the grain, by inhaling the cloud of flour that forms around the grindstone has a good chance, if the grain has been soiled by plague-infected rats, to absorb the microbe through the lungs.”50

Simond was acutely interested in the question of so-called external sources of plague, which offered itself as a major field for experimentation. In order to test the “principal hypothesis that the microbe exists in the exterior milieu,” he proceeded by conducting a series of experiments, reproducing designs previously employed by British and German bacteriologists.51 Of these, the twelve contained in his carnet and in the 1898 article are identical.52 Of the twelve, nine focused on testing different modes of alimentary infection. They involved feeding rats and mice with agar preparations containing plague bacilli (Experiments I, II), organs (Experiments III, V, VIII), blood (Experiments III, IV), and cadavers of plague-infected rats and mice (Experiment III), as well as human bloody expectorations (Experiment VI) and grain mixed with urine of a human plague patient (Experiment VII). Additionally, Simond performed two alimentary-related experiments with other animals, repeating Experiment VII with a monkey (Experiment IX) and feeding a ground squirrel (“rat palmiste”) with bread soaked in the blood of a rat dead from plague (Experiment XII). All animals, with the exception of those in Experiment IV, survived without contracting plague.

Experiment IV involved the inoculation of a rat with plague bacteria. The rat was then placed inside a glass jar containing grain. After its death, the rat was removed and another one was placed in the jar among the grain, which was now “soiled by the excretions of rat no. 1”; the rat was kept there for five days without manifesting plague.53 Yet, when a third rat was given the blood of the first rat diluted in water, it died of plague in four hours. However, Simond dismissed this evidence, arguing that the fact that the third rat had a wound on its lower lip and that it developed a cervical bubo indicated an infection through the wound rather than through the alimentary tract.

It was the findings of these experiments that, according to Simond’s article and carnet draft, led him to seek other possible transmission pathways, and in particular one that involved “parasitism.”

### Fleas in the Archive

In his carnet, Simond noted that following the experiments showing the ineffectiveness of the alimentary tract in plague infection there needed to be “a natural means of penetration of the microbe into the epidermis apart from the accidental lymphothetic excoriations admitted until now,” and that this was no other than “parasitism and the flea is the parasite which by its habits seemed to us a priori capable of playing the role of inoculator.”54 But just how a priori was this assumption in his epidemiological reasoning?

When it comes to Simond’s interest in rat fleas, the archive is much less replete than in relation to rats or the alimentary tract. The first note on the possibility of fleas being involved in plague transmission appears in an undated entry, made before January 16, 1898, on the ninth page of the carnet. The note simply reads: “And the possibility of contamination by rat fleas.”55 The single-line note is located in a half-page section of Simond’s carnet, titled “Plague,” which is composed of eight similarly short, one- and two-line notes. These laconic jottings are for the most part abbreviated, making it difficult to be sure about their intended meaning, but they almost all concern rat-to-rat plague transmission, through rat feces and urine, cannibalism, and grain.

The purpose of these notes is not clear, yet it is evident that, by mid-January 1898, Simond was considering fleas as a mode of rat-to-rat transmission, among others. On the other hand, no discussion of this potential transmission pathway is present in his correspondence at the time. The only other indication of an interest in the subject, prior to the June 1898 experiment, is a short bibliographical note on page thirty-seven of his carnet.56 This stand-alone note, positioned at the top of a page left otherwise blank, is significant as it mentions two key works on plague transmission by means of insects, in the following order: George H. F. Nuttall’s “On the Role of Animals in the Spread of Plague” and Ogata Masanori’s “On the Plague Epidemic in Formosa,” both published in 1897 in the reputable medical journal Centralblatt für Bakteriologie und Parasitenkunde.57

The article by Nuttall, an American-British bacteriologist and pioneer of insect-borne parasitology, contained a detailed comparative review of research on the susceptibility of different animals to plague. He further noted a series of experiments that sought to establish the involvement of flies and bedbugs in plague transmission. Nuttall stated that experiments showed that flies died of plague and that they could “live for several days, after they have received the infected food, and one cannot therefore deny that they can play a role in the spread of plague, when they fall into foodstuffs or leave their excrement on them.”58 By contrast, he found that “plague bacilli eventually die out in bedbugs,” rendering them improbable transmitters of the disease.59 Though crucial at the time for its systematic review of insect-borne transmission, Nuttall’s article makes no mention of fleas.

Ogata conducted his research in response to an outbreak of plague in Taiwan, at the time a Japanese colony. The epidemic had broken out in October 1896 and by June 1897 had led to forty-five deaths. Ogata, who was in charge of bacteriological investigations, reported that during his stay in Taihoku he “had 6 dead rats at [his] disposal.” He added, “Two were brought to me by another doctor, two I found on the street, one came from the barracks and one was brought by a sanitation officer.”60 What follows is a fascinating account of interspecies encounter in a laboratory setting: “Since on opening the package I saw some fleas on the animals, one rat was immediately wrapped up again, the other was doused with a 1/1000 sublimate solution, whereby 20 fleas were caught. This rat had in the middle of its tail a crust of the size of a rice grain, by removing which one saw a deep ulcer covered by pus.”61 Pure cultures of plague being delivered, Ogata proceeded with catching fifteen fleas from the second rat, which was placed in sterile water: “Seven of the fleas caught in the sterile water were now crushed between two sterilized glasses and two mice were inoculated with them. One of these mice died after three days.”62 Ogata’s conclusion was that fleas could carry plague and spread it to animals, including potentially humans. “It is possible,” Ogata concluded, “that plague is actually (as the story of the illness in Formosa taught us) first a rat pest, and this animal is the nearest cause of the spread of this disease to humans.”63

Though Simond’s note on the two works is very short, it crucially mentions that Ogata had shown that “fleas from dead rats transmit plague.”64 Historians’ negation of or doubts regarding Simond’s knowledge of Ogata’s work at the time of his experiments with rats and fleas are thus completely unjustified and unfounded.65 While Simond did not mention Ogata in his 1898 article, he was clearly aware of Ogata’s research and the fact that he was the first to show the transmission of plague by means of fleas. Moreover, the place of the note in the carnet (which followed a strictly chronological order) shows that Simond knew this at the time of undertaking his own rat and flea experiments.

The only other source indicating Simond’s engagement with the rat/flea hypothesis before his summer 1898 experiments came from his correspondence with Hankin. In his letters to Simond, the British bacteriologist brought up rats as early as May 18, 1898, when he mentioned his experiments in Haridwar showing an attenuation of the plague bacterium by means of subsequent passages through animals.66 As was extensively discussed in his testimony to the Indian Plague Commission, Hankin speculated that this could mean that “rats could transport the infection but not conserve it.”67 The supposed inability of rats to act as reservoirs of plague in interepidemic intervals caused Hankin to seek an alternative host. Hankin put the question to Simond thus: “But what does the microbe do during this long incubation period in the locality? Not likely that it passes through the rats, because by that process it becomes attenuated. Perhaps in some other animal it finds the conditions to restore its virulence. Maybe an insect, the fly? Please tell me what you think about these ideas. I hope to be able to experiment on this point.”68

Interest in insects as possible vectors of plague involved an expansive list of species ever since Yersin, who in the article announcing the discovery of plague’s bacterium, declared his suspicion of flies as the disease’s vector.69 As a result, experiments of insect-borne plague infection preceded Ronald Ross’s discovery of the mosquito as a vector of malaria (August 20, 1897), which is usually seen by historians as the milestone in the study of insect-borne transmission. Like Nuttall, Hankin was interested in ascertaining the infectivity of bedbugs, testing his theory by injecting rats with crushed bedbugs caught on or in the vicinity of hospitalized plague patients in Bombay.70 Of three rats thus inoculated in the course of experiments conducted in the first half of 1897, only “one was found to have died of plague.”71 Hankin was also interested in the infectivity of ants, reflecting wider colonial concerns with the insects in India at the time.72 In a “Note on the Relation of Insects and Rats to the Spread of Plague,” published in the same issue of Centralblatt für Bakteriologie und Parasitenkunde as Nuttall’s article, Hankin concluded that after “a long series of researches,” he found that ants “neither die of the disease [n]or retain the infection for any time.”73 However, Hankin reasoned that by eating rats that had died of plague, and “by thus distributing and carrying about infected material they may increase the risk of infection from dead rats,” thus implicating the alimentary tract in insect-borne interspecies plague transmission.74 These experiments were not mentioned in his letters to Simond, though their publication in the same issue as Nuttall’s 1897 article may safely lead us to assume that Simond had read them.

In his correspondence with Simond over May 1898, Hankin dwelled at length on rat experiments aimed at ascertaining the attenuation of plague in rats. The design followed by Hankin in the experiment mentioned in his letter to Simond dated May 31, 1898, is significant, for it greatly resembled that used by Simond in the aforementioned Experiment IV and for his famous, June 2 rat/flea experiment. It involved placing four or five healthy rats in a cage alongside the cadaver of a rat that had been artificially infected with plague: “Gradually after, many delays (some weeks) these rats died, without any sign of plague. I replaced the rats with others and I finally obtained one dead rat with very few microbes having the visual character of plague in its organs. I did not succeed in cultivating this. It is possible that if I had continued to place rats in this cage I would have had a place infected with virulent plague. But this is an isolated experiment. It is possible that the fleas have played a part. Can you not repeat it?”75

Given that the letter is dated May 31 and was sent to Simond (who was at the time in Karachi) from Agra, it is possible, but by no means certain, that it reached him before June 2, when Simond’s famous experiment is said to have taken place. And yet, as Duarte da Silva has pointed out, the letter shows that the question of flea transmission was common among plague researchers at the time, who also shared experimental notes and designs in an effort to elucidate the question.76

If flea-borne plague transmission played a role in Simond’s epidemiological reasoning in the course of the India Mission, up until the summer of 1898 it certainly did not form an “a priori” rationale. Rather, fleas were simply part of the expansive array of hosts and vectors that, in their contingent and relational entanglements, instituted the conditions necessary for the propagation of plague. What appears to have led Simond to experiment with fleas is the combination of three factors: his failure to demonstrate alimentary infection, having recently read Ogata’s article, and possibly having received Hankin’s encouragement to that direction. Simond’s 1898 article suggests that his clinical observations of a blister in human victims made him suspect insect-borne infection, with fleas being among the possible candidates. As there are no notes in Simond’s archives showing the in-progress collection of data on this clinical datum, it is not possible to ascertain if the blister theory was developed before the flea experiments, acting as an incentive, or as a result of them, as a retrospective clinical support to what Simond knew were rather doubtful experimental results.

### Simond’s Flea Experiments

I will now examine the famous experiment of June 2, 1898.77 Whereas Simond’s carnet draft and his 1898 article contain identical descriptions of all other experiments, the description of the experiment purportedly demonstrating plague’s transmission between rats by means of fleas bears striking differences between the two sources. In particular, Simond’s crossed-out words in the manuscript contain valuable insight into his thinking:

The most important experiment is that on the transmission of plague by means of living fleas from a plague-affected rat. I only had the opportunity to execute this experiment once. A sick rat that was captured in the town of Mandvi where the plague reigned is and looked sick died in the cage where I had placed it. The examination of the rat cadaver [unreadable] after at the time of death demonstrated to me allowed me to observe the presence of a few fleas on it. The cadaver was placed at the bottom of a large jar, covered with a sheet of paper placed and above that on the sheet I placed a small cage made of a wooden box of which a single side was lined with an iron-wire mesh and containing three young rats aged one and a half months. After 24h the canvas which covered the jar was lifted, I noticed at this moment that several fleas that were on the internal wall of the jar had escaped and the rat’s cadaver was removed. The small cage containing the three young rats remained another 48 hours in the jar, after which it was removed. One of the young rats died of plague 5 days later. This experiment seems convincing to us. However, as it was impossible for us to find the fleas that probably escaped from the jar during the manipulations, it is desirable that the opportunity missed here be repeated.78

Whereas the published experiment described Simond placing a living rat, suspected of being naturally infected with plague, inside the experimental jar, in the experiment contained in his carnet it is clear that the rat died inside the cage before being placed in the jar. No cat providing additional fleas to those found in the original rat is mentioned in the carnet. The carnet by contrast mentions that the rat’s cadaver was first covered in a sheet of paper, something not mentioned in the article. More importantly, what was then placed in the jar, on top of the paper-covered dead rat, was not “a small iron cage” but a “wooden box” whose one side was “lined with an iron-wire mesh.” There is no indication that this was suspended inside the jar; instead, it is most likely (the crossed-out notes make this ambiguous) that it was placed directly on top of the paper sheet covering the dead rat. Moreover, the box contained not one but three rats. Then the experiment faced quite a predicable problem: To remove the dead rat’s cadaver (twenty-four and not thirty-six hours later, as the article claims), Simond lifted the canvas covering the experimental apparatus and the fleas escaped. The experiment was, in other words, botched. Nonetheless, Simond did not interrupt the experiment but left the three rats inside the box contained in the jar for another forty-eight hours. Of the three rats, only one died, on the fifth day.

It is extremely unlikely that these notes do not correspond to the experiment described in the 1898 article, as they are positioned in Simond’s notebook between other notes on the question of the fleas and clinical observations of the flea-inflicted blister, which are copied verbatim or with slight stylistic revisions in the article. Moreover, neither the experiment as described in the article nor any other similar experiment or attempt to replicate it are recorded in the carnet or in a second notebook, titled “P H Experiments,” where Simond noted seventy-four further experiments conducted between May 25 and July 25, 1898.79

There are two possibilities. First, that the experiment in Simond’s 1898 article did take place as described but was not written down, or the notes were taken in a carnet other than the two existing ones, which was subsequently destroyed or lost. Second, that Simond purposefully misreported what was in fact a botched and inconclusive experiment as an “ideal” one—one that unambiguously proved the transmission of plague between rats by means of fleas. We should here pay attention to the short paragraph following the description of the experiment in Simond’s 1898 article: “The experiment repeated under the same conditions gave us success in a mouse that died of plague in three days, and two failures in adult rats. The dexterity with which these, while we observed them, defended themselves against the attack of the fleas and destroyed them by eating them, make us think that this is a frequent reason for the failure of the experiment.”80

In the 1898 article, Simond furthermore mentioned that in another experiment a rat that died of plague had its fleas removed and was then placed in a jar with seven healthy rats, none of which became infected.81 These additional experiments were not recorded in Simond’s carnet or in his “P H Experiments” notebook. In his letter to Roux dated August 31, 1898, Simond by contrast claimed to have had success in an experiment with one rat and in another with a mouse. These contradicting claims may indicate a way for Simond to be creative with facts: By extracting from his published description of the June 2 experiment the two rats that did not contract plague and by then claiming that these were part of some other attempt to replicate the supposedly successful experiment, Simond might have hoped to conceal the fact that the experiment was inconclusive and, at the same time, balance the failure to produce plague in these two cases by inventing yet two more replications of the experiment, this time successful ones. In this way, the eureka-styled experiment could be rhetorically salvaged and future failures to replicate it could be preempted as coincidental or due to rodents’ supposed habit of eating the fleas infesting them, something that if true would have certainly been noted in his carnet.

In a crossed-out paragraph in Simond’s carnet, he noted: “We do not claim that the experiments we have had already made provide indisputable proof of this mode of transmission. But this is strikingly corroborating, as is the clinical observation.”82 In another passage in the carnet, also not included in the article, Simond further expressed his hesitation, by mounting an apagogical defense: “While none of the present clinical or experimental observations are material evidence of parasite transmission, all of them appear to leave little room for any other modes.”83 Simond’s uncertainty about his findings is most evident in his letter to Roux on August 31, 1898, where the Archimedean feeling of euphoria expressed in Simond’s publications is singularly absent. Simond wrote that he was not “able to carry out absolutely irrefutable experiments so far.” He added, “I am very anxious to hear your feelings about this. Of course you will do with this study of the propagation what you like, I wrote it to submit it to you as a last resort, whatever your judgment, and you may publish it or not, I will not feel any stupid sting of self-esteem.”84

That Simond did not believe the experiments transcribed in his article were conclusive is evident from the fact that, after June 2, he conducted sixty-six more experiments on plague transmission. These, together with eight additional experiments conducted between May 25 and June 2, are contained in the “P H Experiments” notebook.85 All seventy-four experiments are carefully noted and dated, yet none is discussed in his 1898 article. The majority of these experiments involved the inoculation of mice, rats, pigeons, and monkeys with plague cultures or the triturate of organs of infected animals. The notebook also contains twelve experiments with fleas, all conducted after June 2, of which seven involved the injection of crushed fleas into healthy rats or mice and five involved live fleas.

On the one hand, Simond conducted five stand-alone experiments with fleas, mice, and rats between June 30 and July 7, 1898. The first experiment (June 30) was an immediate failure, echoing the trouble with the famous June 2 experiment: Simond placed a young rat in a jar with five fleas extracted from another rat that had died of plague, but before the experiment could proceed, the fleas managed to escape.86 The same day, rather than trying to repeat the experiment, Simond injected a white mouse with a flea taken from a dead rat, diluted in water. Although the animal died on the night between July 9 and 10, an examination of its cadaver revealed no plague bacilli.87 Simond conducted two more stand-alone experiments with fleas, on July 1 and July 7, involving the injection of the debris of a flea extracted from a plague-infected rat into a rat and a mouse, without however recording the results.88 Finally, on July 7 he injected a white mouse with a trituration of two fleas taken from a plague-infected rat. The mouse died soon after, on the night between July 7 and 8, but the postmortem proved inconclusive with the animal’s spleen showing many bacilli, “where some resembled being B.P”—a statement to be contrasted with the usual note, “stuffed with B.P” or “B.P in abundance,” marking cases where Simond actually identified the plague bacillus in experiments contained in the notebook.89 These experiments thus failed to show any connection between fleas and the transmission of plague between rats, even by direct injection, which may be a further reason why Simond was privately not at all certain that his June 2 experiment had conclusively proven the efficacy of flea-borne transmission.

At the same time, Simond conducted composite experiments involving a range of transmission pathways. Given that the notebook never attempted to present a systematic picture of these experimental chains (experiments are written down in chronological order), it is more likely that experiments progressed in an unplanned, on-the-go, case-by-base manner. It is not possible to present these experimental chains here in detail. It is, however, indicative that one such chain involved seven mice and eight rats, which Simond proceeded to cross-infect between July 14 and August 1, 1898, employing multiple infection pathways: plague culture inoculation, inoculation with triturations of infected organs, triturations of fleas, and confinement with rodent cadavers and with fleas. While the experiments led to no conclusive results, this veritable web of attempted infection indicates that, through the summer of 1898, Simond, on the one hand, retained his interest in non-flea-related plague transmission, while, on the other hand, maintained a focus on the rat as an epidemiological dividual: an animal whose role in the propagation of plague could only be understood in terms of its relations with other potential hosts, transmission pathways, and mediums of the disease.

### Conclusion

In this chapter I have examined the multiple ways that Paul-Louis Simond approached the rat’s involvement in the propagation of plague in the context of his role with the Institut Pasteur’s India Mission (1897–1898). Rather than this being limited to the rat flea, or to a parasitological mechanism, as presented in his publications and accepted by historians, my examination of Simond’s archives shows that the Pasteurian doctor developed complex theories and frameworks about the propagation of plague, largely premised upon the rat’s ontology as an epidemiological dividual. In the first eight months of his mission, Simond focused on rats’ relations to other potential propagators of plague, as well as on the role of rat cannibalism in the geographic spread and self-limitation of plague. Upon his return from Saigon in March 1898, Simond began experimenting with different aspects of such potentially pestigenic relations. By August 1, 1898, he had conducted at least eighty-six experiments on different transmission pathways, some involving extremely complex, multipathway chains of infection. Of these, he eventually chose to publicly lay his trust in a single experiment involving live-flea transmission between rats. Simond publicly dismissed all other transmission pathways, although several of his experiments showed these to be at least as viable as flea-borne infection. The reason for this choice is not known, as no direct evidence survives in Simond’s archives.

What the archives do tell us is that Simond’s famous experiment with fleas was botched and inconclusive, as its design was faulty, allowing the fleas to escape, and as two out of three rats did not contract plague. My examination of these long-neglected archives reveals that Simond was aware of these severe limitations of the experiment and that he continued to conduct experiments after the supposed discovery of June 2, 1898. These formed the largest number of experiments on the transmission of plague during mission (sixty-six out of eighty-six), and they demonstrate that Simond remained interested in other transmission pathways. However, Simond opted not to publish these experiments in his 1898 article. He moreover did not repeat the experiment of June 2. Instead, under considerable pressure to produce a breakthrough, in light of his failure demonstrate the efficacy of Yersin’s plague serum actual objective of the India Mission), he eventually misreported the June 2 experiment in the Annales l’Institut Pasteur so as to proclaim the discovery of the borne pathway of plague.

More than simply marking a troubling contribution to historiography of Pasteurian science, the findings of chapter demonstrate that Simond’s experiment operated within a framework of epidemiological dividualism, which, however, Simond chose to publicly refute in favor of a single transmission pathway of plague involving rats and fleas that his experiments had failed to demonstrate. Combining an experimental sleight-of-hand with a radical shift in epidemiological reasoning allowed Simond a place in medical science’s pantheon. It also ushered in a vibrant debate over the validity of his theory, which played a role in future plague studies, and in reconfiguring relation between plague and rats in particular, over following decade.

## Replicating Simond

When, in 1901, the Indian Plague Commission finally published its concluding report, it became evident that Simond’s rat flea theory had failed to have an immediate, universal eureka effect similar to the discovery of the plague bacterium by Kitasato or Yersin. Casting doubt on the experimental design used in the official version of the experiment, the commission’s “conclusion regarding evidence adduced by Dr. Simond” was as brief as it was categorical: “It will become manifest that the process of induction by which Dr. Simond endeavours to establish the proposition that suctorial insects play an important part in the transference of plague from sick to healthy animals is so weak as to be hardly deserving of consideration.”1 In this chapter, I will examine the critical reception of and response to Simond’s rat flea theory by the international scientific community between 1898 and 1905, a period when the pandemic reached truly global proportions and during which the rat came be rapidly adopted as the sine qua non of plague.

Contrary to histories focused on scientific discovery, it was not Simond’s experiment or any other study on rats and their fleas that led to this universal transformation of rats into plague’s protagonists. As I will examine in the next chapter, this was undertaken in the absence of scientific evidence or consensus. Instead, what catalyzed this transformation was international pressure for a framework of the pandemic within which its international spread could be limited without global trade collapsing under the bane of draconian quarantine measures.

In this chapter, I focus on scientific debates regarding Simond’s experiment, and on efforts to test and replicate it in different laboratories across the world, a process that aptly reflects the fact that experiments “are rarely singular events; as a rule, they form series in the course of which epistemic things take shape.”2 However, my aim is not to anchor an iteration of “how plague got rats” on a straightforward process of scientific discovery. On the contrary, I examine how the relation between rats, fleas, and plague became an elusive and troublesome “epistemic target,” which gave rise to an experimental system aimed at elucidating it.3 What resulted were almost-succeeding and almost-failing experiments, which reshaped epidemiological reasoning about plague as a zoonotic disease. What this chapter contributes to the history of epidemics and epidemiology is not so much a reflection on long-established debates on replication and replicability, as a probe into the transformations of “how plague got rats” as a result of introducing a new agent, the flea, in this relation by way of a series of experiments.

As Lisa Onaga and Luísa Reis-Castro have argued, insects, such as fleas, are more often than not included in scientific studies as both “tools and target” of investigation—a productively “ambivalent” position that necessitates new histories of fleas as “epistemic agents.”4 In the case of plague, critiques of Simond’s experiment and studies aiming to restore the rat/flea hypothesis to scientific credibility led to the latter’s radical transformation. This flea-induced scientific dialogue tells a new story about the development of zoonotic framings of plague and the history of epidemiology more broadly. This is a story where the bacteriological revolution is seen in its historical-ethnographic reality, as a messy, inconclusive yet not inept process, whose impact on epidemiological reasoning is very different from the teleological image of a hallowed quest for discovering and conquering microbes. Instead of a discovery, revelation, or conquest, what early twentieth-century bacteriologically informed experiments on plague transmission brought into effect was an unstable, deeply unsatisfactory, and yet dynamic and impactful repositioning of the agency of fleas in the aporetic relation between plague and rats.

### Debunking and Defending Simond

The fact that Simond’s rat flea theory did not manage to galvanize scientific opinion in the years immediately following its publication is evident not only in the final report of the Indian Plague Commission but also in the record of scientific debates about the role of nonhuman animals in plague transmission in the half decade following the publication of his 1898 article. One of the most systematic of these was an article authored by Frank Clemow discussing “plague in lower animals.” Clemow, a leading plague expert, dedicated several pages to rats, which he considered to be “an important, but not the sole, means of spreading the disease.”5 Whereas he discussed soil, grain, rags, and cannibalism all in some detail, infection by means of insects merited only four lines, with just a single mention of fleas across the seven pages of the article. Handling rats, for example during rat-catching operations, continued to be seen as the most common, direct source of human infection from the animals.6

A key objection to the relevance of fleas to plague outbreaks related to a fact observed across the globe-trotting pandemic: No infection had been observed in plague wards and hospitals where fleas were said to be found in great number.7 Additionally, officers performing house-to-house visitations to thousands of “infected houses” across the globe, and presumably coming into contact with fleas, seldom manifested infection.8 In his discussion on plague in Calcutta for the year 1903–1904, H. M. Crake wrote, in line with colonial framings: “As regards fleas, I can only say that whilst lice and bugs are very common, fleas rarely appear to infest the native. At any rate, after spending hours in the bustees, visiting living cases, or superintending disinfection, I do not think I have ever found a flea on my own person.”9

In cases where Simond’s rat flea theory was mentioned, up until 1900, it was rarely taken by doctors or public health officers to be anything more than one among many, not necessary competing or mutually exclusive, options or modes of transmission.10 In the words of a later commentator, “The reception of Simond’s views in the medical world was, on the whole, a very cold one.”11 This was not, however, due to an antagonism by British doctors or officials, as historians have speculated.12 It was instead a reception principally initiated and maintained by continental scientists, leading authorities in their field at the time.

By 1903, a number of authoritative studies had challenged Simond’s theory, showing it to be based on flawed epidemiological reasoning. In works subsequently defending the rat/flea hypothesis, these objections were criticized as based on “negative facts.”13 In a much-translated and circulated response to works critical of Simond, the French colonial doctor F. Noc summarized these as based on two objections: “1st, that Simond’s experiments have not been confirmed by any experimenter; 2nd, that these experiences have taken into account neither the species of Fleas that parasitize on rats, nor the aptitude of these species to attack Man, nor even of their affinities for the species which parasitize Man.”14 In reality, the epistemic landscape of objections to and critique of Simond’s theory was far more complex and nuanced.

The first problem identified by scientists opposed to Simond’s theory was his neglect in identifying the species of fleas with which he experimented. In his influential monograph on plague, Abraham Netter was the first to raise this taxonomic objection, stressing that parasitic insects “specialize in one animal species and that humans rarely take up the parasites of animals.”15 He recognized, moreover, that while ill laboratory mice in particular were often found covered in fleas, the latter had never been observed to bite humans working with them.16 A year later, in his magnum opus on the role of insects in the spread of diseases, one of the world’s leading parasitologists, George Nuttall, accused Simond of being more competent in providing opinions than in producing evidence.17 Nuttall noted that Simond ignored a fundamental fact of parasitology: Fleas that live and feed on rats are different species than those biting and living on humans. Whereas Nuttall’s reproach of Simond on the subject of flea identification was relatively subtle, that same year (1900), a relentless attack was mounted by one of the world’s leading experts on zoonotic diseases at the time, Bruno Galli-Valerio, who ridiculed Simond over a series of public talks and scientific papers.

Being the author of the first scientific monograph on zoonoses and a professor of bacteriology, hygiene, and parasitology at the University of Lausanne, Galli-Valerio was not averse to the idea that rats carried or spread plague.18 What he found truly objectionable, however, was Simond’s lack of esprit scientifique: “To demonstrate to you how unscientifically [avec combien peu d’esprit scientifique] he proceeded, I will tell you that he was unable to recognize what species of flea he had been dealing with, and he made a deplorable confusion between rat fleas, cat fleas, and human ones.”19 Galli-Valerio expressed his indignation at Simond’s admission of ignorance, as stated in his 1898 article: “Truly this affirmation is a bit curious coming from a person who knows how to use a microscope to detect plague bacilli!”20

Rather than being merely a “negative fact,” Simond’s confessed ignorance raised a key epistemological question as regards his experimental system: How could this produce reliable evidence when it failed to specify its exact epistemic object? How could Simond claim to have produced proof about plague’s transmission pathway when he did not even know what it was exactly that had supposedly transmitted the disease in his experiment? Intimately entangled with a dramaturgy of agonistic self-staging, Galli-Valerio’s attack against Simond pointed beyond the 1898 experiment, showcasing an embarrassing ontological limitation in an alarming number of plague studies: the studies’ failure to identify the species of rats or fleas experimented with or observed in the field.

Galli-Valerio could not have known that Simond’s published experiment was a perjury, which reflected neither the design nor the results of the actual experiment undertaken on June 2, 1898. Nor could he have known that, in reality, Simond had been unable to test whether the fleas actually carried plague, as they had escaped his experimental apparatus before he could test them (see chapter 3). Nonetheless, he rightly identified Simond’s stated experimental method as flawed. “Simond,” he argued, “seems to claim that the human flea is the one that haunts the body of all domestic animals; and, I do not know why, wanting to demonstrate that the flea in rats and mice is the agent of plague transmission, he uses the cat flea in some experiments, which has nothing to do with the fleas of rodents nor with those of humans.”21 Galli-Valerio’s critique of Simond’s experiment had far-reaching consequences, with Patrick Manson, who back in 1897 had advocated a “Jehad [sic] against rats” in Bombay, commenting that it “shows on what a slender basis of facts a catching theory may be founded, and how it may gain popularity and even acceptance.”22

This then brings us to the second problem that scientists identified in Simond’s theory: the lack of proof that fleas that live on and/or bite rats could also live on and/or bite humans. Whereas the first critique of Simond’s theory was in its essence ontological, insofar as it concerned a lack of information on the part of Simond of what species of fleas were used in his experiments, the second critique was properly speaking etiological. It concerned the question of whether rat fleas (i.e., fleas whose proper host organisms were rats) could transmit plague to humans, or whether they could only transmit the disease among rats. We need to recall here that Simond performed no experiment on ratto-human transmission. His conclusion that fleas transmitted plague to humans relied strictly and solely on the observation of blisters on the skin of human plague patients—supposedly the result of fleabites, though in and of themselves not forming the subject of specific pathological study.23

Eager to prove that rodent-borne fleas did not bite humans, Galli-Valerio proceeded by way of an old modus operandi of plague studies: self-experimentation.24 He placed “mouse fleas” (Typhlopsylla musculi) that had been starved for twenty-four to forty-eight hours on his own skin, leaving some to roam free and putting some under a bell jar.25 No fleabites occurred in either case, and the fleas seemed eager to leave his skin as soon as possible. Galli-Valerio concluded that the transmission of plague from rodents to humans via rodent-borne fleas was “far from being demonstrated.”26 At the same time, his epistemic wrath was turned against another Pasteurian luminary, who had by that point come to Simond’s rescue: Adrien Loir, Pasteur’s nephew and at the time director of the Institut Pasteur in Tunis, who in future decades became famous for his love of cats and his public-oriented attempts to promote them as rat-killers.27 Based on the assumed fact that rats transmitted plague to humans via their fleas, Loir sought to explain why workers in olive oil presses in Tunis (the capital of the French protectorate of Tunisia) appeared to be immune to plague, an idea popularized in early nineteenth-century medical literature.28 Loir placed a cage containing a rat inside an “Arab house” where fleas were known to be abundant and to have attacked humans.29 By the end of twenty-four hours, fleas were observed on the rat. Two smaller cages were then placed inside the original cage, replicating to some extent Simond’s experiment. Both contained rats, but in one of them the animal was covered in olive oil. The original rat was then killed, and its cadaver left in the larger cage at equal distance from the two smaller cages. The following day, fleas could no longer be found on the dead rat, nor could any fleas be located on the oil-covered rat. By contrast, the third rat was covered in fleas. Loir concluded that fleas were averse to olive oil, something that, in his opinion, explained the long-standing notion that olive-oil workers were not affected during plague outbreaks. Galli-Valerio once again pointed at what now appeared to be a systematic neglect on the part of Pasteurians regarding the species of fleas involved, and an a priori, unfounded assumption that fleas that bite rats also bite humans. His acerbic pen on fire, he wrote: Using the flea theory in order to attribute an exclusive role to rats as regards the transmission of plague to humans was seen not only as unfounded on scientific evidence but also as epistemologically perilous, for it risked blinding researchers to other transmission pathways, including human-to-human contagion, or the agency of flies, bedbugs, and ants.31

As far as I know, Arabs are neither rats nor mice and as a consequence they must be infected by P. irritans and not by P. fasciatus nor by T. musculi…. I will repeat: Mr. Loir, like Mr. Simond, must first of all establish with which species of fleas they were dealing, and demonstrate that these fleas were capable of biting humans. As this demonstration was not made, his work has no value for the solution of the question of the transmission of bubonic plague from rats to humans with the intermediary of fleas.30

Soon, another Italian bacteriologist, Carlo Tiraboschi, added his critique through publications in Italian, German, and French, the most extensive of which appeared in 1904 as a 188-page, richly illustrated article in the Archives de parasitologie.32 Tiraboschi was not a well-known bacteriologist like Galli-Valerio, but his anti-Simond publications would catapult him, an assistant microbiologist at the microbiological laboratory of a provincial Italian Service of Public Health, to international, albeit also short-lived, scientific fame. Tiraboschi was working for the laboratory of the Italian Service of Public Health in Naples when a small epidemic of plague struck the city.33 The outbreak moved the director of the service, Rocco Santoliquido, to order an investigation into the species of rats present in Italy and their distribution, an operation that brought together the microbiological laboratory of the service with zoological museums, local doctors, and sanitary officers across the country.34 Tiraboschi’s contribution focused on both rats and their fleas, from a zoological point of view and in terms of the role they played in the propagation of plague.35 While Tiraboschi found Simond’s “positive” results to be, in principle, of greater evidentiary value than the “negative” results produced by Galli-Valerio, his own concrete experience cast serious doubt on the flea-borne transmission pathway.36

Like Nuttall and Galli-Valerio before him, Tiraboschi lamented the neglect on the part of defenders of the rat/flea hypothesis in identifying susceptible flea and rat species. Examining rat and mice fleas in Italy, he identified two principal species: Ceratophyllus fasciatus and Ctenopsylla musculi. On the basis of experiments on human subjects, including on himself, he categorically stated that these “never bite Man.”37 While he admitted that Ceratophyllus fasciatus closely resembled other flea species that may bite humans, and could thus confuse researchers, Tiraboschi was categorical regarding Ctenopsylla musculi: “Experiments were so extraordinarily numerous and executed under such a great number of individuals and under such favorable conditions … that I cannot admit the possibility of this species biting Man, even under exceptional circumstances.”38

The final question that international scientists raised about Simond’s theory related to whether the bite of a flea that had been feeding on a plague-infected rat could in fact transmit plague to a hitherto healthy rat.39 In his 1900 bacteriological report on Sydney’s first plague outbreak, Frank Tidswell discussed Simond’s blister theory, noting that his efforts to identify this pathology in plague patients had produced mixed results.40 He also examined fleas from healthy humans, a plague patient, and from mice, rats, and cats, with only rat-borne fleas testing positive for plague.41 Tidswell proceeded to inject a trituration of these fleas into a guinea pig, which thereafter died of plague. He also tried to replicate Simond’s experiment by placing a healthy rat in a small wire cage inside a larger sheet-iron cage containing another small wire cage where he had placed a plague-inoculated
