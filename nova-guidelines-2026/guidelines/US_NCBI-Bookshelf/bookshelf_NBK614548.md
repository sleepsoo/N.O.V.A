# Management of Anemia and/or Bleeding in Patients Who Will Not Accept Blood Products

### Abstract

Objective is to provide recommendations regarding patients that will not accept blood transfusions and have symptomatic or severe anemia (with or without bleeding). To provide guidance for providers in managing a relatively infrequent patient population.

Patient Population: Adult hospitalized patients who decline blood products e.g., Jehovah’s Witnesses (JW) and who are expected to lose significant blood volume as a result of a procedure, medical condition, or are already significantly anemic.

Objectives: To provide recommendations regarding patients that will not accept blood transfusions and have symptomatic or severe anemia (with or without bleeding). To provide guidance for providers in managing a relatively infrequent patient population.

Introduction

The transfusion of blood products has become commonplace in modern medical and surgical practice and is the standard of care in many situations. However, it is not uncommon for patients to decline a recommended therapy, including the transfusion of blood products. This guideline hopes to aid clinicians in their discussions with patients who decline the use of blood products and provide information on potential treatment alternatives.

Consent

Patients have the right to participate in decisions about their medical care. For adult patients with decision-making capacity, this includes the right to decline treatment. The diverse cultural, religious, and personal preferences of patients and families should be acknowledged, and clinicians should make reasonable efforts to respect those preferences while maintaining the quality of health care provided.

Adult Patients

A patient with decision-making capacity has the right to decline medical treatment if they can understand the nature of the planned treatment as well as consequences of declining it. For specifics, see Michigan Medicine (MM) Informed Consent for Provision of Health Care Services, 62-10-000 policy. If a patient is assessed and determined to have decision-making capacity, their right to decline treatment with blood products should be honored. The declination should be documented in the medical record. A form for declination of blood and treatment can be found in Appendix A.

Patients without decision-making capacity cannot provide informed consent and cannot decline treatment. In such cases, their legally authorized representative should be consulted to guide treatment decisions.

Patient-Prisoners

A patient-prisoner is a person in the legal custody of a law enforcement or correctional agency brought to MM for treatment and/or admission. Just as any other patient, an adult Patient-Prisoner is presumed to have capacity, and may consent or decline treatment, with some very limited exceptions.

If a patient-prisoner with decision-making capacity declines treatment, it should be documented in the patient’s medical record and communicated to the appropriate law enforcement or correctional agency official. The denial should be honored, but also must be weighed against the law enforcement or correctional agency’s legitimate interests.

Staff should contact the Office of the General Counsel at (734) 764-2178 for assistance with this type of case. The law enforcement or correctional agency will usually work with the patient’s legally authorized representative to help determine the treatment options.

Contributory Factors

There may be many factors for a patient’s declination of treatment with blood products, ranging from the perceived risks of infection, previous transfusion reactions, or personal/religious preference. It is important to identify the reason behind a patient’s declination of treatment with blood products. This allows the provider an opportunity to address any misconceptions and discuss potential consequences and complications, so that an informed decision can be made. It is important to clarify these preferences early, as blood transfusion may become urgently necessary. Consider discussing patient preferences related to blood transfusion on admission (i.e., when discussing code status).

Transfusion reaction risk (or previous occurrence)

Potential adverse reactions to blood transfusion are listed on the blood bank’s website at https://www.pathology.med.umich.edu/blood-bank/adverse-reactions-to-transfusion, and summarized in Table 1. An individualized provider-patient discussion of perceived vs. likely risk is recommended. For patients with previous adverse transfusion reactions, it is best to discuss the specifics of their previous reaction and identify mitigating interventions that may prevent further recurrence. The Blood Bank at Michigan Medicine (734-936-6888) may assist to facilitate and inform the discussion.

Religious or personal preference

The most prominent religious group who declines blood products is the Jehovah’s Witnesses (JW).

JW patients commonly decline whole blood and its components (red blood cells, white blood cells, plasma and platelets), as well as autologous blood transfusion and intraoperative cell salvage if it is separated from the patient’s body. Views on the fractions of blood components (such as albumin, immunoglobulins, etc.) are evolving and should be clarified individually. - Link https://www.jw.org/en/medical-library/medical-information/religious-and-ethical-position-medical-therapy/ shows a high-level overview of the JW perspective on transfusion of blood products.

Each case should be discussed and treated individually, independently of the church’s official policy and with medical confidentiality. An in-depth discussion of blood perceptions and preferences with individual patients is necessary to ensure truly autonomous decisions on blood-based treatment.

Ethical Considerations

The fundamental principles of biomedical ethics include: autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice. Underlying these concepts are issues of informed consent, patient’s right to know, veracity, self-determination, fidelity and honesty. The American Medical Association defines informed consent as legal documentation obtained after a thorough communication between provider and patient, in which the patient agrees to a medical intervention based on knowledge of the diagnosis, risks, and benefits of the intervention, and the patient’s ability to understand the treatment and alternatives.

For all patients, the ethical responsibility of the medical care team is to provide clear information so that competent adults have the right to determine what treatment they will or will not agree to, and this includes the administration of blood and blood products.

Patient blood management (PBM) includes a multidisciplinary patient-centered approach to appropriate use of blood component therapy focused on improving patient outcomes based on the patient’s desired goals of care. Clinical decisions regarding transfusion of blood and blood products should be based on the best available evidence, clinical expertise, and patient values.

Minors or Patients Who Lack Capacity

The process of informed consent is complicated when minors are involved because they lack the capacity and competency to consent. In the United States, a child is legally granted the right to make their own decisions at age 18. However, before that age, parents or surrogates act as proxies. The exception is emancipated minors who are children, usually in their teenage years, who are legally granted decision-making capacity and can express understanding of risks, benefits, and alternatives of the proposed medical intervention. The definition of emancipated minor varies from state to state; therefore, providers should consult their institution’s legal counsel. In some cases, the parent or guardian of a minor may decline to consent for necessary treatment. If the parent or guardian of a minor declines consent for necessary treatment on religious grounds (e.g., Jehovah’s Witness parent declining blood transfusion for their minor child), MM may seek a court order authorizing the provision of treatment. Contact the Office of the General Counsel at (734) 764-2178 for assistance with this type of case. In an emergent situation, life-saving treatment may be provided even without consent. A court order is not needed to transfuse a child with potentially life-saving blood products. The principle is that parental authority is not ‘absolute’ and may need to be regulated for the best interest of a child’s wellbeing. This special consideration does not apply to the fetus of Jehovah’s Witness parents. Expanded access for HBOC-201 is not approved for minors.

### Initial Assessment and Approach

#### Identifying patient beliefs on transfusion of blood products

A systematic and uniform query of patient belief on transfusion of blood products should be performed early in hospital admission (i.e., preemptive to an urgent need for transfusion, when able). Maintaining privacy regarding these personal and religious beliefs can also help to mitigate any potential social consequences that may result.

It is important for patients to understand the many different types of blood products. Some devout Jehovah’s Witnesses generally decline whole blood and the primary components (RBC, platelets, WBC, and plasma), but acceptance of specific blood components is left to the individual’s religious interpretation. Individual preferences need to be clarified and documented in patients who refuse blood products. Clinicians should explain indications for each component, as well as the risk of declining, downstream potential effects, and potential alternatives. A resource to assist in this discussion is provided in Appendix B. Additionally, the Spiritual Care consult service can help to address questions or concerns and provide additional resources as needed.

Documentation of Preference for Blood Product Use. Patients’ acceptance/denial and type of acceptable blood products should be documented in the medical record. The checklist should be imaged into EPIC, an FYI flag should be created, and a comment under the patient’s allergies should be entered. Complete the Refusal of Blood or Treatment form (Appendix A) as appropriate.

#### Anemia evaluation

Anemia is defined as hemoglobin less than 13 g/dL for a man and less than 12 g/dL for a woman. Anemia occurs in the majority of hospitalized patients and can be exacerbated by frequent phlebotomy that many hospitalized patients require. A Complete Blood Count (CBC) will help determine the severity of the anemia as well as determine underlying cause. An overall approach to determining the etiology based upon the size of the RBCs (based on MCV) is presented in Figure 1. Interpretation of the reticulocyte index along with the MCV can also aid in determining the RBC proliferative capacity thereby narrowing potential underlying etiologies of the anemia, so this could be considered for some patients as well. Given that transfusion of blood products is not an acceptable option in this patient population, potential treatment will focus on optimizing endogenous production of RBCs.

#### Treatment Strategies for Anemia in patients who decline blood products

Severity of the anemia will help to direct treatment. Severity assessment (and hence urgency to correct the anemia) involves not only the absolute hemoglobin level but acuity of decrease and associated symptoms. Decreased RBCs can lead to decreased oxygen carrying capacity. If the hemoglobin falls below 8 g/dL, an evaluation for evidence of end organ dysfunction should be initiated. However, proactive avoidance of hemoglobin drops should be made when possible. While routine screening for coagulopathies in this population is not necessarily recommended, it is important to reduce blood loss to the extent possible. Phlebotomy blood losses may contribute to critical anemia and are best minimized by limiting diagnostic testing to only those necessary for immediate clinical decision making., There is a lack of evidence to recommend for or against the use of pediatric size phlebotomy tubes for adult patients., Consider the use of blood conservation devices when possible, such as the use of non-invasive monitoring methods instead of blood gas testing and ensure that bleeding is treated with early intervention.,

Additionally, optimization of endogenous erythropoiesis with empiric treatment with vitamins, iron, and erythropoietin is recommended. A patient is felt to have critical levels of anemia when there is evidence of end organ damage that is a result of the anemia, or if the hemoglobin falls below 5 g/dL. Once critical anemia has been reached in patients who decline blood transfusions, that patient should be managed in an intensive care unit and evaluation for HBOC should be initiated. See Figure 2 for an overview of this process.

#### Maximizing Endogenous Erythropoiesis

Endogenous erythropoiesis should be maximized to the extent possible. Patients with normal bone marrow function will generally respond to stimulation of erythropoiesis with rate of hemoglobin increase of 0.25 g/dL/day, in the absence of bleeding. Patients with bone marrow dysfunction, such as myelodysplastic syndromes, or infiltration by malignant cells may not respond. (See Figure 2.)

Erythropoiesis stimulating agents (ESA) (Figure 2). ESAs stimulate red blood cell formation and are recommended for use in severely anemic patients (Hb<7.0 g/dL).,– There is not a standardized dosing regimen for erythropoietin, but both weight-based regimens (50 – 600 IU/kg/day) and fixed dose regimens (10,000 – 40,000 IU/day) have been successfully employed. The safety of erythropoietin has not been established in patient with sickle cell syndromes or hypercoagulable disorders.

Epoetin alfa contains 2.5 mg human albumin per mL. Darbepoetin alfa does not contain albumin; however, there is not sufficient evidence to make a recommendation regarding the use of darbepoetin alfa in this population. Darbepoetin alfa was reported to be successful in a patient with acute lymphoblastic leukemia, but not in the critical care setting.

Epoetin alfa therapy should be continued until resolution of critical anemia (Hb ≥7.0). Unfortunately, there is not sufficient evidence to determine the expected rate of hemoglobin recovery with high-dose, daily administration. Thromboembolic complications and uncontrolled hypertension have been reported following ESA administration and are contraindications to administration.,

Iron (Figure 2). Iron deficiency is defined as a serum ferritin < 30 ng/mL or a transferrin saturation < 20% with a ferritin < 300 ng/ml in patients whom the ferritin is thought to be elevated due to an inflammatory state (see Figure 1). Iron supplementation should be given concomitantly with erythropoietin in severely anemic patients (Hb < 8.0 g/dL) since optimal response to ESA with increased Hb requires iron supplementation.

There is insufficient evidence to support a standardized dosing regimen or specific formulation of iron. However, successfully reported regimens in this population include IV ferric carboxymaltose 1000 mg once, iron sucrose 200 mg daily for 6 days, iron sucrose 500 mg daily for 6 days, and iron sucrose 100 mg daily for 10 days.– It should be noted that these are higher doses than the usual dose of iron supplementation. IV administration results in better bioavailability versus oral administration. There is a likelihood that not all IV iron preparations will be available on an institution’s formulary. Controversy remains on the safety of using iv iron formulations in patients with active severe infections. While some studies have shown no association with increased risk for infections, scant data is available in patients with known active severe infections. Therefore, caution is advised in using IV iron in patients with active severe infections. Consult with clinical pharmacist for optimal and patient-specific selection of iron product.

Vitamin C. Consider vitamin C supplementation as it may improve erythropoietin response in patients with functional iron deficiency (i.e., hemodialysis patients). There is insufficient evidence to recommend a standardized vitamin C dosing regimen, however, 500 mg three times daily, has been reported. Vitamin C supplementation may reduce methemoglobin levels when administrated in conjunction with HBOC-201.

Folate. Consider supplementation with folate since erythropoiesis may be limited by purine synthesis, which is dependent on folate and vitamin B12 availability. There is insufficient evidence to support a standardized folate dose, however, one reported regimen is 1 mg daily.

Vitamin B12 (Figure 2). Consider supplementation with vitamin B12 since erythropoiesis may be limited by purine synthesis, which is dependent on folate and vitamin B12 availability. There is not sufficient evidence to recommend a single dosing regimen of vitamin B12, however, a dose of 1000 mcg orally once daily may be considered.

Vitamin D (Figure 2). Vitamin D may increase iron bioavailability in anemia of chronic disease by suppressing hepcidin and having positive effects on transferrin saturation and iron status. While a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized clinical trials of vitamin D supplementation in anemia did not demonstrate an increase in hemoglobin, the studies represented a wide variety of dosing regimens and patient types including non-anemic patients. There is some evidence to recommend 100,000 units of D3 daily for 5 days in severely anemic patients to supplement vitamin D.,

#### Oxygen Conservation and Supplementation

Oxygen delivery should be maximized through supplemental nasal cannula when appropriate with the goal to be to maintain a saO2 > 95%. Additionally, oxygen consumption should be minimized to the extent safely possible. Hyperbaric oxygen may be considered; however, it is not currently available for inpatients at Michigan Medicine.–4 Recognize that pulse oximetry is not always a reliable measure of tissue O2 delivery and monitoring other measures of end organ oxygenation should be considered as appropriate (e.g., serum lactate).

Conserving oxygen in this patient population is very important. Methods to decrease intrapulmonary shunt include elevating the head of the bed, bronchodilator therapy, and incentive spirometry. Antipyretics should be used for febrile patients since they reduce O2 consumption by 5-7% for each Celsius degree temperature decreases, but avoid external cooling.,,6 Consider sedation when agitation is present.

Avoid using non-selective beta blockers to control tachycardia since there is a lack of evidence to support efficacy and they also reduce cardiac output, thus reducing delivery of O2. Consider diuretics if a patient is hypervolemic, however, be vigilant to avoid volume contraction, which could further reduce oxygenation.

#### Hemoglobin Based Oxygen Carriers (HBOCs)

Hemoglobin-based oxygen carriers (HBOCs) may provide temporary blood oxygen delivery capacity and may be an acceptable alternative to blood transfusion for some patients who decline blood transfusion. There are currently no FDA approved HBOCs. HBOC-201 (Hemopure®) is an investigational agent manufactured by HbO2 Therapeutics. It is a purified, cross-linked, acellular bovine hemoglobin (Hb) in a modified lactated Ringer’s solution. HBOC-201 is available for treatment use at Michigan Medicine under an Expanded Access Program. HBOC-201 is recommended for patients with critical anemia, in whom transfusion is not an option, and who have adequate bone marrow function to recover from critical anemia with support of erythropoiesis.,

HBOC-201 is investigational and requires enrollment in the Michigan Medicine Expanded Access Program, which is administered by the section of Transfusion Medicine (Blood Bank) in the Department of Pathology. Eligibility criteria for treatment use of HBOC-201 are listed in Table 2. Adverse effects of HBOC-201 are shown in Table 3. Consent must be obtained by a member of the study team. The investigational product can only be ordered by an authorized prescriber, who is a member of the study team. For information about obtaining HBOC-201, contact the Blood Bank, or the Transfusion Medicine attending on-call. HBOC-201 should only be used in conjunction with maximal support of erythropoiesis and only in a care setting where the patient can be closely monitored, such as an ICU.

For patients with critical anemia or a hemoglobin level < 5 mg/dL, an initial dose of 32.5 g (one unit) of HBOC-201 is recommended, to be followed by infusion of additional units as necessary, to achieve and maintain a total hemoglobin concentration > 6 g/dL. Although 6 g/dL is a reasonable guideline for many patients, the appropriate minimal total hemoglobin concentration is patient-specific and determined by clinical judgment. The patient’s volume status should be carefully monitored when using HBOC-201.

One unit of HBOC-201 will typically increase the total hemoglobin by approximately 0.8 g/dL, with a half-life of approximately 16 hours.

The need for additional dose administration should be assessed after each infusion as clinically indicated. Adequate perfusion may be assessed by one (or more) of the following:

1. normalization of the base excess and lactate,
2. stabilization of blood pressure, defined as a supine systolic blood pressure of > 100 mmHg,
3. pulse rate of < 100 beats per minute, and
4. return of urine output to normal levels.

Dosing should be stopped if any one of the following occurs:

- resolution of critical ischemia
- recovery of RBC Hemoglobin (total hemoglobin – serum hemoglobin) to > 6 g/dL
- evidence of circulatory overload manifested by pulmonary edema and/or peripheral edema
- red blood cell transfusion
- clinically significant methemoglobinemia (eg methemoglobin > 20%)

The typical IV administration rate of HBOC-201 is 250 mL/1 hour. The rate should be guided by the patient’s condition and physician judgment. If the patient develops hypertension, the infusion rate should be slowed.

Adverse effects of HBOC-201 are dose and infusion rate dependent, see Table 3 for details.

#### Bleeding Patients Who Decline Blood Products

Bleeding patients who decline blood products require early intervention (Figure 3). Taking into account the original indication, pharmacologic anticoagulation should be stopped and even reversed when appropriate– Institutional guidelines (https://michmed-clinical.policystat.com/policy/10977829/latest/) are available to help direct interventions aimed at reversing anticoagulation. Notably, these interventions may be derived from blood products and treatment plans may have to be tailored based upon the patient’s preferences (Appendices A and B). Procedures (e.g., surgery, endoscopy, angiographic embolization) aimed at controlling identified sources of bleeding should be performed urgently. Early specialty consultation to perform these procedures is essential. Additional local control of bleeding and hemorrhage by direct compression, tourniquet, and topical hemostatic agents, should all be considered early. Examples of topical hemostatic agents include: topical thrombin which will promote local thrombus formation and silver nitrate that is available in either a topical solution or topical stick and will result in chemical cauterization. If local control is not achieved, then use of systemic agents is indicated. If a patient has a bleeding disorder, then specific agents should be given (see Appendix C and D). If no known bleeding disorder is present, then systemic tranexamic acid (TXA) may be considered with different doses for specific indications. Typical dosing of TXA for trauma: 1 gm IV once followed by a continuous infusion of 1 gm over 8 hours; for GI bleed: 1 gm IV once, then 3 gm continuous infusion over 24 hours; and for post partum hemorrhage: 1 gm IV once, and repeat 1 gm if bleeding continues after 30 minutes. Consider compassionate use HBOC (not approved by FDA) when critical anemia develops and in patients with ongoing bleeding who decline allogeneic blood products.

Concurrently patients should be evaluated for underlying coagulopathy while attempting to control bleeding. Should traditional coagulation testing (PT, PTT, INR) not indicate an underlying bleeding disorder and there still is clinical concern for abnormal bleeding that has not been responsive to interventions, then consider viscoelastic testing (ROTEM/TEG). Notably, the ROTEM (rotational thromboelastometry) system uses thromboelastometric analysis to provide a quantitative and qualitative indication of the coagulation state of a blood sample and therefore can potentially help direct the use of blood product fractions or synthetically derived agents. Indications for ordering and/or interpretation of results should be guided by appropriate subspecialty services such as hematology, or possibly trauma surgery, as interpretation of the results require specialized training. Providers should complete the Cornerstone online class Rotem Thromboelastogram Interpretation.

### Surgical Considerations

#### Pre-operative Planning and Coordination

Early recognition of patients who decline blood products and are scheduled for a surgery or procedure is key in their management. This allows for the appropriate optimization pre-operatively as well as adequate planning and coordination for the intra- and post-operative periods.

Optimization in the pre-operative or pre-procedural time should focus on three main categories: identifying and correcting pre-existing anemia, assessing and addressing the bleeding risk for the patient and the procedure, and clarifying, documenting, and arranging any specific treatments and interventions, as appropriate.

At least 6 weeks before the surgery or procedure, the patient’s Hemoglobin (Hb) should be checked. If anemia is identified (Hb < 13 g/dL), the etiology should be determined as directed by Figure 1. 3 For patients with iron deficiency anemia without significant kidney disease, initiate treatment with IV iron sucrose 500mg on day 1 and again on day 7 and recheck the Hb on day 21. If anemia persists, then consider delaying the surgery and consult with hematology for a comprehensive anemia evaluation and management., For patients with anemia of chronic disease, coordination with providers managing the chronic disease processes (e.g.,hematology oncology, nephrology) is essential to determining the best steps to address anemia pre-operatively. Additionally, patients should be instructed to stop all alcohol consumption prior to surgery, as that can promote macrocytic anemia and predispose to coagulopathy

#### Assessing Bleeding Risk

In addition to basic screening for coagulation abnormalities (e.g., PT/INT, PTT, platelet count), clinicians might consider performing a bleeding risk assessment with the Bleeding Assessment Tool. This tool is useful in identifying occult inherited bleeding disorders, which, if present, may warrant a preoperative hematology consultation. Also it is important to evaluate whether the patient is taking any anticoagulant, antiplatelet, or other drugs that could affect clotting or promote bleeding. These medications require a plan for perioperative cessation and/or reversal.

#### Education and Documentation

Patients should be given a clear explanation of the blood products that may be required during or after surgery. Have a thoughtful discussion regarding the risks involved if the patient declines blood products and highlight any available alternative treatments (See Checklist, Appendix B). Clearly document in the medical record which treatments and/or procedures the patient consents to, and which they do not. This includes cell-salvaging techniques.

The majority of JWs will accept intra-operative cell salvage. This should be discussed before surgery and, if agreed upon, initiated at the start of surgery. In addition to cell salvage, determine if the patient will accept compassionate use HBOC (not approved by FDA) if critical anemia or significant bleeding occurs.

#### Intra-operative Protocols

Methods to minimize surgical blood loss should be implemented intra-operatively. Blood-sparing techniques with meticulous surgical hemostasis utilizing topical hemostatics and adjuncts should be the standard. A list of strategies for blood conservation during surgical procedures can be found in Appendix E. A list of hemostatic agents is shown in Appendix C and D. Additionally, consider anesthetic techniques to minimize blood loss with attention to minimize hemodilution with excessive crystalloid use, except when employing acute normovolemic hemodilution (ANH).–50

#### Post-operative monitoring and care

A comprehensive verbal and written hand-over of the patient is essential after surgery. All involved providers should be aware of any adverse intra-operative events and understand and respect the wishes of the patient that were discussed prior to the procedure. Minimize any iatrogenic blood loss through diagnostic laboratory testing and when appropriate, use pediatric testing tubes for labs. Management of any ongoing post-operative anemia should be directed as above.

### Appendix E List of Strategies for Blood Conservation during Surgical Procedures

#### General Techniques to Minimize Iatrogenic Blood Loss

1. Pediatric microsampling tubes
2. Reduction of “serial” laboratory testing
3. Multiple tests per blood draw (batching)

#### Surgical Techniques to Minimize Intra-Operative Blood Loss

1. Electrocautery
2. Argon beam coagulator
3. Gamma knife radiosurgery
4. Microwave coagulation scalpel
5. Bovie cautery
6. Harmonic scalpel
7. Ultrasonic scalpel
8. Cryosurgery
9. Endoscopic approaches
10. Arterial embolization
11. Minimally invasive surgical techniques

#### Anesthetic Techniques to Minimize Intra-Operative Blood Loss

1. Hypotensive anesthesia
2. Deep sedation
3. Induced hypothermia
4. Acute hemodilution
5. Cell saver

#### Agents to Minimize Intra-Operative Blood Loss

1. Topical hemostatics, such as Tisseel, Avitene, Recothrom
2. Topical adjuncts, such as surgical, gelfoam

#### Agents to Maintain Blood Volume

1. Crystalloids (avoid hyperchloremic fluids)
2. Colloids (avoid hydroxyethyl starches which produce dilutional coagulopathy)

#### Agents to Enhance RBC Production and Improve Wound Healing

1. IV Iron
2. Vitamin B12
3. Folate
4. Epoetin alfa (if indicated)
5. Androgenic hormones
6. Vitamin C
