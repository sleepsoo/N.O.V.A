# Segregated Species: Pests, Knowledge, and Boundaries in South Africa, 1910-1948

## Subterranean Swarms

Attempts to categorize, control, and conserve elephants, big game, and wild birds were widely publicized and controversial events in the history of settler science, in which the category of “pest” was contested between different parties. “Veld rodents,” a group of mammals thought to threaten rural settlement across the Union of South Africa, were one of the apex pests of the countryside. For these animals, the status of “pest” was never in question, and there was no controversy over their control: they could be killed without second thought. Like the Addo Elephants or crop-devouring insects, veld rodents transgressed boundaries between wild and domestic spaces, feasting upon crops in the process. As in the case of the Zululand big game, they were indicted by medical scientists as carriers of a devastating disease. Yet unlike all these animals, veld rodents brought into question a fundamental binary: the separation between veld- and town-dwelling animals and microorganisms. Settler scientific debates were not concerned with whether they constituted pests, but what action should be taken against them.

From the 1920s to the 1940s, the mobility of veld rodents created one of the most significant challenges to the Department of Public Health in its early existence: the “veld plague” problem. This disease, an aftershock of the Third Plague Pandemic that ravaged South African port cities on and off between 1900 and 1912, was concentrated in rural areas and claimed just over 1,100 human lives between 1923 and 1939. Yet the difficulties in understanding its epidemiology, combined with the perception that it was encroaching upon cities, led public health officials to believe that it was a disaster in slow-motion. Plague and its rodent reservoirs were both detrimental to the productivity of the Union. Not only did they threaten human life, but infected rats and fleas, public health officials feared, might nest in farm produce and be distributed across food supply networks. As in the case of nagana, in controlling a disease concentrated in rural areas where medical infrastructure was rudimentary, the primary target of public health officials was not Yersinia pestis itself (the microbe that causes plague) but its known hosts: rodents and fleas. Of these two groups of animals, migratory “wild” rodents were identified as the greatest epidemiological risk and took the brunt of the blame for the problem.

Controlling “veld rodents” posed a new challenge for farmers, bureaucrats, and public health officials. Unlike large mammals or birds, rodents were perceived primarily as swarms or populations among scientists and vernacular experts alike. Whereas “rogue” elephants or grain-guzzling “useful” birds could be rationalized as misbehaving specimens of their otherwise noble species, hordes of rodents could never learn to respect boundaries between human and wild animal spaces. To make matters worse, unlike swarms of flying insects, they were virtually invisible: many species spent their days underground, emerging only under the cover of nightfall. This made their movements difficult to monitor. Outbreaks of plague in humans seemed to be occurring in random locations across the country, which provoked fears that rodents were multiplying uncontrollably and “colonizing” vast tracts of soil across the country.

Through analyzing the construction and reception of the concept of “veld plague” among officials working for, or collaborating with the Public Health Department, this chapter examines how the presence of Yersinia pestis in the veld was transformed into an insurmountable problem of mass indigenous animal “migration” toward cities that threatened white settlement itself. It charts how this new department, founded in 1919, developed and coordinated a Union-wide response to the problem in collaboration with zoologists at the Transvaal Museum, animals, local bureaucrats, farmers, and African laborers. To do this, I examine a series of studies into the habits, diets, and susceptibility to plague of rodents, along with experimental strategies for controlling them, which shaped national policies. The first section examines Austin Roberts and rat-catcher William Powell’s zoological investigations near Bothaville in the Orange Free State in 1920–1921, which identified the reservoirs of veld plague. Next, I analyze Secretary of Public Health James Alexander Mitchell’s development of a Union-wide epidemiological strategy to control these reservoirs in the 1920s. These comprised a citizen led “war” on rats and a form of multispecies influx control to limit both Black movement and rodent migrations into cities. Finally, I turn to a series of studies into rodent population ecology in the Witwatersrand Reef and Northern Orange Free State, which would ultimately lessen the magnitude of the problem, at least in the eyes of public health officials. These policies were received differently among different communities, and as they changed, so too did conceptions of veld plague, and veld rodents themselves.

As Christos Lynteris has argued, part of the terror associated with the Third Plague Pandemic was its perceived connection with the medieval Black Death. Plague, he argues, was “rendered an object of knowledge under the bane of its perceived ability to wipe out humanity,” which “amplified … governmental approaches to individual outbreaks, large or small.” The mobility of seemingly invisible “swarms” of wild rodents transporting fleas and the deadly Y. pestis microbe stimulated a categorical crisis within the South African Department of Public Health. These rodents were almost always referred to as veld rodents or field rodents: rodents thought to be confined to the veld, unlike “domestic” city-dwelling black and brown rats. Yet in the 1920s, rodent surveys revealed that veld rodents were gnawing on the doorstep of towns. Epidemiologists, zoologists, journalists, and local authorities began to fear that these rodents would leave their “natural habitats,” “invade” urban spaces, and bring about a resurgence of the Black Death.

Such fears were closely connected with pre-existing segregationist terrors of Black people migrating to cities en masse and “overwhelming” white populations. Black people were already subjected to urban influx controls under oppressive segregationist laws, which limited their ability to legally move between rural reserves and cities. Public health officials constantly feared that their movements were also facilitating the circulation of infected rodents and fleas into towns. The movement of both Black Africans and rodents between countryside and town, thus provoked terror and paranoia for epidemiologists, zoologists, journalists, bureaucrats, and white urban populations, resulting in violent, oppressive control measures.

In this climate of panic, perceived ecological boundaries between veld and town began to disintegrate as veld rodents were thought to enter urban spaces, and black and brown rats colonized the veld. This promoted conceptual chaos, destabilizing a series of binaries that structured how public health officials thought about nature itself. Distinctions between animals deemed wild or domestic, useful or harmful, or healthy or unhealthy, were destabilized, stimulating a period of taxonomic fluidity in which further boundaries between primitive and modern, human and animal, expert and layperson, and importantly, veld and town, were damaged. Ultimately, the attempts of farmers, medical scientists, and bureaucrats to check the advance of rodents and restore borders to “farm, veld and town” provoked vast environmental and architectural changes in the countryside.

The concept of veld plague was shaped by a series of human and non-human actors: from boundary-crossing rodents, to epidemiological maps, to farmers declaring “war” on gerbil “infestations,” to animal “allies” in the “war on rats.” As its epidemiology changed, and so too did relationships between groups of humans and rodents. In 1920, plague on the veld was considered an introduced disease of filth and primitivity to be eradicated. By 1925, it was reconceived as a symptom of a “balance of nature” deeply upset by the modernization of agriculture, which would provoke veld rodents to encroach into cities. It could only be addressed by an all-out war on rats, fought by farmers, technologies, and carnivorous animal “allies.”

Maps were key tools in representing and monitoring the movements of nigh-invisible subterranean veld rodents and are a lynchpin of this chapter. As rodents and Y. pestis moved across human-inscribed boundaries, these maps, which attempted to chart their mobilities, also circulated across government desks, in conference rooms, in the press, and in lecture halls, promoting action against rodents and communicating the success of the war. By the 1940s, the “war on rats” largely came to an end, as animal population ecologists repaired the damaged boundaries between human and animal, wild and domestic, veld and town. Veld plague was reconstructed as a disease of impoverished living conditions and primitivity, spread to humans by self-domesticating veld rodents and feral domestic rats. The end of a twenty-year period of taxonomic fluidity also led to the professionalization of rodent science and control. By the early 1940s, the vernacular knowledge of farmers, once useful to scientists, was denounced as a source of danger that might exacerbate the problem.

### The Genesis of Veld Plague: Investigations in the Orange Free State, 1920–1925

In February 1920, under the blazing summer sun, an elderly settler farmer sat down in the shade of a bush to take a midday rest on his farm, Grootdraai, near Bothaville in the Orange Free State. This simple act proved lethal: about one week later, his fever was racing and he died of the dreaded bubonic plague. Only a month later, a second outbreak occurred eight miles away “amongst natives on the farm ‘Angra Pequina.’ ” These discoveries formed the latest in a series of plague outbreaks that had been appearing in remote rural parts of the Cape since 1914 and the Orange Free State and Transvaal provinces since 1916. Such outbreaks were confounding: in many cases, there was no evidence to suggest human transmission, “ordinary domestic rodents” (black and brown rats) who had spread the disease in cities were few or absent, and no evidence of infection among “wild rodents” could be found. The Johannesburg-based South African Institute for Medical Research had suspected that infection was “being perpetuated and spread by wild rodents; the gerbille or nachtmuis [likely Gerbilliscus brantsii], the long-eared mouse (Malocothrix) and the veld muis (multimammate mouse),” but no such evidence could be found.

Although plague had only been “smoldering” in the countryside, it had spread like wildfire in cities from 1900 to 1905. The Third Plague Pandemic of 1894–1959 had ravaged port cities around the globe, and scientists had conducted extensive research into its etiology, epidemiology, and bacteriology. In South Africa, between 1899 and 1901, infected rats arrived in the docks of Cape Town, Port Elizabeth, East London, and Durban and started a series of plague outbreaks. Cape Town was hit hard, and Mitchell was instrumental in efforts against the disease. Mitchell was an Irish-born medical doctor trained at the University of Glasgow and Edinburgh, and an Assistant Medical Officer of Health of the Cape Colony in the first decade of the twentieth century. In attempt to control plague in Cape Town, a number of strategies were attempted, including rat-destruction, rat-proofing, disinfection, and racially prejudiced quarantine. African people, “Asiatics” (probably meaning both south and east Asian people), and rodents were targets of this epidemiological campaign. Initially, anyone who might have come into contact with the disease was removed to “an isolation camp, and kept there under surveillance for twelve days.” Due to protests from the “educated classes,” the system was revised and only people who had “specially been exposed to infection” or those who could not “be relied upon to remain under surveillance, as in the case of lower-class Asiatics or native ‘boys’ ” were quarantined. Later, the “native population of Cape Town, except the small proportion of educated or civilized natives” was “removed to a location situated outside the town and under Government control” under suspicion that they were living in overcrowded and insanitary conditions.

Meanwhile, noting that outbreaks were almost always accompanied by mass rodent mortality, rodents were attacked with an arsenal of “traps, dogs, ferrets, poisons … and plaster of Paris,” a “disappointing” rodent-killing virus, and by rat-catchers. A bounty of 6d per “rat destroyed” was offered to all citizens. Additionally, rat-proofing measures were instituted: buildings were altered or reconstructed to “prevent the harbourage of and be as far as possible inaccessible to rats.” These strategies, Maynard Swanson has famously argued, were motivated by a racially charged “sanitation syndrome.” Black Capetonians were presumed to live in filthy conditions under which plague thrived and thus were disproportionately targeted by medical officials and blamed for the disease. Such prejudices ultimately led to the creation of the first Black “location” (labor reserve) in Cape Town, Ndabeni, itself originally a plague quarantine camp.

Whether these strategies were effective, or if plague simply burnt out once the rodents had died, is difficult to know. Nevertheless, by 1905, Y. pestis appeared to have disappeared from South Africa, and between 1906 and 1911, “no plague in man or animals was discovered in the Union.” In 1919, after the passing of the Public Health Act, which created a national department of public health, Mitchell was appointed as the first Secretary of Public Health and Chief Health Officer. It was his responsibility to formulate a plan of action against Y. pestis in the veld. Yet nobody had yet proved which animals were the rural reservoirs of disease. The discovery of plague near Bothaville provided a new opportunity for investigation into whether wild rodents were responsible, and the Public Health Department commissioned Austin Roberts to investigate. Much like the cases of the big game in Zululand and the elephants in Addo, Mitchell’s initial solution was to restore categorical and physical boundaries between wild and domestic spaces by destroying the animals responsible for spreading an “urban” disease in rural areas. Roberts was thus given a seemingly simple task: locate the rodents that had carried Y. pestis onto farms—and figure out how to exterminate them.

Roberts arrived in Bothaville on April 30, 1920, and just less than two weeks later, set up camp on Angra Pequina and began his search for the rodents responsible—dead or alive. Live specimens were procured by digging up burrows (individual tunnels) and catching rodents fleeing his spade. Dead specimens were collected by trapping: Roberts would spend an entire day laying out fifty to sixty traps, carrying caught specimens to his camp, disinfecting them, examining them for fleas, and then skinning them by a campfire. This meant working from “about sunrise and not finishing until about 10 p.m.” Roberts’s letters to his colleagues at the Transvaal Museum and to the Public Health Department depict the investigation as a Sisyphean task, marked by failure, loneliness, and misery. Everything seemed to be working against him. Although the plague farm was a paradise for rodents, fleas, and microbes, it was purgatory for humans. The farm had been “disinfected”—likely through fumigation in buildings and rodent poisoning in the fields—yet people were still dying of plague nearby. Every time Roberts handled a specimen, he worried he might contract the disease. He was constantly short of firewood, and by the end of May, winter’s chills had set in. He spent his evenings around a fire bin within his tent, which produced “as much smoke as warmth” and forced him to “open the tent to let the smoke out (and the cold in again!).”

Working conditions were equally dire. Perhaps a result of experiences of racially motivated quarantine regulations in cities, Black laborers in Bothaville and surrounds appear to have been particularly cautious of contracting plague. Only a single laborer agreed to work for Roberts. This man, likely taking precautions against the disease, refused to assist Roberts in rodent catching, and would only “procure fuel, do menial tasks, and remain at camp during my frequent absences.” Finding small and shy rodents while working largely alone was a considerable challenge. Trying to catch them live or kill them in traps was even more difficult. On May 19, Roberts spent the day digging up burrows with a spade, catching only a single mouse. Laying out rat traps was equally frustrating: heavy rains rendered his traps ineffective on several occasions, and mongooses devoured captured rodents while he slept. The few indigenous gerbils and multimammate mice that he caught showed “no positive evidence as to the presence of plague.” Y. pestis remained concealed.

After nearly two months of difficulties, on June 23, Roberts was met with what was, for him, a glimpse of hope. Several new cases of plague had cropped up among Black people in the region, and a “much mutilated and long dead specimen of Dendromus [sic]” was found in a well they had been using. Roberts, now working in midwinter, had still not found any conclusive evidence of plague, and claimed that “it is futile to look for what the Health Dept appear to want.” Desperate to leave the field, he took the new cases as evidence of the presence of infected rodents on Angra Pequina and surrounds, and wrote that he was “convinced that the plague exists amongst the indigenous rodents here … there are many circumstances governing its spread, which is slow, and that the animals which get the plague do not necessarily show the symptoms as clearly as in larger ones, such as Black Rats or man.”

Finally, in mid-July, he was allowed to return home. The land, the weather, the rodents, and the carnivorous mammals had defeated him, and his investigation had failed to identify the chief reservoir or reservoirs of plague on the veld. Its lack of results was terrible news: if one of the foremost zoologists of the country had been unable to find and destroy rodents, what chance did the Health Department have of eradicating plague? From an epidemiological perspective, the plague outbreak in Bothaville (and the earlier mysterious cases of the 1910s) had diverged sharply from turn-of-the-century South African experiences of plague. Plague in the veld appeared to be an entirely different disease to plague in cities. As Lynteris has argued in the case of plague on the Chinese-Russian frontier, although bacteriology identified “what plague was, it was unable to determine what plague did” and thus “could not by itself establish an understanding of plague as a disease.” The same was the case in the Orange Free State: plague in the veld, with its numerous suspected reservoirs and fewer visible symptoms in rodents, was distinct from urban outbreaks. The natural history and bionomics of the veld rodent reservoirs were entirely different to that of black and brown rats. Further investigation in situ was urgently needed.

On July 16, 1920, William Powell, a rat-catcher, arrived to take Roberts’s place. Powell was instructed to locate, trap, and kill rodents, as well as capture their fleas and transmit them to the Health Department. Powell, described by the Cape Town newspaper the Cape Times as a “genius among rat-catchers,” left no field notes, nor scientific publications detailing his investigations, but three reports (1921, 1922, and 1926) provide descriptions of his activities. Following Roberts’s departure, Powell, who evidently had superior resources, and was supported by two “Plague Medical Officers” along with a team of African laborers commenced “extensive digging operations” at suspected gerbil burrows. Powell and his team had considerable vernacular knowledge of rodents, and of strategies for detecting and catching them, and thus were accustomed to labors unfamiliar to Roberts. Yet despite this expertise, Powell could not find any visible evidence of plague among the rodents he caught. In a report penned by Assistant Health Officer L. G. Haydon, Haydon recounted the difficulties of such work. Because these animals were “strictly night animals and burrowers” with burrows of “considerable depth and extent, branching in several directions,” “considerable difficulties were encountered in their capture and in the investigation of their habits.” Thus, operating under Roberts’s earlier speculation about the presence of plague and absence of symptoms, Powell’s team sought to test this hypothesis with a field experiment. The team imported several healthy gerbils and placed them in cages near burrows where plague infection was suspected. According to Mitchell, “for long the results were negative.” However, in February 1921, their efforts were rewarded. In a burrow one of the Health Department’s “digging parties” found “20 or 30 dead and dying gerbilles. … Almost simultaneously some of the caged healthy rodents which had been used as bait also contracted the disease.” Evidently, the infected rodents they had found underground had “exchanged fleas” with the healthy rodents.

Later, Powell’s team discovered that veld rodents were not scarce as Roberts’s data suggested, but superabundant. After a “large colony of burrows” in the area in which the farmer took his midday rest was “excavated,” almost certainly by African laborers, a total of eight gerbils were found, along with evidence that the others had migrated. In addition, 350–400 multimammate mice were found, of which 150 had recently died, and 100 appeared to be sick. The living mice were caught and placed under observation in cages. Most died a week later and were found to be plague infected upon bacteriological analysis. This combination of observational and experimental evidence was taken as proof that the gerbil was “probably the main factor in” plague’s “persistence and spread,” and the “multimammate mouse” was usually the “connecting link” that conveyed fleas to humans. With such evidence at hand, there was “no longer any doubt” in Mitchell’s mind that “wild veld rodents” and potentially the “striped muishond and yellow mungoose [sic]” were responsible. This posed an extremely dangerous problem. The rural Orange Free State produced “cereal food stuffs and forage, which are sent in to the towns,” risking the “transference of infected fleas or rodent remains” or the “extension of the … domestic rat population to the area in question” and accordingly, a “serious epidemic affecting human life and trade may arise, traversing the routes of the transit of grain and other food stuffs.”

Despite the dangers posed by this problem, attempts at eradicating plague on the veld were swiftly abandoned. Gerbils and other veld rodents were small and seldom seen, but they were innumerable. More confoundingly, they were virtually invisible. Numerous species dug extensive burrows and spent their days underground, only emerging at nightfall. Their innumerability and invisibility meant that strategies that were successful in urban contexts, designed to exterminate, starve, or drive away brown and black rats, were of little use in the open and unenclosed veld. Veld rodents were uninterested in poisoned bait, preferring their easily accessible “principal food” such as wild tubers and seeds, could escape human persecution by burrowing in the soil, and outnumbered farmers exponentially. More problematically, according to Haydon, attempts at fumigating their burrows “either with or without special apparatus” were “met with scant success owing to the closure of burrow passages and the branching or the burrows.”

A briefly mentioned possibility was, however “providing or increasing their natural enemies, or of discovering and spreading a disease among them which is not fatal to human beings.” Yet despite this glimmer of hope in Haydon’s report, Mitchell was more pessimistic. The “limitation or eradication of the infection in these wild animals,” he wrote, “is an exceedingly difficult problem.” As such, no attempts at total extermination on a national scale were recommended. A new strategy for fighting plague and a new concept of the disease itself began to develop concurrently. Although bacteriological examination had proven the organism to be Y. pestis, the epidemiology of the disease and the bionomics of its hosts in the veld were entirely different to plague in cities. Public health officials began referring to it as if it were a different disease: “veld plague.”

Veld plague, unlike plague in cities, could not be conceived as an invasion to be eradicated because this would force the Public Health Department to attempt an expensive and impossible extermination campaign. The fact that Y. pestis seemed to have crossed boundaries between discreet spheres of town and veld stimulated a period of taxonomic fluidity within the Health Department, in which boundaries between wild and domestic, human and animal were disrupted. To explain the emergence and spread of veld plague, Mitchell suggested that the porous boundaries between veld and town were responsible for the problem and offered two possibilities. Firstly, tapping into widespread white fears of Black urbanization, Mitchell argued that plague might have spread to veld rodents in 1916 because of the mobility of supposedly flea-carrying Black migrant workers, who moved between veld and towns in search of work.

Secondly, and more unsettlingly, it was possible that Y. pestis had been introduced into the veld as early as 1903, a year in which several outbreaks were detected in King Williams Town (now Qonce) and Queenstown (now Komani), both towns relatively far from the coastal epicenters, also along routes of Black labor-seeking. This raised a disturbing possibility: veld rodents thought to be “wild” were entering cities, exchanging fleas with “domestic” black and brown rats, and spreading an enzootic infection as they moved across the country. Evidence already existed to support this hypothesis. In Knysna, 1905, wild striped mice were found dead of plague on the outskirts of the town. The mobility of these animals disrupted the assumptions of zoologists and epidemiologists that certain rodents were confined to the veld. All other supposedly wild rodents such as springhaases, multimammate mice, and gerbils were now threats that might reintroduce plague into cities. This calamity had to be prevented. With rodent eradication off the table, Mitchell and Powell began gathering information to chart the movements and extent of veld rodents and the Y. pestis microorganism they and their fleas carried. To organize such data, a technology was needed to visualize, simplify, comprehend, and communicate information about the underground world of rodents, fleas, and Y. pestis, as well as their boundary crossing between veld, farm, and town. The technology of choice was a brightly-colored map.

### Constructing Veld Plague: Maps, Coloniality, and Segregation

From 1920 onward, Powell and a team of rodent inspectors, as well as Black and Coloured laborers, surveyed the country for plague and veld rodents. Their findings served as data from which epidemiological maps were drawn, printed, and distributed across South Africa. These maps were updated every year to plot the spread of infection. Powell’s survey reports included accounts of the route taken on each survey, descriptions of the rodents he found, and the extent of infection. Although Powell reported the presence of other rodents believed to be susceptible to plague, gerbils were taken as the primary index of infection: rodent inspectors would chart the extent of disease “noting the presence or absence of surface indications of gerbilles and other wild rodents, and the proportion of deserted burrows.” Because no other gerbil diseases were yet known, Mitchell argued that “extensive mortality in gerbille colonies can be safely attributed to plague.” Vernacular knowledge was also of interest to Mitchell and his colleagues. In some cases, farmers were interviewed—surveyors asked them to communicate changes in rodent populations over time. These surveys were then periodically verified by detailed burrow digging.

The more territory Powell and other rodent inspectors surveyed, the worse the situation became. By 1924, surveys reported that plague spanned a far greater area than just Bothaville and surrounds: infected veld rodents existed in three of four provinces. Such findings were charted on the first published plague map, which was informed by a series of earlier rodent surveys and sketch maps. The earliest of these sketch maps that I have been able to find was produced by the Department of Public Health in 1923. Here, an unnamed cartographer identified both gerbils and multimammate mice as the major risks, dividing an infected portion of the Cape province and its surrounds into plague-infected areas “favourable to the prevalence of gerbilles” and “multimammate mice,” areas “favourable to the prevalence of wild rodents” where the animals remained healthy, and uninfected areas “less favourable to the prevalence of gerbilles and multimammate mice.” The areas were shaded in yellow, blue, and white, respectively. The cartographer also marked hills in black shading, perhaps because these acted as natural barriers to the movements of these animals.

The Department of Public Health’s first published map was printed by the Government Printer in 1924 and appeared in the Annual Report of the Department of Public Health, as well as in the public press. Like its predecessor, the map utilized a series of colors to depict areas infected, plotted against ecological conditions deemed favorable to the proliferation of rodent populations by Roberts and Powell. Although it drew upon the format established by the sketch map of 1923, it singled out gerbils as the primary risk, rather than multimammate mice, who were suspected of transmitting fleas to humans. Yellow represented “Country suitable to abundant populations of gerbilles and other rodents” known to carry plague. Blue represented uninfected country that was suitable for gerbils and other rodents and thus at risk of infection. Red represented country “unsuitable for gerbilles but in which other types of rodents less liable to waves of plague infection exist,” and in which only small and localized outbreaks had taken place. Such areas were declared “comparatively safe from infection.” Hatching represented the extension of plague infection in gerbils. In 1925, the map was updated to include more areas recently surveyed in the Cape Province. This map appears to have formed the basis of Mitchell’s anti-rodent strategy and analysis of the cause of the disease.

These maps were not only devices used to communicate the extent of the infection, nor were they an exercise in the imposition of colonial spatial power and ordering, as numerous historians of cartography have argued. Instead, as Timothy Mitchell has demonstrated in the case of the Great Map of Egypt (in the first decade of the twentieth century), these maps were technologies that “revealed facts that were previously invisible.” The plague maps rendered the arcane world of migratory rodents, fleas, and Y. pestis conspicuous upon a single sheet of paper that could be circulated across the country and coordinate the anti-plague campaign. Moreover, they were, in Lynteris’s terms, “objects of epidemiological reasoning.” As I shall shortly demonstrate, they were tools in transforming the intangible idea of an upset balance of nature into a material object.

Although he left no comments on the maps themselves, nor any documentation as to how exactly he used them, Mitchell reported in 1928 that the maps had been important to his anti-plague strategy: they allowed one to “follow the gradual spread of plague infection among the veld rodents.” The evidence suggests, however, that they fulfilled a far more significant purpose than just following the spread of infection—to borrow from Marta Hanson, they were “persuasive graphics” that “did complex work … to clarify yet unknown causal relationships” and served as tools “for expanding governmental control over human and non-human populations.” The maps shaped Mitchell’s interpretation of veld plague, his definition of the disease, and his strategy for controlling it.

When confronted with the 1924 and 1925 maps, one thing is immediately clear: the Orange Free State, an Afrikaner farmer stronghold known for maize production in the early twentieth century, was heavily infected. By comparison, some “native reserves,” although at this point not yet surveyed, seemed to be plague-free, at least by the reports of magistrates in such areas. According to the Magistrate of Tabankulu, people in his “entirely Native district” prosecuted constant and intensive war against rats and were supported by a large number of “natural enemies.” Whether this meant that Black vernacular experts were intentionally killing plague-carrying rodents, or if their lifestyle had simply spared rodent predators, plague seemed to be a far less significant problem in “entirely native” areas.

Although Black people were blamed for the urban plague epidemic in Cape Town, when they stayed in their “natural” areas (native reserves), they and nature allegedly remained healthy. Throughout the 1920s and 1930s, Black people were only blamed for plague when their living conditions and mobilities contradicted the romantic vision of rural Nguni pastoralism. As Saul Dubow has argued, “under rural conditions, Africans remained in a state of ‘unspoilt’ primitive dignity. By contrast, the cities were portrayed as an alien environment, a corrosive source of decay and pollution from which Africans had to be protected or excluded.”

Such broader concerns about Black urbanization were influential in the Department of Public Health. Not only did Mitchell initially interpret labor migrancy as the cause of veld plague, but Africans were continuously blamed for creating environmental conditions conducive to rodents and fleas. Their blankets, which were worn as cloaks, togas, headdresses, or skirts, were allegedly frequented by plague-infected fleas. Black movement between different areas was at times identified as a source of risk, and various state regulations were printed in African-language newspapers prohibiting the “movement of natives” during plague outbreaks. When on the peripheries of white villages, their homes were accused of providing harborage for multimammate mice who had supposedly domesticated themselves. In such areas, “native” and “Coloured” death rates were considerably greater than “European” deaths. In 1936, even visiting the funerals of their plague-killed family members and friends was declared an epidemiological risk because many traveled long distances to attend such events. Under this logic, which was rapidly being translated into segregationist policy, movement outside of “customary” areas produced ill health. On the other side of the coin, when living under “natural,” pastoral conditions, Black Africans were regarded as promoters of public health.

This presents another side to Swanson’s “sanitation syndrome” argument. Black people were not necessarily considered unhealthy by default, but only if living in “unnatural” conditions. If they stayed in their supposedly “natural” place, this would promote health. When they transgressed such boundaries by migrating or moving to towns, disease would spread. In other words, the closer they encroached upon urban spaces, the unhealthier they became, but when “preserved” in reserves, their lifestyle was one that protected public health. The imposition of influx and movement controls on Black people traveling between veld and town were thus partly justified by racist public health officials as a means of preventing the importation of plague infected rodents or fleas into cities.

Black people (or at least sympathetic writers) contested such racist assumptions in Umteteli wa Bantu, and instead blamed white employers for the problem, but nevertheless played into similar preservationist logic. One reporter stated that it was “not surprising that Natives have been the first to suffer” from plague because, living under “European … conditions,” they had “lost much of that passion for cleanliness … which distinguished so many tribes in the old days.” The cheap and poor diets whites provided to laborers working for them were in stark contrast to the nutritious food they had once consumed, which meant that “Natives in the urban areas have degenerated in disease-resisting stamina” and “are now highly susceptible to the ravages of plague.”

Despite such protests, unlike in the case of the nagana problem, Black vernacular experts were never consulted in combatting, controlling, and understanding veld rodents by public health officials. Nowhere did any scientist argue that the lack of plague in some Black rural areas stemmed from mastery over the environment. Yet the putative absence of plague in “entirely native districts” suggested that humans could serve a critical function in controlling rodent populations. Or perhaps it indicated that indigenous humans had always served such functions before their lands were seized by colonizers. Powell’s survey maps indicated that plague seemed to be concentrated in white settler agricultural areas where wildlife had been persecuted and Africans displaced or forced into wage laborer and sharecropping. The fact that the yellow (infected) areas corresponded with rural agricultural frontiers characterized by small human populations and large monocultures prompted a disturbing realization: “civilizing” forces had produced outbreaks of a medieval disease.

As progressive farmers colonized the highveld, as the enclosure of land was legislated, and as large maize estates were established in the early twentieth century, rodents were spoilt with abundant food and created “colonies” in the soil. In the nineteenth century, vast tracts of country, once used seasonally by small-scale African farmers and trekboers, had been unsuitable for these rodents. As farmers destroyed bush for plantations and persecuted “vermin,” they unintentionally ended what might be called a natural war on rodents fought by birds of prey, jackals, wildcats, and mongooses. Without any “natural enemies” to police rodent populations, this had paved the way for extensive subterranean veld rodent colonization.

Although Mitchell thought “veld rodents” in general were dangerous, he still identified gerbils as the major risk. Colony was the standard word used in this period to describe gerbil habitations and typically referred to a network of burrows and warrens. Although no public health official ever drew an explicit parallel between human and animal coloniality in 1925, there are affinities here. As if mirroring the process of European colonization, the maps seemed to suggest that gerbils were migrating and establishing new “colonies” in settled lands. As Susan D. Jones has observed in the case of prairie dogs in the United States, such rodent coloniality at times competed with settler coloniality. The maps thus provided further evidence for a widely circulated theory in the 1920s: the balance of nature across much of the highveld had been deeply disturbed (see chapter 3).

Veld plague also helped naturalize the logic of urban influx control: keeping Black populations in cities to a minimum was interpreted by racist whites as a part of the natural order. Public health officials thus closely connected segregationist thinking and rodent control. When in native reserves, Black people were thought to wage prolific war against veld rodents in their crop fields. By contrast, when in or on the peripheries of urban areas, their connections with these animals allegedly became less combative and increasingly close and pathological. A fervor for rodent killing was supposedly replaced by an inability to keep them from unclean urban or peri-urban homes. Additionally, Black movement into towns had already been pathologized for supposedly transporting infected rodents or fleas into new areas. In this way, paranoid white doctors imbricated the mobilities of Black people, rodents, and fleas between veld and town. Black influx controls were thus naturalized as essential in maintaining national public health. Soon, such segregationist thinking began to influence the management of rodents themselves.

### The Multispecies War on Veld Rodents: Propaganda, Influx Control, and Anti-rodent Boundaries

In the 1920s, in the context of an aggressive bird protection campaign spurned by economic zoology devotees, plague on the veld was conceptualized as a symptom of a balance of nature, deeply upset by the colonization of the highveld, the planting of maize monocultures, and extermination of “vermin.” What followed was an intensive propaganda push for a citizen-led war on veld rodents, coupled with a concerted attempt to impose legal protection on birds of prey and small carnivora, and an appeal to farmers to allow these animals to settle on their lands. In the 1920s and 1930s, farmers and rural townsfolk were bombarded with a combination of posters, lectures from Roberts and Frederick FitzSimons, and films, along with practical demonstrations on fumigating, poisoning, and trapping rodents, which all aimed to spread a single message: kill rodents or risk dying of plague.

In the process of conducting this campaign, various boundaries that structured life on the veld were blurred. Drawing upon arguments derived from economic ornithology, FitzSimons, Roberts, Mitchell, and others framed carnivorous birds, small predatory mammals, and snakes as public health workers and agricultural laborers that could contribute to human health and wealth. Simultaneously, humans were depicted as rodent predators that could fulfill the roles animals once had in the balance of nature. Although met with varying degrees of acceptance and hostility among farmers and politicians, this campaign nevertheless succeeded in imposing legal protection on numerous predatory birds and agitating some farmers (both white and African) to declare war on rodents on their land and vow to protect their natural enemies.

Not only did this war destabilize the boundaries between human and animal roles in the economies of nature and humankind, but so too did it blur the distinctions between scientific and vernacular expertise. As was characteristic of settler science in other chapters, the crusade against rodents was marked by reciprocal debate and exchange of knowledge among scientists, farmers, and even animals. According to Roberts, South African farmers were men of intellect who played an important role in producing observational and experimental knowledge about rodents that could be integrated into official plague control strategies. As part of Mitchell’s war on rodents, Roberts conducted an extensive series of lecture tours in which he, unlike in the birds and agriculture debate, embraced economic zoology as a strategy of plague control. For Roberts, even if wholesale bird protection was unhelpful to farmers, certain species of predatory birds and carnivores did play an important role in the combatting of plague. During his lecture tours, Roberts felt that at times, he was learning from farmers rather than them learning from him. While scientists were training farmers in anti-rodent warfare, so too were farmers adapting and experimenting with what they had learned, to improve results. In Bethal, a town marked as plague-free but in gerbil-suitable country on the plague map, Roberts wrote

Several farmers and local-store-keepers informed me that they had no results from barium carbonate which I was recommending, but that strychnine had proved very effective. Another had trained two common dogs from puppyhood to hunt rats and mice, and his lands which were formerly infested with the rodents (probably Gerbilles) had been cleared by these dogs. The local Sanitary Inspector informed me that he used a pump with a gas mixture of sulphur and arsenic, which proved to be much more effective than “Capex.”

Not only farmers, but so too did animals contribute to the production of settler scientific knowledge of rodent control: the hunting strategies of predatory animals at times served as models for rodent extermination. In a pamphlet published by William Powell and distributed widely across rural areas, Powell argued that should the farmer be lucky enough to have surviving “natural enemies” of rodents on their lands, these could be enlisted in the fight. The “mongoose” (yellow mongoose), which Mitchell had initially suspected to be a reservoir of plague, could in fact act as an early-warning system for plague. Although these animals attempted to hunt rodents, they seldom succeeded, usually settling for insects, lizards, snakes, or birds’ eggs instead. Sick rodents, however, were easy prey. Hence, if their dung contained primarily insects, there was little to worry about. If it was full of fur, this was a sign of dying rodents, and thus plague. Powell suggested that rodent hunters should closely observe and follow this animal: they should look “to see if a mongoose is hunting in the vicinity and if seen, note where he goes to ground and inspect his warren.”

However, although Powell’s pamphlets suggested that farmers could learn to detect the presence of plague through observing the yellow mongoose, this animal and other “natural enemies” of rodents alone could not teach them rodent destruction. Unlike these animals, humans had various sensory and somatic shortcomings. They lacked the eyes of hawk or the dexterity of the wildcat. Their bodies were too large and cumbersome to access complicated underground rodent colonies: they could not tunnel into burrows and devour their occupants. Unlike owls and jackals, they lacked the ability to sense rats in the night. A combination of human and animal ingenuity was necessary to account for such deficiencies. Numerous weapons of rodent destruction promoted by the Public Health Department arguably sought to mimic the agency of these “natural enemies” of rodents and compensate for human physiological shortcomings. Rat traps could be deployed as automated rodent-predators and placed in strategic locations to catch rats that had once been devoured by jackals and wildcats. Carefully positioned strychnine-syrup-coated wheat could catch rodents emerging from their burrows and seeking sustenance. Capex gas cartridges could be distributed into rodent colonies where snakes had once feasted on rats. Bodies of water could serve as bait: rodents needed to drink every second day, and dogs and cats could be stationed at water sources to kill them.

Thus, within this campaign, both white human and wild animal vernacular knowledge was taken seriously—they were considered experts of a kind on their own lands, whose knowledge could be integrated into rodent settler science. In continuing a “natural war” against veld rodents, a combination of the hunting expertise of animals, the environmental knowledge of farmers, and modern pest-control sciences were deemed necessary. Black and Coloured vernacular knowledge, on the other hand, was completely ignored by these scientists. Although Black farmers were at times considered prolific rodent hunters, their hunting strategies were never studied by the public health department. To an extent, they were shut out of the archive of veld plague, unlike in the case of nagana or wild birds. Nevertheless, there is little doubt that Black and Coloured laborers on farms played a significant role in the prosecution of the war on veld plague and in studies of its epidemiology.

Whether this civilian- and animal-led war on veld rodents made any impact on the plague problem is difficult to judge. Vital statistics produced by the South African Department of Public Health do little to suggest that it was effective. Deaths from plague were tabulated on a yearly basis and indicated periodic outbreaks rather than a general decline across the period.

Part of restoring the natural order to the countryside also meant rodent-proofing the boundaries between veld and town. Extensive connections between Black labor-seeking in cities and the spread of veld plague had already been made, helping to naturalize policies of influx control among racist white officials. In 1925, connected policies, shaped by segregationist and preservationist thinking, were extended to rodents themselves. If restrictions had already been placed on the movement of Black people between infected and uninfected areas, so too did officials believe that restrictions needed to be placed on the movement of rodents between veld and town. The city was typically considered the natural habitat of rodents associated with European colonization and trade: black and brown rats. Allowing indigenous veld rodents into cities thus posed a pathological inversion of the natural order.

In 1925, such fears were expressed by the Cape Provincial authorities, who were entirely unconvinced by a strategy that was primarily prosecuted by citizens and wild animals. Distrusting vernacular expertise, they instead suggested that efforts should be concentrated in small, key areas by rodent-control professionals. The Cape had suffered greatly during the previous urban plague epidemic, and its provincial government was terrified by the prospect of veld plague. The plague map wielded considerable ontological power among these bureaucrats: it suggested that plague was right on their doorstep. The Cape Flats, at this point a sandy plantation area on the outskirts of Cape Town, was a paradise for rodents, and “extremely infested with gerbilles.” Powell had observed these rodents commingling with brown rats, who visited the city at night: if plague-infected rats reached the Cape Flats, the city would undoubtedly be affected. Veld plague was once again at risk of escaping the veld and entering towns. This destabilized boundaries that were thought to separate the health and sanitation of the modern city from the diseased veld. To prevent the encroachment of plague upon cities, physical borders between urban and wild spaces had to be reinforced.

Thus, Cape public health officials and bureaucrats advocated a strategy like David Mitchell’s anti-nagana buffer zone policy in Zululand: the creation of physical boundaries separating veld from town rodents. The Department of Public Health, they insisted, should create vast defenses around cities and agricultural areas, referred to as “rodent-free belts” or “fire belt[s]” to prevent, or restrict, veld rodents from crossing into cities. These barriers would stop rodents from reaching key areas by selecting tracts of country on the plague map upon which rodents seemed to be encroaching and destroying all rodents and rodent-harborage in such areas.

At a series of conferences held in Cape Town in late 1925 to early 1926, the rodent-free belt strategy was debated. At the first conference on September 14, 1925, J. Higgins, the Medical Officer of Health for Cape Town, supplied a large map “showing the boundaries of the Division and the areas of Local Authorities within it,” which he planned to exhibit in the conference room. Within this room, perhaps utilizing the map, Dr. Abdullah Abdurahman, Cape Town doctor, first Coloured city councilor, and advocate of antiracism and nonracialism, suggested that the Union government could create a “fire belt” around the peripheries of the city. Powell assessed this suggestion, and argued that such a belt would need to be a mile wide and twenty-five miles long. It would be cleared by “the systematic working of the area by five gangs consisting of one European Overseer and two Coloured men equipped with ‘Capex’ gas cartridges and spades to exterminate the rodents.” F. C. Wilmot of the Public Health Department immediately responded that the Union government would not pay for such a measure. This infuriated M. C. Vos, the Mayor of Wynberg, then a village on the outskirts of Cape Town, who argued that the government seemed more willing to spend money protecting cattle from scab and ticks than defending humans from plague.

One month later, it was suggested at a second conference that such a belt should extend from Table Bay to False Bay, at a cost of approximately £1,850 per annum. The Public Health Department, the provincial authorities insisted, should pay two-thirds of the costs of creating this belt, as was mandated under the 1919 Public Health Act. The proposal was rejected by the Public Health Department, which felt that such a policy would not be a success. This continued to enrage Vos, along with several other Cape Provincial officials. At a meeting on January 26, 1926, Vos complained that the Union government seemed more interested in protecting domestic animals than humans. Their decision to assist “farmers by destroying Jackals and vermin has been to the detriment of the towns and villages owing to the great increase in the number of rodents,” and now that the “natural balance of nature had been destroyed,” the local authorities were expected to conduct an expensive war against rodents. At a third conference, held one hour later, Vos’s fury had not subsided, and he slated the Union government, arguing that it was to blame for veld plague, having spent £308,760 between 1887 and 1924 on the destruction of jackals, wildcats, and other rodent-eating pests. These actions had been taken to protect a rural minority, and now an upset balance of nature was threatening cities.

In late 1926, after the plague map was updated, Mitchell and his department began to change their tune. The new iteration of the map indicated that the situation had become “very grave.” Infection had “recently spread south and south-west to the Williston and Calvinia districts, extending southwards … and westwards.” It was now threatening to reach the Cape Peninsula and Ceres—the former a densely populated area, and the latter a profitable agricultural region and essential food-supplier to the province. As such, Mitchell recognized the renewed necessity of protecting cities from the supposed invasion of veld rodents, offered the support of the Public Health Department, and sanctioned the construction of a buffer zone. This buffer would not only clear the area of rodents but render it antithetical to their proliferation. Mountains and rivers, the plague map showed, were impassable to rodents and thus could be reinforced to prevent or delay their incursion into Ceres and Cape Town. If the total of forty miles of country between these “natural barriers” could be cleared of rodent-harborage, fumigated, and poisoned, it might render these areas unappealing to them, and “have the effect of preventing, or at least delaying, the spread of infection.” Essentially, conducting perpetual warfare against rodents in key strategic belts would make it easier to maintain physical and conceptual boundaries between veld and town, and separate their respective rodent populations.

In early 1927, exterminators armed with poison, traps, gas, and dogs “cleared” the buffer between the “Olifants and Doorn Rivers and the mountain range to the south east” and reportedly kept it “clear of rodents.” By May, the belt was complete. Care was taken to ensure that it was deadly to all species of rodents: gerbils and hares were to be killed using gas and strychnine-syrup-coated wheat. Karoo rats, which did not take to grain, were “exterminated either by gassing or by burning their nests and killing by sticks or dogs.” Tiny striped mice were met with the same grim fate. These areas were to be periodically patrolled to ensure no rodents trespassed. Such buffer zones were rendered no-being’s-lands: they were uninhabited by farmers, and continuously patrolled by Black and Coloured laborers along with white “overseers” to ensure no rodents trespassed between spheres of veld and town.

Ultimately, Mitchell’s plan was rewarded. The agency of veld rodents, the idea of veld plague, the plague map, and anti-rodent propaganda were all shaping the physical landscape of South Africa itself. Rather than the map appearing as a representation of this landscape, the maps shaped the environment of the Cape Province, and subsequently, the rodent-free belts had altered the map. The rodent-free belts were depicted as reclaimed territories. Powell surveyed the areas once again in 1927, and the belts appeared in red on the map, becoming country unsuitable for the gerbils (see figure on page 176). Rodent-free belts were also constructed around Johannesburg and in parts of the Free State. Perhaps given the apparent success of these belts, the Cape press praised rodent-control professionals, elevating them to the sphere of scientists. In 1928, the Cape Times, declared that the “days of the old-time professional rat-catcher are gone. That individual has been replaced by the economic biologist and chemist.”

By 1933, enthusiasm for the belts had not waned. Despite Wilmot warning that the belts could never “obtain 100% rodent clearance” and that cleared areas were liable to be “re-infested,” rodent-free belts remained widely used in the 1930s. To name one example, by 1936, the Johannesburg medical authorities were still circulating maps with the press to document their efforts in keeping veld rodents away from town populations of black and brown rats and to offer a sense of security to citizens.

Here, maps played key critical conceptual roles in attempts to limit veld rodent migrancy between spheres of veld and town. The maps had provoked a need to create rodent-free belts to protect the peripheries of the Cape Province and Johannesburg from infection. Belts were cleared, which, in turn, changed the maps. The representation of rodent-free belts in red served as circular evidence that the defenses were checking the forward march of rodents. Clearing the belt colored it red on the departmental map, and this coloration in turn was taken as evidence that the rodent incursion had been quelled. In correspondence between the Public Health Department and local authorities, the map was taken as a matter of fact—and on one occasion used to negate local information about plague outbreaks. Although this buffer-zone strategy had been utilized in the nagana campaigns to control the movements of big game, and in the Addo Bush to teach the elephants to remain within the reserve, it had never been met with much success. Here, it created a sense of security—it suggested that Cape Town and Johannesburg were protected by human and animal soldiers of public health, and that healthy boundaries between veld and town were maintained.

In 1932, Mitchell stepped down from his position on account of ill health. After reading the annual report of that year, he must have felt he had reason to celebrate. The death toll from plague had been sixteen, the lowest figure “recorded for the past nine years.” A year later, he died in his Rondebosch home from complications due to hypertension, likely satisfied that he had solved the mystery of veld plague. In the twelve years of its conceptual existence, veld plague had uprooted previous understandings of the disease in urban contexts and suggested that all-out anti-rodent warfare was necessary to check its advance. By the 1930s, the movement of boundary-crossing “veld” rodents had provoked the production of settler scientific knowledge about rodent habits, diets, and mobilities. Such studies were shaped by economic zoology and charted on epidemiological maps, which produced a new concept of “veld plague.” Unlike its urban counterpart, veld plague spread slowly and smoldered with occasional conflagrations. It had few visible symptoms in rodents and was concentrated among wild rodents. It was a consequence of ending a natural war between veld rodents and their predators.

The construction of this new disease—a direct result of the mobility of boundary-crossing rodents—provoked a period of taxonomic fluidity in which categories of veld and town, wild and domestic, protected and pest, were disrupted. Its preventative remedy lay in the reinforcement of veld-town boundaries by clearing vast tracts of land of rodents around cities and the transformation of both human and animal militia into an anti-rodent army. Segregationist and preservationist thinking ultimately proved influential in shaping its control policies: public health officials closely entangled Black “influx control” and rodent control. Black labor-seeking in cities was initially blamed for seeding and spreading the disease, and attempts were also made to separate town rats and veld rodents through keeping the latter out of cities via the construction of “rodent-free belts.”

### The Conceptual End of the War on Veld Rodents: Rodent-Free Belt Failures and Investigations in the Witwatersrand Reef and Northern Orange Free State

In 1932, Mitchell’s place was taken by Assistant Health Officer Edward Thornton, who sought to adjudicate the anti-rodent strategies. As Thornton took the helm of the department of Public Health, Mitchell’s strategy began to sink. Rodents seemed unwilling to respect the boundaries created by “rodent-free belts.” In 1933, Wilmot reported that veld rodents had crossed the Cape Province belt. As such, a new line was drawn between “the Piquetberg Mountains and mountain ranges on the opposite side of the Piquetberg Valley,” and rodents were cleared within these areas at a considerable cost of £5,834. Wilmot was not convinced that this would stop the forward march of rodents, but that it would “divert or delay spread to the most extensively cultivated portions of the Piquetberg and Tulbagh areas.” If rodents reached these areas, they risked contaminating food supplies, which then would spread infection by rail to the cities. With a tone of inevitability, his hope was that if the infection could be confined to “field rodents, it may be years before it reaches Cape Town.” He evidently did not think that the advance of these animals could be stopped.

At the same time, Thornton scrutinized the efforts of farmers to destroy rodents on their properties and was shocked by their negligence in some areas. In 1936, Thornton complained that farmers in Heilbron had become apathetic to the problem. He thought that they were entitled and dispirited: they apparently felt that it was the duty of government rat-catchers to eradicate plague, and refused to fumigate, poison, and hunt rodents until rat-catchers arrived to assist them. Thornton seems to have been under the impression that this was a general trend in the countryside. One farmer even claimed that it was the governments duty to “remove fleas from his servants!”

Between June 1934 and 1936, Thornton’s fears turned to reality. Plague decimated the veld rodents, resulting in an outbreak of human plague, which claimed the lives of 349 people, the largest ever recorded in the veld over a three-year period. In 1936, the Cape rodent-free belt failed once again, and the infection became “so attenuated that we have now practically lost the line of spread of infection over most of the area.” This must have been baffling to Thornton: Mitchell’s epidemiological measures were visibly failing, and the maps depicted an ever-expanding spread of infection, but plague had not hit major urban centers. Why had it remained in the veld for more than twenty years despite these failures? There was no evidence to suggest it would ever reach cities. Perhaps there was no real disturbance to the natural order, or perhaps the risks of Y. pestis, fleas, and rodents moving between veld and town had been overestimated. Or could it be that there already existed a natural barrier preventing the spread of plague between veld and town rodents? Under the tenure of Thornton and his successor Eustace Cluver, conceptual boundaries between wild and domestic rodents, veld and town were rebuilt, and the all-out war on veld rodents came to an end.

### Louis Fourie and the Witwatersrand Reef: Debunking the Balance of Nature

Between 1936 and 1937, Assistant Health Officer Louis Fourie, a former South West African (now Namibian) Medical Officer and renowned plague expert, conducted a series of rodent and plague surveys on the Witwatersrand Reef. His investigations added further evidence to Thornton’s speculations on the inefficacy of the war on veld rodents. Fourie’s initial investigations, conducted in mid-to-late 1936, commenced with a different understanding of plague: the danger was not the alleged encroachment of veld rodents upon cities, but the advance of domestic rats upon rural areas, and the domestication of wild mice. Although Mitchell had recommended rat-proofing in rural areas in the 1920s, this was clearly not well enforced, and the position remained unsatisfactory in the Reef. At the beginning of 1937, Fourie produced a report critical of Mitchells fixation on offensive measures such as exterminating wild veld rodents at a cost of defending buildings from domestic and semi-domestic rodents through rat-proofing. “One of the most striking facts in the epidemiology of plague in South Africa,” he wrote, “is that the incidence of human cases has been very small indeed among the population which has actually been exposed to the risk of infection.”

Fourie’s report suggested that the gerbil, upon whom anti-rodent warfare had been fixated in the 1920s, was not a species that wished to move to cities. Rather, gerbils were completely wild animals and had no intention of ever leaving the veld: they were terrified of humans. Although they were occasionally found near “dwellings on the outskirts of townships,” given that they seldom associated with humans, Fourie claimed that the “the risk of direct spread of infection from this rodent to man is negligible.” Anti-rodent warfare measures developed to fight veld rodents in the 1920s, such as the “socalled rodent-free belts” had done little more than produce “a false sense of security.” In Fourie’s opinion, the idea of a protective belt was fundamentally flawed: ridding vast areas of veld rodents was impossible, and the “risk of spread of infection by contiguity from a commonage into a town” was “so small as to be negligible.”

The danger was the multimammate mouse: an animal that, like the Addo Elephants, occupied a liminal space between wild and domestic. Rather than remaining within the veld or the farmhouse, multimammates were comfortable associating with veld or house rats and inhabited gerbil burrows and human homes alike. Their categorical mobility rendered them a source of grave risk: they formed an epidemiological link between veld and home that could transport Y. pestis to humans. This was further complicated by the fact that within the Reef, “domestic” rats (Rattus rattus alexandrinus) were transcending urban spaces and colonizing the veld. Faced with these two anxiety-provoking rodents, Fourie insisted that “the campaign against veld rodents should be modified and relegated to its proper place.” Unlike gerbils, who were happy to remain within their “natural” veld habitats, multimammate mice were border crossers who urgently had to be excluded from areas of human habitation. Measures thus needed to be taken to separate the homes of humans and rodents, rather than waging an expensive war against rats in “miles of barren veld, as has been done up to the present.”

As in cities, where the sanitation syndrome dominated approaches to plague control, Fourie was convinced that there existed a relationship among Black people, rodents, poverty, and plague. In a preliminary report written in August 1936, Fourie declared that a major issue was that “native huts in rural areas are infested with house or multimammate mice.” This was explicitly a problem of race and poverty: the allegedly primitive living conditions of some humans disturbed the human-animal boundary and had enabled multimammate mice to occupy domestic spaces. In Fourie’s opinion, no “evidence of the presence of multimammates in better class dwellings … was met with anywhere.”

Fourie’s work was critical in re-establishing the multispecies boundaries between veld and town. In his work, veld plague was depicted as an issue for wild animals, but not for humans. However, wild rodents in domestic spaces, as well as domestic rodents colonizing wild spaces, were a major problem, and thus these boundary-crossing rats needed to be violently suppressed. In service of this, more rodent surveys and experienced staff were needed, and rodent-proofing and fumigation needed to be enforced in “European dwellings” and “native compounds” (mining hostels). Moreover, those living in what Fourie saw as backward living conditions, such as the “Malay Location, New Clare,” needed to submit to having their homes fumigated until new housing could be built for them. In other words, Fourie accepted that gerbils should be allowed to remain relatively undistributed within areas uninhabited by humans, and that subjecting them to expensive urban border control measures was a complete waste of effort. The correct strategy was to enforce the separation of human and domestic rodent homes in urban and peri-urban areas.

Such work required meticulous training and expertise, and in a critical change from Mitchell’s earlier policies, Fourie sought to close the porous boundary between vernacular and professional rodent experts. In his opinion, only qualified rat-catchers should take charge of the problem. The vernacular knowledge of farmers could not be integrated into settler science, and a citizen-based war on veld rodents was useless: in the case of veld plague, settler science should be the purview of professionals only. At the request of the Department of Public Health, Fourie had agreed to give a series of lectures to 172 people across eighty municipalities in the Reef. This, he thought, was likely to hinder the cause, since “knowledge of anti-rodent work cannot be acquired from written page or spoken word but only by intelligent and conscientious practical application to the study of rodents and their habits. If those who attended the course should have left under the impression that they had been equipped with a full knowledge of the subject, more harm than good would have been done by the lectures.”

In 1937, in a letter to all local authorities on the Reef, Thornton circulated copies of Fourie’s report, and instructed them to change their anti-rodent policies by impressing upon them the need that “the rodent should be built out and the domestic rat problem concentrated upon more fully than that of the veld rodent. The latter should not be entirely neglected but should be exterminated in the vicinity of mines or residential areas.”

One year later, Thornton retired, and was replaced by Eustace Cluver, previously an assistant health officer. Cluver accepted his tenure as Secretary of Public Health at a time in which the importance of ecology had reached a zenith in both public debates and government affairs in South Africa. Followers of the South African school of holistic ecology, spearheaded by Jan Smuts, John Phillips, Iltyd Pole-Evans, and William Bews, had been engaged in high-profile debates with British mechanists such as Arthur Tansley. Holistic ecologists were presenting human, plant, and animal ecology as solutions to African administration and the poor white problem, white settlement in Africa, and the degradation of nature. The plague problem intersected with all of these issues, and ecology was thus worth exploring.

Cluver did not, however, turn to the local school of holistic ecology, but looked toward Charles Elton’s animal population ecology. Elton, an ecologist based at the Oxford Bureau of Animal Population, had created a model to predict the rise and fall of rodent populations. He and biologist Julian Huxley had already provided a critique of rodent-destruction policies in the United Kingdom, which could readily be adapted to the South African context. In Huxley’s introduction to Elton’s Animal Ecology (1927), he suggested that the fluctuations in populations of rodents was “perfectly normal for many of the smaller mammals.” As Karen Sayer has noted in Britain, this periodicity was often misinterpreted by officials as evidence for the success of plague control policies. Falls in rodent populations were seen as a reversion to natural balance in response to rodent destruction. Yet such population decline had little to do with human intervention. Rodent destruction either had no effect or allowed plague to smolder by preventing rodent populations from reaching their normal limits.

Cluver and Fourie were evidently readers of Elton and seem to have recognized this issue. In his annual report of 1938, Cluver wrote that in the case of lemmings in Norway and Canada, as well as short-tailed field mice in Britain, Elton had obtained a “mass of evidence” that rodent populations swelled to enormous numbers within three to four years, until reaching a natural capacity, defined by climatic and environmental conditions. Once capacity was reached, an epizootic would reduce them to near-extinction. In 1938, Fourie published a paper in the South African Medical Journal that applied these insights to the veld. To do this, he drew upon a series of rodent population data from various sources—newspapers, annual public health reports, and personal observations—and produced a speculative analysis of the role of disease in rodent population regulation. In the 1920s, numerous reports of “enormous numbers” of wild mice in various parts of the country were published. In the Pongola-Mkuzi region of Natal, complaints about rodent swarms had appeared in the public press in 1917, 1924, 1929, 1933, and 1937. This periodicity of approximately four years corresponded with Elton’s existing theory. From these data, Fourie hypothesized that each epizootic ran its course in about six months, and subsequently surviving rodents dispersed and formed new colonies, at which point the population would recover. Elton’s population theory combined with historic data from southern Africa thus revealed a critical insight into the natural population dynamics of rodents. Fourie suggested that “in addition to the activities of predatory birds and animals, some other natural agencies for controlling their numbers must have been in operation from time immemorial. … Ample evidence has been obtained during recent years that the natural enemies alone have been unable to maintain an effective check on their numbers; even in regions where they have suffered but little from human interference.”

This key point was a challenge to the theoretical underpinning of the war on rodents, as well as the economic zoology practiced by FitzSimons and others in the previous chapter: disease, paradoxically, was protecting farmers from rodent overpopulation, rather than predatory birds. Elton’s theories undermined the idea of a balance of nature policed by predators. In Animal Ecology and Evolution, he explicitly declared the concept of balance and animal population equilibrium to be “untrue”: a long-standing ecological myth.

According to Fourie, plague outbreaks did not stem from an “upset” balance of nature but were correlated with the rise and fall of rodent populations. There was no stable balance of predators to prey among veld rodents: their population was characterized by boom and bust, and the agents that kept them in check were not predators, but microorganisms. This meant that plague had likely replaced an older, indigenous disease like Tiger River Disease (a rodent disease discovered in 1927) and was continuing the natural war upon rodents. With this new understanding of population cycles and periodicity, the idea of gerbils colonizing vast tracts of the veld and attempting to “invade” cities was no longer an apt visual metaphor to describe the problem. The problem was not one of rodents conquering space, but of time: it was a matter of predictable cycles.

Animal population ecology thus facilitated a re-evaluation of the concept of veld plague: an independent system of plague, rodents, and fleas existed. This was both impossible to interrupt and unlikely to change: the risk of plague reaching towns was minimal because veld plague was dependent on rural conditions for its proliferation. Y. pestis and its flea vectors were also unlikely to travel to cities on the backs of migratory rodents. Gerbils and multimammate mice showed “antagonism” to urban rats and could not survive in cities. This would explain why, in twenty years, the disease showed no signs of colonizing towns. Veld plague was thus redefined as a natural part of the population cycles of gerbils. This meant that veld plague cases in humans was a result of a lack of state power over health on rural frontiers, which had caused wild rats to become domestic, and enabled Black people to live in “unhygienic” conditions. As with urban plague, it was a medieval and not a modern disease: one of unsanitary living conditions. It was not a symptom of ever-spreading environmental degradation caused by the modernization of the highveld, but a result of close connections between vermin and humans in allegedly “primitive” homes. As its conceptual framework shifted, so too did strategies toward managing plague: creating buffer zones was an expensive waste of time. Gerbils were not urban invaders, but periodic visitors who arrived, reproduced, and died without encountering humans. Preventing plague in rural areas lay now in policing boundaries between human and rodent worlds through rat proofing.

### David H. S. Davis in the Northern Orange Free State: Rodent Forecasting and the Development of Medical Ecology

Although Fourie’s work began to re-establish boundaries between human and animal, veld and town, and primitive and modern, it also implied that there was a permanent chain of infection from gerbil to multimammate to domestic rat to human across much of the country. If the cartographic image of veld rodents invading white areas and barriers impeding their path was no longer appropriate, epidemiologists needed to find a new means of predicting and visualizing the rise and fall of rat populations. As if in service of this, in 1938, Cluver announced that a series of follow-up studies would be conducted into rodent population ecology at a field station in a plague area. Such studies could determine exactly at what population density plague epizootics tended to occur, and how plague could survive in periods of rodent population decline. It would enable epidemiologists to make “forecasts of epizootics” and thus fumigate rodent burrows at strategic times. Accordingly, David H. S. Davis, a graduate of Oxford University and student of Charles Elton, was appointed to the department.

In July 1938, Davis submitted a research proposal to Cluver. This document proposed investigating a broad range of population, ethological, and ecological relationships among veld rodents, fleas, plague, and climate at a single field station. Fourie’s findings that “domestic” rats and multimammate mice were the source of danger, whereas the gerbil was the main reservoir of plague, were taken for granted. As Jones has noted, students at the Bureau of Animal Population were trained to “take animal censuses and conduct experiments, collect animals, and record and analyse the resultant data in ways that could illuminate regular patterns.” Thus unsurprisingly, Davis advocated the use of quantitative methods to predict the risk of plague in particular areas during “rodent years.” Like Fourie, Davis was suspicious of vernacular expertise. However, he did recognize that it had some limited uses. Rather than mobilizing farmers as militia in the war against veld rodents, Davis suggested that they could act as an “intelligence service” and provide information on the activities of veld rodents in enzootic areas. Ultimately, his proposed investigation would connect Elton’s methods with South African research in order to quantify and chart rodent populations. This information could inform a new epidemiology of veld plague.

Davis’s proposal was approved by the Public Health Department, and in late September 1938, he commenced work at a forestry station in Holfontein, near Kroonstad, Orange Free State, along with “one European and two permanent Native assistants.” This was previously a plague-infested station, in which it was “rare to find farm buildings without a colony [of gerbils] within a hundred yards.” In the absence of field notes and records from this station itself, a series of monthly reports Davis submitted to Cluver, combined with his papers published before and after the end of World War II, provide a window onto his activities in Holfontein.

In short, Davis’s strategy was to scale down the enormity of the veld by carrying out “intensive observations on the natural history and interrelations of the plague carrying rodents in a small area.” Davis’s work was to act as cement in the brickwork that Fourie had erected between wild and domestic spaces within the developing disease ecology of veld plague. In his opinion, plague was not normally a disease of humans, but one “of rodents and it is only by the accident of contact with infective fleas from rodents that have died of plague that man becomes infected.” The task for public health was thus to reduce the chance of humans commingling with or encountering boundary-crossing rodents. His studies orbited around three major goals: (1) find a way to measure the “density of the gerbil population and the amount of surface activity shown by the state of the burrows in a colony,” (2) record the impact of the climate and veld rodent reproductive dynamics, and (3) determine a “flea-index,” meaning the average number of fleas dependent upon gerbils within a single colony.

Davis’s first task was to find a way to approximate gerbil populations in any area without having to dig up their burrows to count them individually. Previous survey work conducted by Powell and his teams had provided valuable information here: although nocturnal and subterranean-dwelling gerbils were largely invisible to human eyes, they could be detected by examining their burrows and the habits of carnivores in the area. Colonies of healthy gerbils showed evidence of digging, feeding, and spoor. Dug-up and halfeaten roots and tubers were also often visible. When plague arrived, burrowing activity gradually ceased, cobwebs formed at entrances to burrows, and scraps of fur or carcasses could be found. Here, the yellow mongoose was useful once again. As Powell had observed in the 1920s, mongoose dung could be used to as an index of plague infestation.

In order to chart which burrows were in use, Davis’s team demarcated an experimental area in which numerous burrows were present and set out a line of stakes, each fifty yards apart, creating an experimental grid of pegs in a fifty-by-fifty square-yard area. Pegs and labels were used to delineate quadrants that had active or deserted burrows. Laborers then walked through the squares and closed all burrows with a dusting of soil. The following morning, the team found that numerous burrows had been opened. These were closed again, counted, and marked within the grid. Building upon Powell’s earlier work, Davis selected a key category for study—“active” burrows, in contrast to deserted burrows. A reopened burrow “was designated as ‘active’ and was adapted as the unit of measurement of population changes.”

Davis was convinced that gerbils operated within closed systems, and thus “active burrows” could be adopted as an index to estimate gerbils populations. To do this, Davis and laborers under his employment counted the number of active burrows and then set a series of traps at the mouths of every third burrow. The team then blocked the burrows to enclose the gerbils within their individual burrows and continued trapping until a “complete census” had been achieved. His results were tabulated, and a ratio created. From this, Davis concluded that “about eight ‘active’ burrows were the equivalent of one gerbil.” This result corresponded with “trapping experiments” conducted in the eastern and southern Transvaal, which had produced ratios of between 8.1 and 8.7, and thus, eight could be taken as an approximate.

From these experiments, Davis assumed generalizations about the biology of the species could be made: a ratio of eight burrows to a gerbil was the natural state of gerbil communities. Using this ratio, one could simply count the number of active burrows to calculate gerbil populations anywhere in the country. Monitoring active burrows could enable officials to chart rodent population rises and declines, and from these data, predict plague outbreaks. What was once conceived as an infinitely complex balance of nature that threatened human health as it blurred boundaries between veld and town had been simplified to a question of counting burrows. Maps, which had once charted the advance of rodents, gave way to mathematical ratios and were used primarily as representations of quadrants or to communicate the enzootic areas. Officials could now calculate when gerbils had started dying en masse. At this point, fleas would lose their gerbil hosts, and humans were at risk: these insects would seek new hosts and occasionally found “domestic” rats or the multimammate mouse.

Although the investigation was cut short by the war, Davis’s two years of research provided empirical support for Fourie’s earlier speculations: although gerbils were the primary reservoir of veld plague, unlike the multimammate mouse, they posed little risk to humans. Likewise, the ecology of gerbils was relatively stable, and fears of the animals leaving the veld and invading cities were unfounded. Davis’s work ultimately enabled officials to chart gerbil populations more quickly and cheaply than the debunked concept of a “balance of nature” could allow. Through counting “active burrows,” epidemiologists could monitor the rise and fall of populations, forecast epidemics, and exterminate multimammate mice and domestic rats in at-risk areas.

As Davis’s work circulated across South Africa, strategies for fighting plague began to change. He wrote various memoranda that were circulated with local health authorities, offering instructions on how to conduct rodent surveys. To do this, he suggested that regular surveys of gerbil colonies should be made and monitored for activity. Once a decline in population to the point of 75 percent of their former numbers had been observed, this meant that danger was at hand: fleas would begin hopping onto “domestic” rats, who freely wandered into human homes. At this point, he wrote that farmers should be mandated to violently separate human and rodent spaces by “gas[sing] their stores, garages and outhouses and in particular their native huts and sleeping quarters.” In 1942, the Public Health Department ordered that such action be taken in Heilbron, Vredefort, and Kopjes.

This rendered farmers, who had once been experts in the war on rats, intellectually useless to epidemiologists and ecologists. Under Davis, the previously porous boundary between vernacular and scientific knowledge continued to close. Unlike Mitchell’s earlier economic zoology-based epidemiology, Davis did not view farmers as partners in combatting or studying veld rodents. As with Fourie, when it came to plague, settler science was the purview of professionals only: vernacular knowledge produced by decades of farming experience and everyday interactions with rodents were no longer of much use. Perhaps because of the outbreak of war, perhaps because of the technicality of the discipline, no widespread efforts were made to teach farmers population ecology or to learn from them. In all of Davis’s studies, farmers served as sources of data or hospitality and little else.

Despite this shift in expertise, in the 1940s, the Union government continued to encourage separating rodent and human homes through exterminating the animals in domestic spaces, plowing fields, and other areas of human occupation, or rat-proofing farm buildings. In service of this, the government Veterinary Department commissioned propaganda for circulation in the agricultural press. Davis began writing literature intended solely for farmers. Pamphlets such as “Gerbille Control” (1941) were intended entirely for farmer readers and recommended fostering the natural enemies of rodents, and continuously killing rats in the veld. Like his predecessors, Davis advocated the use of biological and chemical control of rodents. Such advice continued to be heeded by some farmers and rural dwellers in the 1940s. To name one example, in January 1940, in Harrismith, a Black father was horrified to find his baby, Gayana Mokwena, being strangled by a mole snake. This “tame” snake had been kept by the father in his hut in order to “counteract a plague of rats and mice.” After nearly an hour of “artificial respiration,” the baby recovered, and Mr. Mokwena “destroyed” all four mole snakes he had been keeping.

Yet despite this continued enthusiasm for snakes and other predators among farmers, public health officials were no longer advocating an all-out war to address an upset balance of nature. 1925–1936 was a brief period in which the appearance of a medieval disease in a rapidly modernizing agricultural milieu broke barriers between human and animal, wild and domestic, protected and pest, veld and town, primitive and modern. Population ecology repaired these barriers. Veld plague was no longer a “slumbering volcano” that could erupt and decimate cities. Gerbils could no longer be conceived as urban invaders whose entry into cities needed to be prevented or restricted: they were shy, secretive creatures who would never reach towns, and whose presence in the countryside needed simply to be accepted. As the Union of South Africa turned its attention toward killing humans in Europe and Africa at the end of 1939, the war on veld rodents slowly came to an end.

### Conclusion

An entanglement of disciplines, technologies, animals, peoples, insects, and microbes brought the concept of “veld plague” into being. The fieldwork of Roberts and Powell showed that the disease, despite sharing the Y. pestis microorganism, was conceptually different to plague in cities. Unlike this disease, which had spread like an inferno across port cities, veld plague lacked visible symptoms and had numerous reservoirs. It lived underground and smoldered across the country with occasional conflagrations. The epidemiological maps made an invisible world of rats, fleas, and Y. pestis perceptible and traceable. The combination of these maps and contemporaneous economic zoology publicity campaigns produced a new epidemiology of the disease. Veld plague spread in areas in which the “balance of nature” had been upset due to the extermination of carnivora, and these tended to be areas of intensive cultivation on the highveld. From 1925 to 1936, this produced an anxiety within the Public Health Department that veld rodents had become a competing colonial force as a result of the modernization of agriculture. The mobility of swarms of these animals who appeared to be carrying a medieval disease between human and wild animal spaces created a moment in which established boundaries between veld and town, wild and domestic rats, protected animals and vermin, primitive and modern, and human and animal were breached.

Restoring such boundaries necessitated a combination of intensive anti-rodent warfare and creating boundaries to both Black African and rodent movement between veld and town. Controlling veld plague quickly dovetailed with segregationist policies of influx control as the mobilities of Black people between native reserves and cities were pathologized as a source of rodent and flea circulation. Later, fearing that gerbils were poised to invade cities, movement controls were also imposed upon these animals through the creation of “rodent-free belts” that sought to confine them within the veld.

Despite Black critiques being launched against such racist epidemiology in widely circulated newspapers, and the colonial valorization of some Black farmers as rodent-killing experts, they were never consulted as experts in controlling pests, unlike in previous chapters. Nevertheless, African “invisible technicians”—laborers, rodent exterminators, and farm workers—did play a key role in uncovering the reservoirs of the disease, charting its extent, and combatting it. Photographic evidence, posed as it may well have been, such as the figure on page 157, suggests that Africans were at times engaged in some of the most dangerous work—digging up burrows and examining dead or caught gerbils for infected fleas.

As Fourie and Davis revealed that the allegedly porous movement of gerbils between veld and town was little more than paranoia, the epidemiology and control of veld plague changed once again. Gerbils were accepted as rural dwellers who were happy in the countryside and would never disturb white townsfolk, whereas the “self-domesticated” multimammate mouse was reframed as the major epidemiological risk. These animals, who were thought to transgress wild-domestic and veld-town borders, especially within Black urban and peri-urban areas, were violently suppressed, and vigorous attempts were made to separate their homes from those of humans. Thus, the period of taxonomic fluidity ended: damaged boundaries between veld and town, wild and domestic, primitive and modern, were repaired. In the process, an epistemic boundary was also closed: by the 1940s, all vernacular knowledge about veld rodents and veld plague—whether African or white—was dismissed as of little use to rodent settler science. The Public Health Department adopted the recommendations of Davis and Fourie, rather than continuing to pander to the panic-stricken demands of provincial bureaucrats and their constituents.

The work of Fourie, Davis, and their unnamed Black and Coloured laborers reduced an infinitely complex problem that could only be tackled by a community of citizen contributors to settler science, to a question of counting “active burrows.” Davis’s ratio of eight burrows to one gerbil enabled public health officials to forecast epidemics and thus pre-emptively fumigate at-risk properties to eliminate “domestic” rats and multimammate mice. Once reading the same pamphlets as government officials, farmers were issued popular literature. The barrier between scientific and vernacular knowledge, once as porous as the movement of rodents between the imaginary boundaries of veld and farm, was made impermeable.
