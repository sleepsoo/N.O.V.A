#### **<mark>Introduction</mark>**

The Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) and Department of Defense (DOD) Evidence-Based Practice Work Group (EBPWG) was established in 2004, with a mission to use clinical and epidemiological evidence to improve population health within the Veterans Health Administration (VHA) and Military Health System (MHS) and develop clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) for the VA and DOD populations.(1) This CPG is intended to provide healthcare providers with a practical framework by which to evaluate, treat, and manage pressure injuries, address the individual needs and preferences of patients, and improve clinical outcomes.

The VA and DOD EBPWG initiated the creation of the VA/DOD Pressure Injury Prevention and Management CPG in 2024. This CPG provides an evidence-based framework for evaluating and managing adult patients, 18 years or older, who are eligible for care in the VA and/or DOD healthcare systems and may benefit from pressure injury prevention and management.

The system-wide goal of evidence-based guidelines is to improve the patient’s health and well-being. To that end, this CPG is intended to guide providers who care for patients along management pathways supported by evidence. These guidelines were written to emphasize the role of:

- Emphasizing the use of patient-centered care using risk factors and event history;

- Minimizing preventable complications and morbidity;

- Optimizing each patient’s health outcomes and improving quality of life; and

- Assessing the patient’s condition and, in collaboration with the patient, family, and caregivers, determining optimal treatment.

#### **<mark>Scope of the CPG</mark>**

This CPG is intended for use by providers in the healthcare team in the Department of Defense, Department of Veterans Affairs healthcare systems, and healthcare team partners in the community who assess and manage patients at risk for pressure injuries.

##### **A. Populations Included in this Guideline**

This CPG is intended for adult patients aged 18 years or older with or at risk of a pressure injury who are users of the VA and DOD healthcare systems. Most of the KQs focus on this overall clinical population which includes, but is not limited to, patients with spinal cord injury (SCI), burns, limited mobility, frailty, dementia, those with a lifetime limiting illness, or who are in an extended care, acute care, an intensive care unit (ICU), medical unit, surgical unit, or the operating room (OR) setting.

#### **<mark>Recommendations</mark>**

The following recommendations were made using a systematic approach considering four domains as per the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach as detailed in the section on Methods and Appendix A in the full 2026 VA/DOD for the Prevention and Management of Pressure Injuries CPG. These domains include confidence in the quality of the evidence, balance of desirable and undesirable outcomes (i.e., benefits and harms), patient or provider values and preferences, and other implications as appropriate (e.g., resource use, equity, acceptability, feasibility, subgroup considerations).

##### **A. Key Highlights**

This document is a new VA/DOD Clinical Practice Guideline (CPG) for the Prevention and Management of Pressure Injuries. This CPG provides recommendations for healthcare providers on the assessment, prevention, and management of pressure injuries across a variety of healthcare and community settings. This CPG recognizes the significant impact of pressure injuries on patient health, quality of life, healthcare utilization, and outcomes. The recommendations are based on the most current and clinically relevant scientific evidence available.

A major strength of this CPG is the collaboration and development by members of the interprofessional CPG Development Work Group, ensuring broad representation from clinicians, researchers, subject matter experts, and healthcare administrators involved in pressure injury prevention and management.

Highlights of this CPG include the following:

- The Work Group developed a total of 29 evidence-based recommendations, including 1 strong recommendation, 14 weak recommendations, and 14 insufficient recommendations focused on reducing the incidence and severity of pressure injuries and improving patient outcomes. The recommendation topics include:

 - Risk Assessment and Early Diagnosis;

 - Pressure Injury Prevention

 - Patient-Centered Prevention Programs and Education

 - Early Mobilization and Repositioning Strategies

 - Support Surfaces and Pressure Redistribution

 - Dressings and Heel Offloading Devices

 - Prevention Technologies and Monitoring Interventions

 - Pressure Injury Management

 - Pressure Relieving Surfaces

 - Pressure Injury Management Devices

 - Surgery

 - Biologics

 - Virtual Care and Remote Interventions

The Work Group found the following evidence-based practices are important in prevention and management of pressure injuries,

- Use of a validated risk assessment tool.

- Implementation of patient-centered prevention programs.

- Education for healthcare teams, families, and caregivers.

- Thermography or sub-epidermal moisture devices for risk assessment and early diagnosis.

- Implementation of early mobilization for appropriate patients.

- Use of sacral foam and heel offloading devices for PI prevention

- If assessed nutrient needs cannot be achieved through food intake alone, use oral nutrition supplements that combine energy, protein, and micronutrients to support the healing process of PI.

- Encourage smoking cessation for the reduction of pressure injury size and wound improvement.

The Work Group also found evidence to suggest against the routine use of prone posturing for PI prevention.

In addition, the Work Group reviewed evidence but found it insufficient to make recommendations for repositioning reminder systems, specific timing or position of head tilt, and continuous body pressure mapping to prevent PI. There was also insufficient evidence on use of reactive air surfaces, foam surfaces, gel surfaces, and alternating pressure surfaces for management of PIs. The Work Group felt that these were fertile areas for future non-vendor sponsored research.

The Work Group adhered to the Guideline for Guidelines and rigorously applied the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) methodology.

#### **<mark>Wound Care Teams</mark>**

##### _Purpose_

The wound care team provides a multidisciplinary approach to optimize prevention and treatment of pressure injuries. Team activity includes early identification of patients at risk for developing an alteration in skin integrity, promoting healing, preventing infection, minimizing complications in acute and chronic wounds, providing patient education, and improving quality of life.

#### **_A. Wound Care Service (WCS)_**

1. Implement a person-centered pressure injury prevention and management program using an interprofessional approach.

2. Perform wound and skin care assessment as requested by any clinician for high-risk patients, any suspected pressure injury, open wounds, or skin damage. Stage and document all confirmed pressure injuries in the patient's medical record using the EPUAP/NPIAP.

 - Perform a head-to-toe skin assessment at the initial consultation for all patients at high risk for skin breakdown.

 - Stage and document observed pressure injuries, including if present on admission (POA) or hospital-acquired pressure injury (HAPI), in the Federal Electronic Health Record (FEHR).

 - Enter specific prevention and wound care orders in the FEHR.

3. Coordinate ongoing wound treatment as patients navigate different healthcare settings and providers to ensure continuity of care to improve patient outcomes, experience, and costeffectiveness for a more efficient patient-centered healthcare system.

4. Facilitate the implementation of the pressure injury and skin integrity management and treatment procedure through consultation and staff and patient education to enhance their knowledge, increase participation in care, and reduce the risk of future complications.

#### **<mark>Community Factors</mark>**

Literature indicates that the development of community-acquired pressure injuries (CAPIs) is associated with a complex interplay of factors, but there remains a lack of understanding of the components and outcomes associated with effective care in the community.(3) Despite a majority of pressure injuries occurring in the community, research efforts to prevent pressure injuries focus on acute care settings. The prevalence of CAPIs and their associated risk factors is well-documented. Key patient characteristics such as immobility, comorbid conditions, malnutrition, and advanced age are associated with increased pressure injuries in the community.(4)

Increased incidence of pressure injury can occur when there are gaps in education for patients and caregivers regarding the mechanism of skin breakdown and the consequences of pressure injuries. Education is needed for appropriate mobility techniques, proper equipment, and pressure injury management. Taking time to understand a patient’s individual needs and available resources can also assist in reducing the incidence of pressure injuries. It is also important for providers and the healthcare team to understand patient challenges and resources available in preventing pressure injuries so that perspectives and expectations are aligned.(5) Lack of caregiver support, mobility support, access to appropriate equipment, or a history of a previous pressure injury can further increase the risk of pressure injuries in the community.(3)

Despite limited research, evidence suggests that lower socioeconomic status (SES) may also be a significant risk factor for CAPIs, primarily attributed to the increased risk associated with lower household income and inadequate healthcare access.(4,6) Access to timely and adequate healthcare is critical in the prevention and management of pressure injuries. Communities with limited healthcare facilities or those experiencing healthcare disparities often face higher rates of pressure injuries.(7)

#### **<mark>Pressure Injury Prevention</mark>**

##### **A. Pressure Injury Assessment**

##### _Risk Assessment_

1. Complete a risk assessment to determine a patient’s risk of developing a pressure injury using a validated risk assessment tool (e.g., Braden Scale). The overall risk assessment will be documented in the health record. Assess the risk score per policy.

2. The following risk factors place patients at higher risk for pressure injuries because they impair oxygenation, circulation, and mobility:

- i. Use of vasopressors

- ii. Incontinent of bowel and bladder

- iii. Limited self-mobility

- iv. Advanced Age

- v. Diabetes

- vi. Prior recent hospital stays

- vii. Shock/sepsis

- viii. Recent cardiac arrest

- ix. History of pressure injuries

- x. Prolonged surgery or procedure time

- xi. Spinal Cord Injury

- xii. Stroke/paralysis

- xiii. Obesity

- xiv. Malnourished or at risk for malnutrition

- xv. Poor perfusion

- xvi. Mechanical ventilation

- xvii. Medical devices

- xviii. Dark skin tone

- xix. Chemical paralysis (e.g., sedation, nerve block)

- xx. Prone position

- xxi. Poor perfusion

- xxii. Neuropathy

###### _Skin Inspection_

Skin inspection is a formal evaluation of the patient’s skin, front to back, head to toe, for signs of erythema, induration, fluctuance, rash, ulceration, erosion, actual wound, or localized heat, especially in individuals with darkly pigmented skin, where subtle color changes are more difficult to detect. Attention should be directed to the skin over bony prominences and under medical devices.

1. Observe skin over bony prominences, such as the sacrum, occiput, heels, scapula, elbows, and ischial tuberosities.

2. Ask about or observe for pain or discomfort that could indicate or be related to pressure-related injuries.

3. Monitor areas of pressure caused by medical devices (i.e., catheters, oxygen

 - tubing, tracheostomy ties, prostheses, orthopedic boots, etc.). Move/remove devices to fully assess skin. Contact the provider as needed to confirm if a device can be moved/removed for skin inspection.

4. As applicable, repeat as defined by policy, state law, and/or directive.

_Pressure, Friction, and Shear Reduction_

1. Pressure injury risk is addressed with appropriate, well-established interventions: i. We suggest repositioning “at-risk” patients utilizing clinical judgment. If the patient is not responding as expected to the repositioning regimen, reassess repositioning.

- ii. When turning patients, consider individualized pressure-relieving plans and modalities.

- iii. Shear forces and friction increase the risk of pressure injuries; implement strategies to reduce both friction and shearing occurrences (e.g., head of bed elevation, transfer devices, etc.).

- iv. Use protective dressings over bony prominences to reduce shear and friction injuries. v. If able, encourage chair-bound patients to reposition, shifting the points under pressure every 15 minutes for 1-2 mins.

- vi. When selecting the most appropriate support surface or specialty mattress, take into consideration factors such as the patient’s level of mobility within the bed, comfort, and the need for microclimate control.

- vii. Ensure patients are placed on the appropriate support surface for their respective size (e.g., varying weight, varying height, etc.).

###### _Moisture Management_

Moisture-associated skin damage increases the risk for pressure injury development in high-risk environments; address it according to facility-specific protocols/recommendations.

1. Cleanse and apply moisture barriers after each incontinence episode

2. Consider urinary or fecal management systems for high-risk patients

3. Use high-absorbency pads for incontinence

4. Apply moisture-wicking material to skin folds and under moisture-contributing medical devices (e.g., trach ties) as appropriate

###### _Prone Positioning_

Special circumstances will require additional pressure injury prevention measures. Patients requiring manual or mechanical pronation require protective skin care, frequent skin assessments, positioning precautions, and additional management of medical devices for pressure injury prevention.

While the routine use of prone positioning is not recommended for the prevention of pressure injuries, there are special circumstances that require the use of prone positioning (e.g., adult respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), surgery).(8)

1. Perform skin inspection, assessment, and care at regular intervals per policy.

 - i. Inspect skin at the face, thorax, clavicles, breast region, iliac crest, symphysis pubis, genitalia, knees, tibial plateau, and toes

 - ii. Increase awareness for those with a dark skin tone

2. Support Surfaces & Positioning

 - i. Use pillows under the chest, pelvis, and shins to offload pressure and protect joints

 - ii. Use facial off-loading device(s) to reduce pressure on eyes, ears, and ET tube (as applicable)

3. Skin & Face Care

 - i. Reposition head regularly per local policy

 - ii. Keep skin clean/dry and use preventive dressings on pressure points

 - iii. Protect eyes (lubricants/tape) and mouth (bite block)

4. Medical Devices

 - i. Remove and avoid placing patients on unnecessary devices

 - ii. Reposition/secure devices away from pressure areas

 - iii. Follow manufacturer guidance and reassess ET tube positioning and securement frequently

###### _Optimize Nutrition and Hydration_

1. Upon admission, all patients should be assessed for malnutrition using a validated screening tool (e.g., Malnutrition Screen Tool)

2. Assess nutritional status, hydration status, and the patient’s ability to eat independently on admission, transfer, and during every shift to assess factors that compromise a patient's dietary intake.

3. Support adequate intake of food and fluids through encouragement and assistance as needed.

4. Document food and fluid intake or deviations when appropriate (e.g., document Intake & Output, percentage of foods and beverages consumed, rate of enteral and/or parenteral nutrition, and when the goal rate is not met.)

###### _Patient Handoff and Communication_

1. Pressure injury assessment is recommended to be performed by a healthcare professional upon admission, intake, or when transferred from one healthcare site to another (e.g., ward, unit).

###### _Patient and Family Education_

1. Educate patients and their families on the prevention of pressure injuries for at-risk patients

throughout hospital admission and prior to discharge.

- i. Document education provided in the Federal EHR.

- ii. Discharge teaching:

 - The nurse discharging the patient will review home care for pressure injuries with the patient/family. When possible, use various delivery methods (e.g., verbal, telephone, written, video) to support different learning styles.

 - Provide appropriate resources (e.g., handouts, internet resources) in preventing pressure injuries to include: Etiology of pressure injuries, including how pressure injuries develop, nutrition and hydration, pressure re-distribution strategies, hygiene and continence management, healthy lifestyle choices (e.g., exercise appropriate to co-morbidities, tobacco cessation).

viii. Pain: presence or absence

7. Pressure injuries are always classified by their original stage, i.e., a Stage 4 pressure injury that now involves the epidermis and dermis is classified as a healing Stage 4, not a Stage 2.

 - i. Pressure injuries can worsen and progress to a higher stage (i.e., from a Stage 2 to a Stage 3).

 - ii. Deep tissue pressure injuries (DTPIs) may resolve or progress into a different stage of injury.

 - iii. Unstageable pressure injuries can be further staged once the true depth of the injury can be determined.

 - iv. Mucosal pressure injuries are described but not staged according to standard staging criteria.

11. Continue ongoing assessments at designated intervals per local policy.

#### **<mark>Clinical Evaluation</mark>**

Clinical evaluation of a pressure injury involves not only a comprehensive assessment of a patient’s current skin and wound attributes but also an overall detailed review of patient-specific characteristics that put the patient at risk for delayed healing of any current pressure injury and for the development of new pressure injuries. This broad baseline cataloging of both pressure injury and patient parameters is a critical first step in allowing a clinician to formulate a treatment plan.

The evaluation process begins with documenting the wound type and location, including findings noted on visual inspection, such as size (length, width, depth) and stage according to the NPIAP Pressure Injury Staging System.(8) Providers should document the presence of eschar, slough, or granulation tissue; the extent of epithelialization, nature of wound margins, exudate type and amount; and notable aspects of peri-wound skin, including degree of erythema, warmth, induration, and any signs of infection.(9,10) Wound palpation and probing may be necessary to rule out the existence of sinus tracts, tunneling, or crepitus. Written documentation should include detailed measurements of the pressure injury and clockface references for any noted tunneling or undermining. Although not all clinic settings may have the resources to do so easily, official photographic documentation at each visit is recommended for monitoring progression.(10) In addition to noting physical characteristics of the pressure injury, clinicians should evaluate and document pain, malodor, and any signs of local or systemic infection, with attention to potential complications like underlying bony involvement.(9,11)

Risk factors for worsening of existing pressure-related injuries and for the development of new pressure injuries are another facet of any clinical evaluation. Nutritional status, mobility, comorbidities, and devicerelated pressure should be considered and mitigated where possible. Ongoing clinical evaluations should be repeated regularly to monitor healing or deterioration.(12) The ACP notes that while risk assessment scales (e.g., Braden, Norton, Waterlow, Cubbin, and Jackson) can help predict risk, their sensitivity and specificity are low, and their diagnostic accuracy does not differ substantially from clinical judgment alone. The choice between using a formal tool or clinical gestalt should be individualized, with tools being particularly useful for clinicians

#### **<mark>Nutrition Screening</mark>**

Malnutrition screening tools are meant to identify the likelihood that someone is at risk for malnutrition. A positive screen indicates the need for a comprehensive nutrition assessment by a registered dietitian. Findings from this assessment are then applied to a standardized diagnostic framework to determine the presence and severity of malnutrition, which then guides the development of an individualized nutrition care plan. Several validated frameworks are available; the AAIM (Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics and American Society for Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition Indicators for Malnutrition) has demonstrated

criterion validity compared with the subjective global assessment (SGA) as well as predictive validity for patient outcomes, including mortality, hospital length of stay, and 30-day readmissions.

The two often work hand in hand. A malnutrition screening tool can be used to identify whether a person is at risk for malnutrition. The dietitian can use the AAIM malnutrition criteria to confirm whether or not this person has malnutrition.

##### **A. Malnutrition in Older Adults**

Malnutrition is a significant problem among the older adult population. Unfortunately, older adults have less muscle and are at a higher risk for malnutrition than their younger counterparts. Factors that contribute to malnutrition in older adults include unintended weight loss, disease and illness, depression, medications, changes in taste, social isolation, and being unable to prepare meals or feed themselves.

It is estimated that 12-50% of older adults in the hospital are malnourished. And 23-60% of older adults living in long-term care settings (13) are malnourished. The challenge in addressing malnutrition in older adults is that it affects individuals beyond those experiencing hunger or limited food access.(8) Any older adult can develop malnutrition. Up to 1 in 2 older adults are at risk for malnutrition.

##### **B. Why it Matters**

Malnutrition can increase the risk of falls, hospitalization, loss of independence, impaired skin integrity, and early death. It has a significantly negative effect on the quality of life. Malnutrition places significant financial burdens on older adults and the healthcare system. It is estimated that malnutrition in older adults costs the United States $51.3 billion per year.

Early identification and intervention are essential for preventing malnutrition. Routine malnutrition screening enables practitioners to identify adults at risk, and because malnutrition can affect individuals regardless of background or circumstance, all adults should be screened.

##### **C. Common Malnutrition Screening Tools**

A malnutrition screening tool can be used in any medical setting or in the home and can be completed by anyone

Three common malnutrition screening tools are validated for accuracy and reliability:

###### **a. Malnutrition Screening Tool**

The focus of this tool is on unintended weight loss (14) and appetite. The malnutrition screening tool is simple, short, and to the point. While it is not just for older adults, research has shown that it is a strong and reliable screening tool regardless of age.(15) This tool is published through Abbott Nutrition.(16) This tool and involves asking two questions: Have you lost weight without trying (if so, how much), and have you been eating less because of poor appetite?

###### **b. Mini Nutrition Assessment (MNA)**

The Mini Nutrition Assessment has both a long and short form version. The short form has been shown to be as reliable as the long form. Therefore, the short form will be referenced in this document. This tool is published through Nestlé Nutrition Institute. It was developed specifically for older adults aged 65 and older. The MNA Short form includes 6 questions. These questions assess decline in food intake, weight loss, mobility, illness, mental health, and body mass index (BMI).

This tool is well known and used by geriatric dietitians as a go-to for screening older adults for malnutrition.

###### **c. The DETERMINE Checklist**

The DETERMINE Your Nutritional Health Nutrition Screening Initiative (NSI) is also known as the DETERMINE Checklist. This tool is more often used in community nutrition settings (often programs under the Older Americans Act).

#### **<mark>Routine Pressure Injury Care</mark>**

Many of the commonly utilized topical treatments for pressure injuries have not been well studied specifically for use in pressure injuries. Treatments that are well studied for other types of wounds have been utilized by clinicians based on this evidence and clinical understanding of correlative physiology. Choice of treatment is driven by the clinical characteristics of the wound being treated and the patient. For these reasons, defining routine or usual standard pressure injury topical care proved challenging when analyzing the literature. Given the unique properties of each wound, comparator effectiveness with active comparators studies were generally avoided when exploring topical treatments.(17-19)

Moisture balance, bacterial balance, and debridement are fundamental principles of chronic wound healing. The “TIME” framework published in 2003 (17) and updated in 2012 (18) and augmented in 2019 to “TIMERS” models these principles when providing care topically to wounds. Because pressure injuries are wounds, the “T”IME framework can be applied to pressure injuries. “T”issue reminds the clinician to

free the wound bed of nonviable tissue, dressing, and cellular debris. All chronic wounds should be cleansed to loosen nonviable tissue and remove excess dressing matter. There is little data to support one cleanser over another; instead, the clinician should consider an antimicrobial cleanser if there is evidence of increasing bacterial burden. Cleansers include potable water, sterile saline, surfactants, superoxidized solutions (e.g., hypochlorous acid), and antiseptics (e.g., povidone-iodine). After cleansing, the clinician chooses dressing or techniques that foster autolytic, enzymatic, biologic (larval), sharp, or surgical debridement if appropriate. Wet-to-dry, a common form of mechanical debridement, has no place in chronic wound healing. This form of debridement desiccates the wound bed, indiscriminately debrides dead and healthy tissue, and is painful to the patient. “I”nfection/”I”nflammation directs the clinician to decide whether a topical antimicrobial is necessary. “Covert” signs of local wound infection may be hypergranulation, bleeding, epithelial bridging and pocketing in granulation tissue, worsening exudate, and delayed wound healing.(19) Composite dressings, ones that combine moist wound healing properties with antimicrobials, may contain silver, gentian violet, iodine, or medical-grade honey to counteract infection. Best practice recommends that these antimicrobials not be used for too long, as some may be cytotoxic. Consider the wound appearance, the patient’s immune system, and the patient’s hygiene resources with the use of these antimicrobials. “M”oisture balance dictates that the chronic wound not be desiccated, as this will prevent keratinocyte migration, but also not be so wet that the edge breaks down and the wound becomes infected. Hydrogels, hydrocolloids, and petrolatum-impregnated gauze all contribute to a moist environment. While all of the above can foster autolysis, hydrocolloids in particular are efficient autolytic debriders. Moisture-wicking dressings include alginates, hydrofiber dressings, and foams. “E”dge is often overlooked in the component of wound healing in the “TIME” framework. The keratinocytes migrate from the wound edge to foster wound closure. The edge must be protected from excessive moisture and occasionally sharply debrided to help that advancement. “R”epair/regeneration fosters wound closure via advanced therapies. Examples include topical oxygen, growth factors, negative pressure wound therapy, and cellular, acellular, or matrix-like products. The matrix-like products stimulate cell activity and should only be used after appropriate wound bed preparation (i.e., adequate debridement). Because of the high cost of these therapies, careful consideration of patient adherence to the underlying care plan should also be taken into account. “S”ocial-patient related factors acknowledge the importance of the patient in the midst of this difficult disease process. The clinician engages the patient, including determining their level of understanding, in caring for a wound that may be hard-to-heal.

As outlined above, many treatments are routinely used in the topical care of pressure injuries based on the application of evidence from other wound types and clinical theory, with success. Analysis of active comparators in the literature is challenging based on the unique characteristics of each patient and wound. A comprehensive understanding of the anatomic and physiologic changes contributing to the wound is crucial to the appropriate selection and application of treatments. The above-outlined routine pressure injury topical care options are widely accepted, and treatments should be chosen as clinically appropriate in each situation.

#### **<mark>Methods</mark>**

This is a new VA/DOD evidence-based CPG. The methodology used in developing this CPG follows the Guideline for Guidelines, an internal document of the VA/DOD EBPWG updated in January 2026 that outlines procedures for developing and submitting VA/DOD CPGs.(20) The Guideline for Guidelines is available at http://www.healthquality.va.gov/policy/index.asp. This CPG also aligns with the National Academy of Medicine’s (NAM) principles of trustworthy CPGs (e.g., explanation of evidence quality and strength, management of potential conflicts of interest [COI], interdisciplinary stakeholder involvement, use of SR and external review).(21) **Appendix A** provides a detailed description of the CPG development methodology.

#### **<mark>Patient-Centered Care</mark>**

VA and DOD encourage providers to be sensitive to demographic, cultural, and other differences that affect patients’ values, needs, and preferences, aimed at treating the condition while also optimizing the individual’s overall health and well-being. Regardless of the care setting, all patients should have access to individualized evidence-based care. Patient-centered care can decrease patient anxiety, increase trust in providers, and improve treatment adherence.(22,23) A whole health approach (https:/www.va.gov/wholehealth/) empowers and equips individuals to meet their personal health and well- being goals. Clear communication is essential and should be supported by evidence-based information tailored to each patient’s needs. Guideline recommendations should be applied in a holistic approach to care that is patient-centered, culturally appropriate, and available to people with limited literacy skills and physical, sensory, or learning disabilities.

#### **<mark>Shared Decision-Making</mark>**

This CPG encourages providers to practice shared decision-making, a process in which providers, patients, and patient care partners (e.g., family, friends, caregivers) consider clinical evidence of benefits and risks as well as patient values and preferences to make decisions regarding the patient’s treatment.(24) Shared decision-making is emphasized in “Crossing the Quality Chasm,” an Institute of Medicine (now National Academy of Medicine [NAM]) report, in 2001 (25) and is a core component of a patient-centered, whole health approach. Providers must be adept at presenting information to their patients regarding individual treatments, expected risks, possible outcomes, and levels and/or settings of care, especially where patient heterogeneity in weighing risks and benefits might exist. The VA and DOD have embraced shared decision-making. Providers are encouraged to use shared decision-making to individualize treatment goals and plans based on patient capabilities, needs, values, and preferences.
