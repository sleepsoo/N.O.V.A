#### **QUALIFYING STATEMENTS**

The Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) and the Department of Defense (DOD) guidelines are based on the best information available at the time of publication. The guidelines are designed to provide information and assist decision-making. They are not intended to define a standard of care and should not be construed as such, nor should the guidelines be interpreted as prescribing an exclusive course of management.

This clinical practice guideline (CPG) is based on a systematic review of both clinical and epidemiological evidence. Developed by a panel of multidisciplinary experts, it provides a clear explanation of the logical relationships between various care options and health outcomes while rating both the quality of the evidence and the strength of the recommendation.

Variations in practice will inevitably and appropriately occur when providers consider the needs of individual patients, available resources, and limitations unique to an institution or type of practice. Therefore, every health care professional using these guidelines is responsible for evaluating the appropriateness of applying them in each unique clinical situation using a patient-centered approach.

These guidelines are not intended to represent VA or DOD policies. Further, inclusion of recommendations for specific testing, therapeutic interventions, or both, within these guidelines does not guarantee coverage of civilian sector care.

# **I. Introduction**

The Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) and Department of Defense (DOD) Evidence-Based Practice Work Group (EBPWG) was established in 2004, with a mission to use clinical and epidemiological evidence to improve population health within the Veterans Health Administration (VHA) and Military Health System (MHS) and develop clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) for the VA and DOD populations.(1) The development and updating of VA/DOD CPGs is funded by VA Evidence Based Practice, Office of Quality and Patient Safety. The system-wide goal of evidence-based guidelines, including this CPG, is to guide providers in non-surgical osteoarthritis management in addition to offering management pathways supported by evidence. This CPG is intended to provide health care providers with a practical framework by which to evaluate, treat, and manage osteoarthritis, address the individual needs and preferences of patients, and improve clinical outcomes.

In 2014, the VA and DOD published a CPG for the Non-Surgical Management of Hip & Knee Osteoarthritis (2014 VA/DOD OA CPG), which was based on evidence reviewed from January 2002 through December 2012. This was updated in 2020 using the same process, based on evidence reviewed through June 3, 2019 (2020 VA/DOD OA CPG). Since then, a growing body of research has expanded the general knowledge and understanding of the impact of non-surgical management interventions on hip and knee osteoarthritis. Consequently, a recommendation to update the 2020 OA CPG was initiated in April 2025 using published data from June 3, 2019, to July 31, 2025. This updated CPG’s use of the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach reflects a more rigorous application of the methodology than previous iterations.(2) Therefore, the strength of some recommendations might have been modified because of changes in the quality of the supporting evidence (see Evidence Quality and Recommendation Strength).

This CPG provides an evidence-based framework for evaluating and managing adults, 18 years or older, with osteoarthritis of the hip or knee who are eligible for care in the VA or DOD systems, including community care. These guidelines were written to emphasize the role of:

- Utilizing patient-centered care;

- Decreasing preventable complications and morbidity;

- Improving health outcomes and quality of life (QoL); and

- Using shared decision-making frameworks in patient collaboration efforts.

# **II. Background**

## **A. Epidemiology and Cost of Osteoarthritis**

Osteoarthritis (OA) is one of the most common chronic conditions worldwide and in the United States (U.S.). According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), OA affects more than 30 million Americans (3) with prevalence estimates of 13.9% of adults age 25 years and older, and 33.6% of adults age 65 years and older.(4) However, the prevalence and incidence rates vary across studies depending on the definition of OA (e.g., radiographic, symptomatic, selfreported, or provider-diagnosed) and study setting (e.g., clinical or population-based). The epidemiology of OA also differs by sex, age distribution, body weight, and race. Osteoarthritis is

more common in women, with worldwide estimates of symptomatic OA of 18% in women and 9.6% in men over 60 years of age.(5) OA increases after 50 years of age, the knee is the most common site, and studies generally indicate that OA is more common in Blacks compared to Whites.(6)

It is estimated that, at large, one in two patients may develop symptomatic knee OA (KOA) in their lifetime,(7) and one in four may develop symptomatic hip OA (HOA) in their lifetime.(8) Obesity increases the lifetime risk of OA significantly; two out of three people with obesity will develop OA.(9) By 2040, the number of U.S. adults with provider-diagnosed arthritis is projected to increase 49% to 78.4 million (25.9% of all adults), and the number of adults with arthritisattributable activity limitation will increase 52% to 34.6 million (11.4% of all adults).(10)

OA is more common in military Service Members and Veterans than in the general population. A 2013 CDC study estimated that 25.6% of Veterans have arthritis and incidence rates increase with age.(11) One study found that OA was more common in Veterans compared to non-veterans with a prevalence of 25.6% and 23.6%, respectively. In another study, the overall incidence rate for physician-diagnosed OA in Active-Duty Service Members was 7.9 cases per 1,000 personyears (95% confidence interval [CI]: 7.82-7.91).(12) The adjusted incidence rate for OA was approximately 20% higher in women Service Members compared to men (relative risk [RR]: 1.19, 95% CI: 1.17-1.21) and age was a significant risk factor. Between 1999 and 2008 there was a 19fold higher incidence of OA in Active-Duty Service Members greater than 40 years of age compared to those less than 20 years of age (no specific military-only forecast exists in the recent published data).(12)

Active-Duty Service Members regularly experience repetitive joint loading during physical activity and military tasks and activities. Military-service-related overuse, as well as traumatic and nontraumatic joint injuries, are contributing factors to an increased risk of developing osteoarthritis.(13) Military rank and branch of military service may also correlate with variation in the incidence of OA. For example, junior and senior enlisted Service Members and those serving in the Army experienced the highest incidence rates for OA.(12) Additionally, Service Members tend to develop OA at an earlier age with a recent article reporting that one in six military officers in the U.S. who suffered a knee injury developed OA by the age of 30.(14)

The large use of healthcare resources to manage OA contributes significantly to healthcare costs.(15-17) In fact, the medical costs related to OA account for an estimated 1-2.5% of the gross domestic product of high-income countries.(18) Overall, the economic burden of OA and related conditions exceeded an estimated $340 billion annually in direct healthcare costs. The combination of direct and indirect costs for OA and related disorders is estimated to be $461 billion.(19) Hip and knee joint replacements represent a major portion of these costs.(18) The indirect costs associated with OA greatly surpass healthcare costs and exert significant personal costs for people with OA due to work loss and premature retirement, leading to loss of income and subsequent reductions in personal savings.(18)

Osteoarthritis is associated with substantial disability, with epidemiologic data indicating that up to 80% of affected individuals experience limitations in movement and nearly 25% have difficulty performing activities of daily living.(5,20,21) Hip and knee OA are leading causes of disability worldwide accounting for 2.4% of all years lived with disability (YLDs), an overall measure of disease burden.(22) From 1990-2016, OA was in the top 20 contributors to YLDs and ranked

second to diabetes as the condition associated with the most rapid increases in disability, with a 46% increase in YLDs.(23) By 2020, OA is expected to be the fourth leading cause of YLDs globally.(18) OA is second to back pain as the most common cause of military separation, and it leads to significant disability during peacetime and war.(24)

OA has negative impacts on physical health that may also influence a patient's QoL and mental health.(25) Depression and catastrophizing commonly affect patients with OA.(26,27) Sleep disturbances were found in 72% of adults >55 years with OA.(28) Additional OA-related sequelae can include muscle weakness, instability, and balance problems that can lead to falls and development of other comorbid musculoskeletal conditions as musculoskeletal conditions often coexist (e.g., fibromyalgia).(29)

## **B. Disease Course, Clinical Presentation, and Risk Factors for Osteoarthritis**

OA of the hip and knee represents a chronic disease with a slow, progressive course. OA becomes more common with age and is diagnosed at a median age of 55 years.(30) Unilateral OA may progress to bilateral OA over time, and bilateral joint involvement is more common in patients with advanced OA.(31) HOA is frequently unilateral.

Patients with OA of the hip and knee may present with minimal symptoms or incidental findings on physical examination (bony enlargement, crepitus, joint swelling, or decreased range of motion) or radiographic abnormalities (osteophyte formation or joint space narrowing). Patients with symptomatic KOA usually present with knee pain and stiffness. Thigh muscle wasting and bony enlargement may occur with severe OA. Inflammation is usually absent or only modest, although small to medium-sized effusions are common at the knee.(32) Patients with

HOA frequently present with pain in the groin that can radiate to the anterior thigh or buttock.(33)

Typically, joint pain in the hip or knee is exacerbated by activity and relieved by rest.(34) Over time, OA of the hip and knee can progress to severe, disabling pain that can occur at rest. When the pain becomes severe, it can interfere with sleep and limit a patient’s ability to walk, stand for prolonged periods, or climb stairs.(35) Joint stiffness is usually brief (less than 30 minutes) and occurs in the morning or after periods of inactivity.

Risk factors for OA of the hip and knee can be categorized as patient- or joint-related factors. Patient-related risk factors include older age, female sex, obesity, genetic predisposition, and engagement in work or recreational activities that involve repetitive joint loading over time.(36,37) Obesity is a strong, modifiable risk factor, especially for KOA.(37) Physical training, military tasks, and activities or work that requires frequent kneeling, heavy lifting, or heavy physical loads on joints are associated with OA development.(38,39) Studies also show a strong association between high-impact sports (e.g., football, hockey) and the development of OA.(40,41) Notably, there was a dose-response relationship with strenuous sports and risk of OA. Only participants in the highest tertile of participation in strenuous sports or exercise had an increased risk for OA.(42)

Joint-related factors also contribute to the development of OA. These factors include prior joint injury (e.g., ligament or meniscal damage, trauma), joint malalignment or deformities, joint laxity, increased bone density, and abnormal joint loading.(43,44) Less well-defined potential risk factors for OA development include knee extensor muscle weakness and smoking.(45)

## **C. Prevention Strategies for Osteoarthritis**

Preventing OA has been a rich area of research, though efficacy has been variable or limited. Several reviews have found little evidence for preventing OA (46-50) despite some basic science trials showing promise.(46)

While obesity is a modifiable risk factor for developing OA, there is inconsistency in the literature exploring the efficacy of weight loss in preventing OA. Mechanistically, losing 1 pound (lb) can reduce load on the knee by 4lbs,(51) and, empirically, the Framingham study found that a decrease in Body Mass Index (BMI) ≥2 reduces one’s risk for OA by >50%.(52) Similar to the Framingham study, one cohort of nearly 800 women found that a 5.1 kilogram (kg) weight loss reduced the risk of radiographic progression of KOA by >50% over 10 years.(53) However, most literature exploring the effects of weight loss on OA assesses symptom reduction, not radiographic changes,(54) and there’s been no direct evidence showing that weight loss prevents OA.(49)

Considering the impact of physical activity on OA development, one must consider the volume and intensity of exertion. A United Kingdom community-based cohort study tracking participants without OA at baseline over an average of 11 years found increased risk for developing OA in participants active in strenuous sports (Hazard Ratio [HR] 1.34), in physically demanding jobs (HR 1.25), and with jobs requiring mainly walking or standing (HR 1.23). In contrast, walking for pleasure (HR 0.93) and walking to work (HR 0.91) predicted a lower risk of developing OA.(42) Similarly, a randomized controlled trial (RCT) assessing a combination of exercise, diet, and glucosamine on OA prevention in obese women did not find a significantly lower risk of OA development over 2.5 years.(55) Indirect evidence, such as the association of quadriceps atrophy and KOA suggests that exercise may be protective against OA by maintaining normal joint function and preventing maladaptive joint loading.(56)

Focusing on diet, data is limited in the context of OA prevention. One prospective cohort of the Osteoarthritis Initiative found that a Western diet was associated with radiographic and symptomatic progression of OA at 4 years of follow up.(57) A systematic review (SR) of the Mediterranean diet and OA found that higher adherence to the Mediterranean diet was associated with lower prevalence of KOA.(58) In contrast, another systematic review found that dietary interventions were not effective in the prevention of KOA.(59) This variability may be due to heterogeneity in the diets themselves or in the methodology and outcome measures of these studies. Diet remains an area of interest in OA prevention given its role in weight management.

Nutraceuticals have gained interest as potential disease modifying osteoarthritis drugs (DMOADs). Curcumin has shown some anti-inflammatory properties in in-vitro studies, but in vivo studies are lacking.(60-62) Similarly, polyunsaturated fatty acids have shown some success in reducing inflammation in vitro and reducing OA in rats, but not in humans.(63) Chondroitin may reduce joint space loss by 0.28 millimeter (mm) at best but doesn’t consistently reduce pain more than placebo.(64-67) Glucosamine has been reported to reduce joint space loss by at most 0.51mm.(66,68,69) However, reductions in joint space loss have not consistently been shown to correlate with clinically significant improvements in function or pain scores.(66,68,69) Hyaluronic acid (HA) also showed mixed efficacy with just one study showing <3% difference in rate of knee cartilage loss (70) and no significant change in other trials.(71,72) Studies exploring non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAIDs),(66,72-74) vitamin D,(75) and vitamin E (75,76) failed to show

effect in OA prevention. One review exploring nutraceuticals as prevention for OA failed to find any studies meeting their inclusion criteria for S-adenosylmethionine, methylsulfonylmethane (MSM), vitamin C, or platelet-rich plasma (PRP).(77)

Considering avenues for future research, Losina et al. (2014) created a cost-efficacy model for DMOADs suggesting that a candidate DMOAD would need to cost less than $3000 per year to be cost-effective assuming 40% efficacy, 5% failure rate per year, and an increase in 0.04 qualityadjusted life year (QALYs).(78) Messier et al. (2024) have started a phase III assessor-blind 48 month RCT exploring the effects of weight loss, exercise, and weight-loss maintenance on OA progression with radiographic progression on Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) as the primary endpoint in women at risk for OA but who have not yet developed OA.(79) The approach of targeting “pre-OA” has been suggested by others who have failed to find efficacy in OA prevention.(80)

## **D. Osteoarthritis Force Readiness**

Osteoarthritis is a common cause of disability in military populations and remains an important contributor to duty limitation and medical separation. Active-Duty Service Members face unique OA risks related to repetitive joint loading, occupational demands, traumatic joint injury, and posttraumatic osteoarthritis.(13,24) Consistent with the Defense Health Agency’s role as a Combat Support Agency, effective OA management supports warfighter medical readiness by preserving function, reducing duty-limiting pain, and facilitating return to mission-capable status.(81) These guidelines emphasize early identification, structured exercise-based therapy, physical therapy, weight management when appropriate, and shared decision-making to improve function and reduce unnecessary invasive interventions.(82-85) Timely, evidence-based conservative care helps sustain deployability, force generation, and operational readiness across the Joint Force.(81-85)

# **III. Scope of This Guideline**

This CPG is based on published clinical evidence and related information available between June 3, 2019, to July 31, 2025. It is intended to provide general guidance on best evidence-based practices (see Appendix A for additional information on the evidence review methodology). Although the CPG is intended to improve the quality of care and clinical outcomes (see Introduction), it is not intended to define a standard of care (i.e., mandated or strictly required care).

## **A. Guideline Audience**

This CPG is intended for use by providers who manage, co-manage, or otherwise care for adults with osteoarthritis of the hip or knee.

## **B. Guideline Population**

This CPG is intended for adults (18 years or older) with osteoarthritis of the hip or knee who are eligible for care in the VA or DOD systems, including community care.

The current document is an update to the 2020 VA/DOD OA CPG. The following significant updates highlight the importance of clinicians reviewing this version of the CPG:

This CPG provides clinical practice recommendations for patients who would benefit from nonsurgical management of osteoarthritis of the hip or knee (see Recommendations). In addition, the Algorithm integrates the recommendations in the context of the flow of patient care. This CPG also includes Research Priorities, which list areas the Work Group identified as needing additional research. To accompany this CPG, the Work Group also developed toolkit materials for providers and patients, including a provider summary, patient summary, and quick reference guide, which can be found at https://www.healthquality.va.gov/index.asp.

## **C. Demographic Terminology in This Guideline**

The demographic terms used in this guideline are derived from the published literature sources included in the SR and evidence base. The Work Group used terms such as Black rather than African American and White rather than Caucasian to avoid presumptions about ancestry and improve clarity and consistency. To accurately present the research evidence on which this CPG is based, the Work Group made every effort to use the same terminology as reported in the published literature base of SRs, clinical trials, and other studies. Consequently, usage of demographic terms in this CPG may vary and appear inconsistent.

# **VI. Summary of Guideline Development Methodology**

The methodology used in developing this CPG follows the Guideline for Guidelines, an internal document of the VA/DOD EBPWG updated in January 2019 that outlines procedures for developing and submitting VA/DOD CPGs.(86) The Guideline for Guidelines is available at http://www.healthquality.va.gov/policy/index.asp. This CPG also aligns with the National Academy of Medicine’s (NAM) principles of trustworthy CPGs (e.g., explanation of evidence quality and strength, management of potential conflicts of interest [COI], interdisciplinary stakeholder involvement, use of SR and external review).(87) Appendix A provides a detailed description of the CPG development methodology.

## **A. Evidence Quality and Recommendation Strength**

The Work Group used the GRADE approach to craft each recommendation and determine its strength. Per the GRADE approach, recommendations must be evidence-based and cannot be made based on expert opinion alone. The GRADE approach uses the following four domains to inform the strength of each recommendation (see Determining Recommendation Strength and Direction)(88):

- Balance of desirable and undesirable outcomes

- Confidence in the quality of the evidence

- Patient or provider values and preferences

- Other implications, as appropriate (e.g., resource use, equity, acceptability, feasibility, subgroup considerations)

Using these four domains, the Work Group determined the relative strength of each recommendation ( _Strong or Weak_ ). The strength of a recommendation is defined as the extent to which one can be confident that the desirable effects of an intervention outweigh its undesirable effects and is based on the framework above, which incorporates the four domains.(89) A Strong recommendation generally indicates High or Moderate confidence in the quality of the available evidence, a clear difference in magnitude between the benefits and harms of an intervention, similar patient values and preferences, and understood influence of other implications (e.g., resource use, feasibility). A recommendation’s strength (i.e., Strong vs. Weak) does not reference its clinical importance (e.g., a Weak recommendation is evidence-based and still important to clinical care).

In some instances, the systematic evidence review might have found little or no relevant evidence, inconclusive evidence, or conflicting evidence for a particular therapy or intervention. The way this finding is expressed in the CPG might vary. The Work Group might include a statement among its recommendations acknowledging insufficient evidence for or against a commonly practiced intervention, particularly if it lacks supporting clinical evidence and poses potential risks (e.g., high opportunity cost, misallocation of resources). In other cases, the Work Group might choose to remain silent in cases evidence is lacking for a rarely used intervention or when an intervention, despite the absence of recent evidence, is considered the standard of care and has a favorable balance of benefits and harms.

Using these elements, the Work Group determines the strength and direction of each recommendation and formulates the recommendation with the general corresponding text as shown in Table 2. The strength of each recommendation is shown in Recommendations.

This CPG’s use of GRADE reflects a more rigorous application of the methodology than previous iterations; the determination of the strength of the recommendation is more directly linked to the confidence in the quality of the evidence on outcomes that are critical to clinical decision-making. The confidence in the quality of the evidence is assessed using an objective, systematic approach independent of the clinical topic of interest. Therefore, recommendations on topics for which designing and conducting rigorous studies (e.g., RCTs), might be inherently more difficult, are typically considered lower quality evidence and, in turn, are usually Weak recommendations. Recommendations on topics for which rigorous studies can be designed and conducted (e.g., RCTs) may more often be Strong recommendations. Per GRADE, if the quality of evidence differs across the relevant critical outcomes, the lowest quality of evidence for any of the critical outcomes determines the overall quality of the evidence for a recommendation.(2,90) This stricter standard provides a consistent approach to determining recommendation strengths. For additional information on GRADE or CPG methodology, see Appendix A.

# **VII. Approach to Care in the Department of Veterans Affairs and the Department of Defense**

## **A. Patient-Centered Care**

VA and DOD encourage providers to be sensitive to demographic, cultural, and other differences that affect patients’ values, needs, and preferences, aimed at treating the condition while also optimizing the individual’s overall health and well-being. Regardless of the care setting, all patients should have access to individualized evidence-based care. Patient-centered care can decrease patient anxiety, increase trust in providers, and improve treatment adherence.(96,97) A whole health approach (https:/www.va.gov/wholehealth/) empowers and equips individuals to meet their personal health and well-being goals. Clear communication is essential and should be supported by evidence-based information tailored to each patient’s needs. Guideline recommendations should be applied in a holistic approach to care that is patient-centered, culturally appropriate, and available to people with limited literacy skills and physical, sensory, or learning disabilities. The focus is using an individual’s risk factors and event history to guide the various treatment and management strategies among patients with OA of the hip and/or knee.

## **B. Shared Decision-Making**

This CPG encourages providers to practice shared decision-making, a process in which providers, patients, and patient care partners (e.g., family, friends, caregivers) consider clinical evidence of benefits and risks as well as patient values and preferences to make decisions regarding the patient’s treatment.(98) Shared decision-making is emphasized in “Crossing the Quality Chasm”, an Institute of Medicine, now NAM, report in 2001 (99) and is a core component of a patientcentered, whole health approach. Providers must be adept at presenting information to their patients regarding individual treatments, expected risks, possible outcomes, and levels and/or settings of care, especially where patient heterogeneity in weighing risks and benefits might exist. The VA and DOD have embraced shared decision-making. Providers are encouraged to use shared decision-making to individualize treatment goals and plans based on patient capabilities, needs, values, and preferences.

## **C. Patients with Co-Occurring Conditions**

Co-occurring conditions can modify the degree of risk, impact diagnosis, influence patient and provider treatment priorities and clinical decisions, and affect the overall approach to OA management. Many Veterans, Active-Duty Service Members, and their families have one or more co-occurring conditions. Because OA is often accompanied by co-occurring conditions, collaborative management with other care providers is often best. Some co-occurring conditions may require early specialist consultation to determine necessary changes in treatment or establish a common understanding of how care should be coordinated.

## **A. Diagnosis**

#### **_Recommendation_**

1. For the diagnosis of osteoarthritis of the hip and knee, we suggest against magnetic resonance imaging, computed tomography, and ultrasound. **(Weak against | Reviewed, New-replaced)**

#### **_Discussion_**

The Work Group reviewed the evidence which evaluated the efficacy of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), computed tomography (CT) and ultrasound (US) in the diagnosis of OA of hip and knee.

Radiologic exams are frequently ordered in the primary care setting to make or confirm the diagnosis of OA of the hip and/or knee. Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) grades of OA have been the gold standard of using plain X-Rays to confirm the presence on OA. Advanced imaging techniques include MRI, CT scans and US and several studies have been done to evaluate the role of these studies in the diagnosis of OA.(101,102)

MRI can show articular cartilage loss, joint effusions, injury to intra- and extra-articular soft tissue structures and evaluate the presence of bone marrow edema consistent with OA. The reviewed evidence from the previous Work Group included two diagnostic cohort studies by Xu et al. (2013) and Segal et al. (2015) that did not show a clear benefit of MRI over plain radiographs for the diagnosis of OA of the hip and/or knee.(101,102) However, a study by Menashe et al. (2012) did show a benefit only in ruling out OA, rather than diagnosing it.(103) Because Menashe et al. (2012)(103) did not meet the inclusion criteria for this CPG’s systematic evidence review, it was not considered in determining the strength of this recommendation and the previous Work Group concluded MRI should not be routinely used in the diagnosis or workup of OA.

The Work Group reviewed the current literature and will discuss the findings on the accuracy of MRI, CT and US vs X-ray for the diagnosis of OA. The strength of evidence of three studies was low, and one was moderate. We did not identify any new studies related to HOA.

Muñoz-García, et al. (2020) looked at the accuracy of MRI vs X-ray in the diagnosis of KOA using arthroscopy as the reference standard. MRI alone compared to X-ray showed a greater specificity (SP) (88.6% vs 60%) but a lower sensitivity (SN) (70.8% vs 75%) in diagnosing OA. When evaluating the degree of OA, they found that MRI underestimated the degree of early OA when compared to arthroscopy and demonstrated a large variability in the KL grades of MRI vs arthroscopy. We did not identify any studies related to HOA. The Muñoz-García et al. (2020) study evaluated MRI compared to arthroscopy as a reference standard. The study found there was no added benefit of weight-bearing radiographs to the MRI findings, however, the evidence demonstrated a large variability in severity of OA on MRI versus arthroscopy. MRI and X-ray were more predictive of diagnosis more advanced cases of OA and there was no benefit to combine both studies in the diagnosis of OA.(104)

There is some variability in patient expectations regarding the use of MRI for hip and knee OA. Some patients inaccurately believe that MRI is better than plain radiographs and, as such, may request an MRI in the clinical setting. They also may feel that MRI is safer due to decreased radiation. The Work Group recognizes this can pose difficult clinical scenarios; however, providers

should feel comfortable educating patients on the lack of superiority of MRI over radiographs from a diagnostic standpoint. Additionally, the overall burdens associated with MRI (e.g., increased resource use, cost, time, and logistic difficulty), far outweigh those associated with weight-bearing radiographs. Moreover, while there is minimal physical harm associated with MRI testing, sometimes results can mislead patients and providers as to proper diagnosis and treatment. Some orthopedic surgeons may request an MRI for surgical planning; however, this applies to a minority of patients and should be left to surgeon discretion following surgical consultation.

In the evaluation of CT for KOA Segal et al. (2021)(105) compared weight bearing CTs to lateral radiographs using MRI as a reference standard. This study evaluated patellar femoral arthritis and showed a weight bearing CT had a greater SN (85%-97% vs 48%-57%) for the diagnosis of KOA compared to a lateral X-ray but a lower SP using an axial weight bearing CT for detecting lateral full-thickness cartilage loss (32% vs 68%) in the diagnosis of OA. The CT scan when compared to an X-ray is costly, requires more resources, greater exposure to radiation and can be a time burden to patients and providers, and possible increased pain to patients due to prolonged standing for weight bearing CT. The authors commented in their discussion that future studies would compare weight bearing CT to the gold standard of a Skyline view.

Two US studies, Brom et al. (2020)(106) and Yates et al. (2025)(107) were evaluated for their ability to detect OA of the knee. Currently there is no defined US protocol to detect OA, so Yates et al. (2025)(107) proposed a US based protocol for OA evaluation of radiographic KOA. They demonstrated that an experienced ultrasonographer could detect osteophytes and thinning of the femoral cartilage associated with the detection of OA. Brom et al. (2020)(106) reported US was able to detect OA with a 95% SN for the detection of femoral cartilage thinning or presence of osteophytes in knee OA and 94% SP when combining the loss of femoral cartilage and presence of osteophytes. Most of the findings were in patients with KL 3 grades and they noted an experienced ultrasonographer may be able to detect earlier stages of OA.

Although US demonstrated excellent SN and adequate SP in the detection of KOA it was still limited in early onset arthritis and limitations were present secondary to body habitus, user variability, acoustic windows which could not detect the physiological changes associated with weight bearing. The benefit of US is that it may be readily available in specialty clinics and is a non-painful diagnostic tool. The harm is that it is not always available in primary care clinics and risks variable results due to variability in user expertise.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(101- 104,106,107) There were two new modalities added for the diagnosis of OA of the knee. Therefore, it was categorized as _Reviewed, New-replaced_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of evidence was Low. Advanced imaging has improved in the detection of OA **_,_** but the reviewed literature did not demonstrate the superiority of MRI, CT, and US to plain, weightbearing X-rays in the diagnosis of OA of the knee. Many patients believe MRI is superior to any other imaging and may detect early OA, but the SN was poor compared to arthroscopic findings in the detection of OA. The weight bearing CT was better than a lateral knee X-ray for diagnosis of patellar femoral arthritis but not compared to a Skyline View X-ray which is the current gold standard. US did show promise in the detection of OA but is still developing the protocols needed to detect OA. US is not always readily available and does require an experienced ultrasonographer. The harms **_,_** included increased cost, resource use, and time burden for the

patients and providers, increased pain due to prolonged standing for weight bearing and CT, and radiation for CT and MRI overall the harms outweighed the lack of evidence-based benefits. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak against_ recommendation.

#### **_Discussion_**

Obesity is defined as a chronic, relapsing, multifactorial disease characterized by abnormal or excessive adipose tissue accumulation that impairs health and increases the risk of comorbidities and premature mortality.

The causes of obesity are multifactorial and include genetic susceptibility, environmental and social determinants, behavioral patterns, and physiological dysregulation of multiple systems.

Overweight is often viewed as a precursor or a less severe form of this same chronic condition.* Obesity is defined as a BMI of 30kg/m2 , and overweight is defined as between 25-29.9kg/m2 . In the DOD, normal BMI is defined between 19-27.5kg/m2 to account for characteristic enhanced musculature in Active-Duty populations compared to their civilian counterparts. BMI is the most used screening tool for overweight and obesity and is calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2 ); it is widely utilized due to its low cost, easy to use, scalable, and convenience.•◘○* While commonly used, BMI is not without its faults and does not have a component in its calculation to differentiate lean tissue mass, including bone, blood, and essential fat mass, from other adipose tissue.

Throughout the review of evidence, improved outcomes on principle areas of clinical importance were more consistently noted in patients with KOA than with HOA. The strength of evidence of KOA outcomes was mostly moderate, compared to the low/very low quality of evidence for HOA. Meta-analysis showed that the greater the weight loss, the better the clinical function, reduced joint stiffness, and pain reduction. Dose dependent outcomes were regularly observed across 12 weight loss modalities ranging from cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) to bariatric surgery.(108) Across 18 studies, each decrement of 1% in weight affected an average decrement of about 2% in the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) function, with 13 studies corroborating the same dose effect for WOMAC stiffness.(108)

> * See the VA/DOD Clinical Practice Guideline for the Management of Adult Overweight and Obesity. Available at: https://www.healthquality.va.gov/guidelines/CD/obesity/

The same response was also noted for WOMAC pain over the analysis of 22 studies.(108) Important risk reduction was noted for knee surgery and inversely associated with weight loss percentage. Each reduction in unit BMI from baseline was associated with a 2-3% decrease in knee replacement,(109,110) with individuals losing >7.5% demonstrating the most significant reduction in risk for knee replacement.(110)

Meaningful, clinically significant HOA outcomes were less frequently observed across continuous measures of weight loss, and instead, were more significantly concentrated on weight loss that categorically occurred at >10%. Dose dependent responses were also observed across the Hip Disability and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (HOOS) pain, range of motion, and QoL such that a 1% reduction in weight loss was associated with 0.54, 0.43, and 0.67 unit reductions in HOOS subscales, respectively.(111) Overall, inconclusive evidence was synthesized for total hip replacement with reduced risk for surgery only being identified with >5% weight gain (110) and no difference in risk for surgery from weight stable groups across all weight loss cohorts.(109,110)

The Work Group did note some significant limitations to generalizability regarding participants and methods. While over half the studies included in network meta-analyses (NMA) were RCTs, examining the studies included from Panunzi et al. (2021)(108) constituted a population of 75% women. Despite the generally recognized higher prevalence and incidence of OA in women, the VA/DOD populations are predominantly male, and outcomes may not generalize to Veterans and military Service Members.

Similarly, the inclusion of exercise in many weight loss modalities examined in Panunzi et al. (2021)(108) may conflate some of the improvements noted from weight loss with potential improvements from benefits more exclusively observed and reported from physical activity alone or in combination with other therapies.

Throughout the body of evidence included for the review of weight loss on KOA and HOA outcomes, an overwhelming consensus favoring benefits over harm was upheld. In patients with obesity/overweight, weight loss as an outcome is consistently regarded as appropriate, safe, and advantageous for many health outcomes. These benefits include improved function, stiffness, pain, and risk reduction for surgery. Modalities of weight loss such as pharmacotherapy, bariatric surgery, and specific exercise/dietary interventions accompany significant risk. Specific weight loss modalities and safety were not included within the scope of the evidence at this time.

Some variation in patient preferences may exist regarding weight loss. Overweight, and certainly obese patients, are likely to have had some form of care or conversation with their primary provider before beginning the OA clinical pathway. Failure or inability to reduce weight may be negatively associated with self-efficacy or partially negate social determinants of health (SDOH) beyond the patient’s capacity to address. Great care must be rendered navigating these complex intersectionalities to preserve provider-patient trust and maintain quality of care. Furthermore, some patients may experience disproportionate rates of adequate food access, education, economic stability, and urbanization. The culmination of these factors can greatly impact access to care making some weight loss modalities, like bariatric surgery, unfeasible for some.

Modality aside, there are also patients that may require more individualized care for discussions of weight loss such as elderly patients who are diagnosed with sarcopenia or Active-Duty military who have a high BMI with significant musculature.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to these recommendations.(108-111) The strength and quality of evidence was consistently higher for outcomes of interest including WOMAC and HOOS function, pain, stiffness and QoL related to KOA over HOA.(109,110) Within KOA studies, results consistently upheld 1% changes in body weight with 2% improvements on WOMAC function, stiffness, and pain scores.(108) Weight loss was also well represented as a protective measure against total knee replacement, but the evidence for hip replacement was largely insufficient.(109,110) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence for the hip and knee was Moderate. The body of evidence had some limitations including the generalizability of the study's findings, particularly for VA/DOD populations. The research, which drew heavily from a meta-analysis where 75% of participants were female. Furthermore, the common inclusion of exercise within the weight loss modalities studied makes it difficult to distinguish the benefits of weight loss from those of physical activity, possibly conflating the results. The benefits of weight loss were consistently viewed as safe and effective and significantly outweighed the potential harm of select weight loss modalities that were beyond the scope of the Work Group to evaluate at this time. Patient values and preferences had some variation because of the feasibility, resource availability for some modalities like surgery, and appropriateness due to considerations like enhanced musculature in Active-Duty populations and presence of sarcopenia in older patients. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Strong for_ recommendation for KOA and a _Weak for_ recommendation for HOA.

#### **_Recommendation_**

4. For the self-management of osteoarthritis of the knee, we suggest tailored patient education and physical activity. **(Weak for | Reviewed, New-replaced)**

#### **_Discussion_**

Self-management is the active engagement of individual patients in daily tasks or activities that aim to improve their QoL and long-term management of the health condition.(112) Providing education and self-management support which could help enable patients to adopt self-directed behaviors which are known to promote joint health are likely to be helpful (see Appendix L for education content).(113) Patients implement these structured activity strategies on their own though most care plans include initial patient education and additional support to be successfully implemented. A growing body of evidence indicates that telehealth supported exercise programs may be a practical strategy to reduce pain and improve functional outcomes in KOA.

Xiang et al. (2024)(114) performed a SR with 11 RCTs comparing telehealth supported exercise vs inactive controls in patients with KOA. The results favored the telehealth-supported exercise programs for improvement in function and reductions in pain. The included studies demonstrated considerable risk for bias, heterogeneous interventions, and statistical analyses of outcomes showing a wide confidence interval. The strength of evidence was therefore rated as low. Similarly, in another SR containing 5 RCTs (115) telehealth-based exercise interventions delivered by web or smart phone were effective in improving pain and physical function in patients with KOA, comparable to traditional face to face exercise treatment and better than usual care or

an inactive control. The included studies demonstrated limitations in study quality with small sample sizes and wide confidence intervals. The strength of the evidence was therefore rated as low.

Three additional RCTs (116-118) investigated web or app-based applications with reminders promoting program adherence. Each demonstrated improvements in function in patients with KOA when compared to usual care or an inactive control. One small RCT (119) demonstrated no difference in function between home-based circuit training and a no exercise control group. However, findings in this study favored the home-based intervention over control for reduction in pain.

Modes of telehealth interventions varied across the studies included in the evidence base, though meaningful differences were more commonly observed in interventions with a structured program (education, patient logging, continual access to program and implementation guidelines) compared to programs consisting of only telephone contact and reminders. Methods included delivery by telephone, mobile app, internet, wearable device, or applications combined with wearable devices. There were no adverse events or harms reported.

The evidence found treatment with certain self-directed exercise interventions to improve function, pain, and QoL when compared to usual care/standard of care. The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of evidence was Low. The body of evidence had limitations in the methodological quality of the included RCTs, indirectness due to some RCTs included in the SRs not meeting the minimum follow-up, and imprecision due to wide confidence intervals and limited information on specific components of the exercises. The benefits of improved pain, QoL (physical), physical function, and mobility in patients with KOA outweighed the potential harm of adverse events, which were negligible. ## **C. Physical Therapy**

#### **_Recommendation_**

5. For osteoarthritis of the hip and knee, we suggest a structured physical therapy program. **(Weak for | Reviewed, Amended)**

#### **_Discussion_**

Physical therapy is a structured rehabilitation intervention that focuses on optimizing movement and physical function in patients with a variety of conditions including musculoskeletal conditions such as hip or knee osteoarthritis. For knee and hip OA, physical therapists frequently combine progressive exercise, manual therapy and gait/movement retraining to

address pain and functional deficits. Physical therapists also provide education related to pain, lifestyle and activity modifications. Extensive literature exists to support the use of physical therapy as a core treatment in the comprehensive management of patients with OA of the hip or knee. For example, a RCT by Deyle et al. (2020) compared physical therapy to glucocorticoid injections in a military health system and results favored the physical therapy group on both primary and secondary outcome measures at one year.(81) Since this study was not included in this CPG’s systematic evidence review, it did not impact the strength of this recommendation. An individualized physical therapy management plan can reduce pain and improve function in patients with hip or knee OA and, therefore, augment medical and pharmacologic approaches.(120-122) Physical therapists may utilize both manual physical therapy treatments and prescribed exercises in the treatment of knee and hip OA. Manual therapy treatments utilize hands-on techniques such as soft tissue mobilization, joint manipulation, and joint mobilization. Physical therapists also provide patients with education related to pain, lifestyle, and activity modifications. In addition to exercise and manual therapy, physical therapists may incorporate adjunctive modalities.

In patients with features suggestive of nociplastic or centrally mediated pain, physical therapy management may also incorporate biopsychosocial interventions aimed at addressing psychological and behavioral contributors to pain. As outlined in Appendix I, these approaches may include strategies consistent with CBT, Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), Pain Reprocessing Therapy (PRT), and Cognitive Functional Therapy (CFT). Within some care settings, including interdisciplinary pain programs, physical therapists may play a primary role in delivering or reinforcing these interventions as part of a comprehensive, multimodal treatment approach.

#### **_Knee Osteoarthritis_**

A SR by Anwer et al. (2018) examined the effects of manual therapy in patients with KOA.(121) They compared manual therapy alone or manual therapy in combination with exercise-toexercise therapy alone or control. The SR consisted of 11 RCTs (n=494). Their review indicated short-term benefit of exercise therapy with manual therapy compared to exercise alone and a stronger effect on pain relief from manual therapy alone compared to exercise alone in short-term follow-up. The Work Group noted serious limitations in the results due to considerable heterogeneity of the manual interventions and the quality of some of the individual trials. Manual therapy interventions included Maitland mobilizations, mobilizations with movement, soft tissue mobilization, myofascial techniques, and high velocity low amplitude thrust techniques to other lower extremity joints. Only four of the 11 trials were a Physiotherapy Evidence Database (PEDro) grade of six or higher with the remaining scoring below six, indicating overall low quality. Two RCTs directly compared manual therapy plus exercise to exercise alone in KOA.(123,124) Abbott et al. (2015) performed a four-arm parallel group factorial RCT in 75 patients and found that exercise with booster sessions and exercise plus manual therapy produced the strongest improvements in WOMAC scores and sit-to-stand tests at one year compared to exercise alone, with favorable pain reduction in the manual therapy group.(123) Fitzgerald et al. (2016) conducted a similar four-arm RCT in 300 patients and found no differences between groups at one year on WOMAC or performance-based measures; however, secondary analysis suggested short-term benefit of manual therapy and booster sessions, with increased odds of pain relief at one year.(124) Overall, physical therapy

approaches utilizing manual therapy with or without exercise appear to show positive effects on pain and function in KOA at both three-month and one-year follow-up.

#### **_Hip Osteoarthritis_**

Beumer et al. (2016) performed a SR and meta-analysis of exercise and manual therapy on pain with HOA.(120) The SR included 19 RCTs though only four studies directly compared manual therapy and exercise vs exercise alone vs control. The results indicated a short-term benefit for pain relief and function when comparing aquatic and land-based exercise to control. However, the findings did not demonstrate additional benefit of manual therapy, with or without exercise, compared to exercise therapy alone or a minimal control in short-term comparisons. There was considerable heterogeneity among trials in terms of size and methodology, and the authors emphasized the need for future well-designed trials evaluating manual therapy and exercise in HOA. The effect of manual therapy in HOA therefore remains less clear compared to KOA. Shared decision-making between the physical therapist and patient is important when considering manual therapy interventions, and clinicians must also weigh the economic burden associated with various physical therapy approaches, including lost wages, transportation costs, and co-pays.

This is a _Reviewed, Amended_ recommendation and no new evidence was reviewed. The Work Group issued an addendum focused on refinement of the recommendation wording. The strength of evidence remains unchanged from the prior recommendation. The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of evidence continues to be low, consistent with previously identified limitations including small sample sizes, confounding in analyses, and attrition of participants. Despite these limitations, the Work Group determined that the benefits of exercise-based physical therapy, including improvements in pain, function, and QoL, outweigh the potential harms. Physical therapy is generally safe and associated with minimal risk of injury. Identified harms include the burden of time, effort, and adherence, potential financial cost, and the possibility of transient soreness or temporary increases in pain. Higher-intensity exercise modalities, particularly eccentric loading, may carry a greater risk of muscle injury, though serious adverse events (SAEs) remain rare.

Patient values and preferences are largely aligned with prior recommendations, with many patients favoring non-pharmacologic approaches. Variation exists, as some patients hold erroneous beliefs that physical therapy or strengthening exercises may worsen OA, while others perceive therapy as burdensome due to visit frequency. Preferences may also vary by delivery mode as discussed in the next recommendation. From a resource-use perspective, exercisebased therapy is relatively low cost, though certain modalities such as aquatic interventions require access to specialized locations. Overall acceptability is high, feasibility is supported by the availability of multiple delivery formats (including in-person and remote options), and subgroup considerations suggest that some patients may derive greater benefit than others depending on individual factors.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation from the existing CPG evidence base and no new evidence was identified or reviewed for this update. Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, Amended_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Low. The body of evidence had some limitations including small sample sizes, confounding in analyses, and attrition of participants, as reported in prior SRs and

randomized trials evaluating exercise-based interventions for OA.(125,126) The benefits of exercise-based physical therapy, including improvements in pain, physical function, and QoL outweighed the potential harms which were small and primarily related to treatment burden, cost, and transient symptom exacerbation. Patient values and preferences varied somewhat because many patients favor non-pharmacologic approaches, while others perceive physical therapy as burdensome due to visit frequency, cost, or concern that exercise may worsen OA Other considerations included generally low resource use, high acceptability, broad feasibility across multiple delivery formats (including in-person and remote options), and a low incidence of adverse events, with higher-intensity exercise modalities such as eccentric loading carrying a slightly increased but still rare risk of muscle injury.(127) Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

#### **_Recommendation_**

6. For osteoarthritis of the hip and knee, there is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against a specific type or mode of delivery of a structured physical therapy program over another.

**(Neither for nor against | Reviewed, New-added)**

#### **_Discussion_**

The Work Group determined there is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against one specific type of exercise-based physical therapy program or one mode of delivery over another for individuals with OA of the hip or knee. The available evidence primarily evaluates exercise interventions rather than the broader components of physical therapy (e.g., manual therapy, modalities, or other adjunctive treatments), and therefore this recommendation is focused on exercise program characteristics rather than the full scope of physical therapy practice. Physical therapy that includes structured exercise is widely supported as a key part of OA management. However, patients often have questions about which type of exercise program works best. Programs can vary by setting (land-based vs. aquatic), intensity (high vs. low), focus (general strengthening vs. motor control), and delivery method (in-person, home-based, or telehealthsupported). After reviewing the available research, the Work Group found that there is not enough consistent evidence to recommend one specific type of exercise program or delivery method over another. The current evidence does not show clear, reliable superiority of any single exercise approach or format across important outcomes such as pain and function.

Multiple recent SRs evaluating a wide range of exercise modalities and delivery formats, including land-based exercise, resistance training, aerobic exercise, aquatic therapy, motor control-focused programs, and technology- or telehealth-supported intervention, generally found similar improvements in pain, function, and QoL. No modality consistently outperformed others.(128-132)

Mo et al. (2023) conducted a SR and NMA of 39 RCTs looking at exercise therapies for KOA, and found that multiple exercise modalities, including aerobic exercise, resistance training, yoga, stationary cycling, and traditional exercise, were effective in improving pain, stiffness, physical function, and QoL.(128) In treatment rankings, yoga and aerobic exercise tended to rank higher for pain and stiffness reduction, resistance training ranked higher for physical function, and mindbody or mixed exercise programs ranked favorably for QoL, though the authors emphasized that differences were based largely on indirect comparisons and did not demonstrate consistent superiority of one modality across all outcomes.

Xu et al. (2023) conducted a SR and meta-analysis of 11 RCTs evaluating aquatic exercise in people with KOA and found that aquatic exercise produced significant short-term improvements in pain, stiffness, and physical function compared with no exercise.(129) However, when compared with land-based exercise, there were no significant differences between aquatic and land-based exercise in pain, stiffness, or physical function, and no SAEs were reported.

Zhai et al. (2025)(131) conducted a SR and meta-analysis of 9 RCTs evaluating high-intensity training in patients with KOA, finding that high-intensity exercise significantly improved knee flexion strength, leg press strength, Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS) symptoms, and KOOS QoL compared with control interventions.(131) However, it had no significant effect on overall pain (WOMAC/KOOS pain), 6-minute walk test (6MWT), knee extension strength, stair climbing, or timed up-and-go (TUG) performance.

Lastly, evidence from systematic reviews and a non-inferiority RCT indicates that digital and mobile health-supported exercise or rehabilitation interventions produce improvements in pain and physical function that are comparable to in-person physiotherapy or usual care, with no consistent evidence that digital delivery is superior to traditional approaches.(132-134)

The Work Group also considered patient values and preferences as Veterans and Service Members consistently express a preference for non-pharmacologic, active treatments. Variability in exercise type, intensity, and delivery modality is particularly valued, given variable duty demands and/or access to care. From a resource perspective, exercise-based physical therapy and self-management interventions are relatively low cost and scalable, especially when delivered through home-based or telehealth-supported formats. Acceptability of these interventions is high across clinical settings, and feasibility is supported by multiple delivery options. Subgroup considerations include the need to individualize programs for those with high BMI, chronic pain, or prior musculoskeletal injury, as well as for older Veterans with mobility limitations, where lowerimpact or progressive programs may be preferred. No evidence outside the scope of the SR was used to inform the strength of this recommendation.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation, including multiple SRs and RCTs evaluating different types of exercise, exercise intensity, and modes of delivery for physical therapy in patients with OA of the hip and knee.(128-134) Therefore, this recommendation is categorized as _Reviewed, New Added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Low, as the body of evidence had several limitations, including heterogeneity in exercise interventions and outcome measures, small sample sizes in individual trials, and short follow-up durations in some studies. Across the evidence base, no single exercise modality (e.g., resistance training, aerobic exercise, aquatic therapy, motor control training), intensity, or mode of delivery (e.g., supervised, home-based, or digital/telehealth-supported) demonstrated consistent superiority over others for improving pain, function, or QoL. The benefits of offering physical therapy and exercise-based interventions, including improvements in pain, function, and overall physical performance, outweighed the potential harms, which were generally small and consisted primarily of transient musculoskeletal discomfort or symptom exacerbation, with SAEs being rare. Patient values and preferences varied somewhat, as most patients valued non-pharmacologic, active treatments but differed in preferred exercise type, intensity, supervision level, and delivery format based on lifestyle, access, and tolerance for discomfort. Considering the favorable balance of benefits to harms, generally similar patient values with some variation, and

low certainty of evidence without clear superiority of one approach, the Work Group decided upon a _Neither for nor against_ recommendation.

## **D. Devices**

**_Recommendation_**

7. In select patients with osteoarthritis of the knee, we suggest bracing. **(Weak for | Reviewed, New-replaced)**

#### **_Discussion_**

Soft braces and valgus knee braces improve pain and physical function in the knee joint in individuals with KOA.(135-137) Soft braces are elastic non-adhesive orthoses which do not provide realignment of the knee.(136) Soft braces are thought to improve proprioception via stimulation of cutaneous mechanoreceptors, additionally they exert a mild compression effect and are thought to improve the patient’s sense of joint stability leading to greater confidence in the knee joint.(136) Valgus braces are hinge braces with a leverage system designed to apply a corrective force to redistribute load away from the medial compartment, additionally these braces are also thought to improve joint stability and proprioception.(135)

The previous evidence synthesis review identified one SR on soft braces by Cudejko et al. (2018) which showed a statistically significant difference favoring soft braces in three RCTs compared to a usual care control group.(136) The soft brace had a moderate effect on decreasing pain up to 24 weeks (standard mean difference [SMD] 0.61) and a small to moderate effect on physical function up to 24 weeks (SMD 0.39). The strength of this evidence was rated as low with potential for bias related to blinding in some of the studies and the impossibility of blinding participants in brace trials. Additionally, there was insufficient evidence to recommend one specific soft brace over another. The choice of usual care as a control group in RCTs has potential limitations because of variability and inconsistency typically present in usual care across trials. No data for harms for soft brace were reported however the Work Group acknowledged potential downsides including slipping, discomfort related to excessive heat retention, and constraining/swelling if brace is poorly fitted. Overall, the Work Group felt these harms to be minor and likely infrequent compared to potential benefits.

The previous evidence synthesis review identified one meta-analysis on valgus braces by Moyer et al. (2015)(135) which found a statistically significant difference favoring valgus knee bracing for pain and function in patients with medial compartment KOA up to 52 weeks. The effect sizes were moderate for pain (SMD 0.56) and function (SMD 0.48) compared to the control group without orthosis use, but only a small statistically significant effect for pain (SMD 0.33) compared to a control group with a control orthosis.(135) Minor complications such as slipping, skin irritation and constraining or poor fit were common with estimates 1-22% while other complications such as swelling or blisters were infrequently reported as 3-4% event rate. Instructions varied considerably for brace use (i.e., how many days per week, how many hours per day and for what activities). Additionally, there was insufficient evidence to recommend one specific valgus brace over another. The strength of this evidence was rated as low with potential for bias related to blinding noted in some of the studies and the impossibility of blinding participants in brace trials. The current evidence synthesis review found one additional trial Gueugnon et al. (2021) comparing a specific valgus type brace (ODRA brace) to usual care up to 52 weeks. Statistically significant

improvements in pain, function, and QoL were reported.(137) The quality was graded as very low due to concerns for imprecision and risk of bias. The strength of evidence for this study was rated as very low.

The Work Group also considered patient values and preferences indicating general acceptance for soft bracing whereas valgus bracing may elicit more variable preferences due to concerns regarding bulk and fit. Patient adherence/wear time with brace can be influenced by comfort, appearance, and ease of application. Military profiling may be needed for the use of braces in Active-Duty Service Members. Some patients may find braces more uncomfortable in hot weather or interfere with certain clothing. Braces are generally inexpensive though valgus braces carry higher cost compared to soft braces and frequently require fitting and potentially custom sizing to accommodate deformity. Braces do require some resource use as they need to be fitted properly, and valgus braces may require additional appointments to adjust the brace to ensure proper fit. All braces have potential for slipping and may need periodic readjustment throughout the day. Patients with reported gait instability may require additional consideration of ambulatory aid. Valgus braces are primarily used for patients with medial tibiofemoral compartment KOA and varus deformity. Soft braces are often recommended to improve proprioception and sense of joint instability. Hinge braces were not part of the evidence review though the Work Group noted that common indications for hinge brace include the presence of medial or lateral instability.

Some providers may be hesitant to prescribe a brace due to concerns of muscle atrophy or functional loss. However, a study by Yu et al. (2016)(138) which was within the SR by Cudejko et al. (2018)(136) found that instead of functional loss, within group comparisons showed improvement on TUG and 6MWT up to 52 weeks in the soft bracing group.(138) Additionally, a secondary analysis of a RCT by Callaghan et al. (2016)(139) on bracing in Patellofemoral Joint (PFJ) OA, although outside the scope of the evidence review, did not influence the strength of the recommendation and found that use of a brace improved quadriceps muscle maximal voluntary contraction at 6 weeks compared to no brace. The original RCT by Callaghan et al. (2015)(140) was within the SR by Cudejko et al. (2018) which the Work Group reviewed.(136) The Work Group is not aware of any direct studies measuring quadricep muscle atrophy with brace use however, the potential concern would be highest for braces which immobilize the knee rather than a functional brace which allows for normal function and muscle use.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(135- 137) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-replaced_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low. The benefits of bracing to improve functional outcomes and pain slightly outweighed the potential harms which are very minor and relate to fit of the brace. The body of evidence had some limitations including small sample size, variability in comparators’, blinding, imprecision, heterogeneity between studies Moyer et al. (2015) and Cudejko et al. (2018).(135,136) Patient values and preferences may have some variation, with most patients likely agreeable to trialing a brace though some patients may have different views on use of a brace and overall adherence/wear time with brace is influenced by comfort, appearance, ease of application etc. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

#### **_Recommendation_**

8. For osteoarthritis of the knee, there is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against the use of orthopedic insoles.

#### **(Neither for nor Against | Reviewed, New-added)**

#### **_Discussion_**

There is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against orthopedic insoles including motion control insoles, lateral wedge insoles, and biomechanical footwear for improvement of pain and function in KOA. Orthopedic insoles are typically designed to alter ground reaction forces to reduce biomechanical moments of the knee and adjust joint force distribution to improve comfort and support during walking. For example, lateral heel wedges have been proposed to limit medial compartment stress by reducing the knee adduction moment (varus moment).

Yu et al. (2021)(141) performed a SR with 10 RCTs comparing orthopedic insoles primarily lateral wedge insoles to a control insole (neutral or flat insoles). There were no significant differences shown for pain or function for orthopedic insoles compared to control insoles. Most studies within the SR had low numbers of patients in each treatment group and some studies did not adequately match for age and sex which increased heterogeneity across trials. Further heterogeneity was noted across trials with respect to controlling for severity of KOA and/or duration of symptoms and with respect to the variability of outcome measurements across trials. The strength of evidence was therefore rated as very low. Hsu et al. (2022)(142) performed a small RCT (n=25) which compared a lateral wedge insole to a neutral insole. No differences were noted between baseline and 20 weeks for within group comparisons for pain and selfreported function for the lateral wedge insole group. The strength of evidence for this study was graded as very low due to concerns around imprecision, sample size, and blinding. Ferreira et al. (2021)(143) performed a small RCT (n=38) comparing lateral wedge insoles to neutral insoles and found no difference for pain, self-reported function or QoL at 12 weeks. The strength of evidence for this study was graded as very low due to concerns around imprecision, sample size and blinding. Paterson et al. (2022)(144) performed a small RCT (n=40) comparing motion control shoes, designed to offload the lateral knee compartment, to neutral shoes. No differences were reported for pain, self-reported function, or QoL between the two groups up to 6 months. The strength of evidence was rated as low due to study size and imprecision. A RCT by Reichenbach et al. (2020) (n=220)(145) compared biomechanical footwear to a control footwear. The biomechanical footwear involved individually calibrated convex pods designed to adjust the center of pressure and alter low limb biomechanics and compartment pressure. There was a statistically significant difference up to 16 weeks favoring the biomechanical footwear group for pain and self-reported function compared to control. However, this was the only randomized study on this intervention reviewed by the Work Group, and the group also weighed concerns regarding the cost, required training, and resource demands associated with biomechanical footwear. While the results appear promising, further studies are needed to replicate the results. There are likely no major harms from insoles and minor adverse events (AEs) for insoles are not well reported across studies. Reichenbach et al. (2020) reported the most common minor AEs

were musculoskeletal pain related (15%) in the knee, back, hip or foot.(145) The Work Group also acknowledges potential for discomfort occurring with lateral wedge insoles which likely increase with increasing angulation (143) and insoles may require a break in/adjustment period.

The Work Group also considered patient values and preferences for orthotics, feeling that there would be some variation in patient values and preference toward orthopedic insoles and that comfort is likely to significantly impact adherence and tolerability of insoles. Military profiling may be needed for the use of specialized orthotics in Active-Duty Service Members. Orthotics can range in cost and can vary in quality and ability to alter external knee adduction moment. All insoles require some resource use to ensure proper sizing and fit, and customized insoles can considerably increase the cost and resources demand. There is variability in the literature regarding application and wear time. There is potential for increased foot/ankle, hip/low back discomfort related to changing biomechanical variables associated with gait.

The Work Group systematically reviewed new evidence related to this recommendation.(141-145) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-Added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low. The body of evidence had some limitations including significant heterogeneity across trials, small sample sizes, differences in interventions across trial. The benefits of orthotics on pain and function are inconsistent and appear to be balanced with the potential harms which are small related to increased musculoskeletal discomfort. Patient values and preferences are likely varied because patients sometimes discontinue use of insoles due to discomfort. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Neither for nor Against_ recommendation.

#### **_Recommendation_**

9. For osteoarthritis of the knee, there is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against the use of transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation.

 - **(Neither for nor Against | Reviewed, Not changed)**

#### **_Discussion_**

Assessing electrostimulation devices was not a key question for the 2025 CPG update so no new evidence was addressed or included in the query protocol. Although there is interest in use of non-invasive and non-pharmacologic interventions for the management of OA-related pain, the Work Group during development of the KQs thought it unlikely that there would be new evidence of sufficient rigor to better inform providers (e.g. change to either for or against recommendation). However, the Work Group felt carrying the 2020 recommendation forward was important as some patients prefer non-pharmacologic management options. Based on the 2020 review **,** a SR by Chen et al. (2016) found that transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) use in OA of the knee had a statistically significant but not clinically meaningful difference in pain compared to control.(146) Chen et al. (2016) noted that for small studies, the largest effects of TENS occurred at a two-week follow-up, while larger studies indicated no difference between TENS and control, especially in long-term follow-up.(146) The included studies also failed to show any improvement in range of motion or TUG test compared to control. The included RCTs had small sample sizes with considerable heterogeneity and inconsistencies in their findings, which makes it difficult to reach definitive conclusions.

Cherian et al. (2015) compared 40 patients with TENS to a standard control group and found statistically significant improvement in pain (Visual Analog Scale [VAS]) at three months but no significant difference in functional measures between groups.(147) A RCT by Inal et al. (2015) compared sham, high-frequency TENS, and low frequency TENS for patients with knee OA.(148) Inal et al. (2015)(148) found similar reductions in pain at rest and pain with movement but no differences between groups at two or six weeks. Cherian et al. (2016) found, among 30 patients

randomized into a TENS group and a control group, the improvement in the TUG test, timed stairclimb test (SCT), and 20-time step test at three months favored the TENS group.(149) However, there was no significant difference between groups for pain (VAS) at three months. Another RCT by Cherian et al. (2016) compared standard care and TENS for OA of the knee and found no difference in pain (VAS) or functional outcomes measured at one year.(150) All four RCTs were small and had significant study limitations including limited description of randomization methods, allocation concealment, and blinding of outcome assessors.

Concerning harms, Chen et al. (2016) found no significant difference in all-cause discontinuation of TENS compared to control.(146) The uncommon, mild potential harms of TENS included increased pain, skin breakdown, redness, and skin irritation.(147,150) The benefits of TENS appear to be small, short-term effects, which are unlikely to be clinically significant. Within the SR by Chen et al. (2016), there was insufficient evidence to demonstrate clear effectiveness of a particular type of TENS application or stimulation frequency in comparison to interferential stimulation.(146) The systematic evidence review also did not yield any evidence examining the use of TENS for HOA.

The Work Group also considered patient preferences, feasibility, and subgroup considerations. Patients with comorbidities may not be able to participate in an exercise program. This intervention may benefit these patients if other non-surgical treatments are not feasible. The Patient Focus Group noted that patients view exercise programs, including physical therapy, as helpful, but pain can impede successful adherence to an exercise program. TENS may offer short-term pain relief. In patients who had prior success with using TENS therapy for pain relief, a provider could consider TENS as an adjunct therapy for short-term pain relief.

The systematic evidence review specifically searched for but was unable to find studies meeting the inclusion criteria for other types of electrotherapeutic devices (e.g., H-Wave, AlphaStim, Biowave). Considering the resource use and lack of clear effectiveness demonstrated for these devices, there is a lack of evidence to recommend these devices over TENS for use in clinical practice in knee or hip OA.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(146-150) Therefore, it is categorized as a _Reviewed, Not Changed_ recommendation. The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of evidence was Very low. There were significant limitations in the evidence given the heterogeneity of methods, application of devices, size of the studies, risk of bias, and inconsistent results on pain and function. The benefits of TENS for OA of the knee slightly outweighed the harms. Patient preferences and subgroup considerations also impacted this recommendation. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Neither for nor against_ recommendation.

## **E. Pharmacotherapy**

### **_a. Topical Pharmacotherapy_**

#### **_Recommendation_**

10. For osteoarthritis of the knee, we suggest topical capsaicin.

**(Weak for | Reviewed, Amended)**

#### **_Discussion_**

Topically applied capsaicin has been used for the management of various types of pain, including neuropathic pain syndromes (e.g., postherpetic neuralgia, diabetic neuropathy, post-mastectomy pain) and pain arising from OA. Although topical capsaicin is generally used as adjunctive therapy, it may be used as monotherapy in patients with mild-to-moderate OA pain. Capsaicin reversibly depletes substance P (SP), an endogenous neuropeptide involved in the pathogenesis and modulation of pain. Capsaicin is derived from chili peppers, and its application stimulates the release of SP, initially causing a painful, burning sensation. With prolonged exposure and continued application, SP is depleted from afferent neurons, and transmission of painful stimuli is reduced or absent.

Adverse events include temporary burning, stinging, and pain at the application site. A SR by Laslett et al. (2014) included five RCTs comparing topical capsaicin 0.025-0.075% versus placebo and one trial comparing Civamide 0.075% (zucapsaicin, cis-isomer of capsaicin, is not available in the U.S.) to placebo.(151) All five RCTs assessing the benefit of topical capsaicin were relatively small (n=475) and of short duration (<3 months). Four were already a decade old when this data was used in the previous CPG evidence review. Among the studies included in Laslett et al. (2014),(151) Deal et al. (1991),(152) Altman et al. (1994),(153) and Kosuwon et al. (2010)(154) enrolled patients with OA of the knee. McCarthy et al. (1992)(155) enrolled patients with OA of the hand and McCleane (2000)(156) enrolled patients with OA in mixed joints (e.g., knee, shoulder, hand, hip). Evidence from the SR supports at least a moderate effect of capsaicin (SMD= 0.44) in reducing moderate pain associated with OA.(151) Studies included in the SR evaluated the benefit of capsaicin as monotherapy, with background acetaminophen or acetaminophen as an adjunctive treatment for improving pain associated with OA. AEs of capsaicin were local (i.e., burning, stinging, and pain at the application site) and reported in 35100% of patients randomized to capsaicin, which resolved or declined in most patients with continued use. No systematic AEs were reported with capsaicin.

The new data collected as part of the 2025 OA CPG update did not reveal any significant new findings that would affect the previous recommendation for treatment of KOA with topical capsaicin. Thus, the above recommendation is based on evidence from the previous review. Given that there was no evidence for or against use of topical capsaicin for HOA in the previous or most recent evidence review, it was decided to eliminate the previous recommendation addressing use of capsaicin for HOA. The Work Group determined that the benefits of use outweighed the harms, which was why the recommendation was maintained as a _Weak for_ supporting the use of topical capsaicin for KOA.

Topical capsaicin provides an alternative means of pain control for patients that would prefer to avoid systemic therapy. Given that it does carry the risk of application site burning, and that the overall effect size from use is small, it was decided to maintain a _Weak for_ recommendation supporting its use for KOA.

Patients and caregivers should be educated regarding the potential benefits of capsaicin treatment, the limited harms, the importance of adherence to therapy since an adequate trial may take up to 2-4 weeks; to wash their hands thoroughly after use; and to avoid contact with irritated skin, eyes, or mucous membranes. Although capsaicin does not possess systemic AEs and may be preferred in patients with multiple comorbidities who need to limit the use of oral therapies for

OA, some patients may be unable to tolerate the AEs. However, the local AEs resolve with continued use.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation, primarily from the Laslett et al. (2014)(151) systematic review cited in the previous CPG. The previous recommendation was modified to reflect that its use is not necessarily limited to only pain relief. Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, Amended_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Low. The body of evidence had some limitations including the date of collection in some cases over a decade ago, as there was no new evidence identified that meet inclusion criteria for this CPG update. The benefits of topical capsaicin outweighed

the potential harms as Laslett et al. (2014)(151) supports a moderate benefit of topical capsaicin administered 3-4 times daily in reducing pain associated with OA. Patient values and preferences had some variation, with some preferring to use nonsystemic therapy options, and some having concerns for efficacy with topical treatment compared to systemic. There is also a common side effect noted with use of topical capsaicin of burning at the application site. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation for capsaicin use for KOA.

#### **_Recommendation_**

11. For osteoarthritis of the knee, we suggest topical non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. **(Weak for | Reviewed, New-replaced)**

#### **_Discussion_**

Topical NSAIDs are a common first-line measure of pharmacotherapy for mild KOA, with availability increasing to the public since it was approved for distribution without a prescription in the U.S. in 2020. Topical diclofenac is the most commonly available form of topical NSAID and has been shown to have between 5-to-17-fold lower systemic absorption when compared to oral NSAIDs. The most commonly noted adverse effect with use is dry skin at the application site; this favorable safety profile, is why it is frequently trialed by patients before systemic agents.(157)

The Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation for topical NSAIDs for patients with OA of the knee, which changed from the previous _Strong for_ recommendation. This was based on new evidence comparing topical to oral pharmacotherapy, which came from one SR and one NMA. Zeng et al. (2021)(158) also showed that patients assigned to topical NSAIDs had improved function at 4 weeks, but no improvement in function at 12 weeks when compared to oral acetaminophen. Topical NSAIDs also had lower gastrointestinal (GI) adverse effects, no difference in cardiovascular (CV) adverse effects, and no difference in pain reduction in comparison to oral acetaminophen. Wolff et al. (2021)(159) showed that patients assigned to oral diclofenac had greater functional improvement when compared to topical diclofenac, but no difference in pain reduction. The study also showed that there was no difference in pain reduction when comparing topical ketoprofen to oral celecoxib.

This recommendation considered that many patients express interest in avoiding systemic agents for pain control due to concern for risk of potential adverse effects.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation based on the Wolff et al. (2021) and Zeng et al. (2021) SR and NMA (158-159). These studies showed mixed evidence of comparable efficacy vs lower efficacy of topical NSAIDs when compared to oral

NSAIDs and oral acetaminophen when assessing pain reduction and functional improvement, but lower incidence of adverse GI effects.(158,159) The strength of the recommendation was changed from _Strong for_ to _Weak for_ in this updated guideline. Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-Replaced_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low. This was because of serious limitations identified in the included studies, including high risk of bias, short duration of trials (typically only 4 to 12 weeks), and inadequately reported key design elements. This included randomization, allocation concealment, and blinding of participants and outcome assessors. The included NMA also incorporated large numbers of poorquality trials, further reducing confidence in pooled estimates. The benefits of topical NSAIDs for KOA outweighed the potential harm of adverse skin reactions associated with use. Patient values and preferences were similar because many patients prefer to avoid systemic agents for pain reduction when possible. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

### **_b. Oral Pharmacotherapy_**

#### **_Recommendation_**

12. For osteoarthritis of the hip and knee, we suggest oral non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs or acetaminophen.

**(Weak for | Reviewed, Amended)**

#### **_Discussion_**

Oral therapies with NSAIDs and acetaminophen remain a backbone of therapy and a key oral medication option for OA. Effect sizes are generally small and need to be weighed against the known harms of these medications. We address three key areas in this narrative, comparative efficacy data, foundational data for NSAID use, and foundational data for acetaminophen use. Each will be discussed in detail here.(160-171)

This evidence was generated from the key question “What is the comparative safety and effectiveness of oral pharmacotherapy for the treatment of OA of the hip and knee?” and includes analysis of the comparative efficacy of these agents as well as the evidence from the previous version of this guideline addressing their overall efficacy vs. placebo. The overall strength of most comparisons was very low with low ratings due to limitations in the methodological quality of the included RCTs, indirectness of evidence derived from NMAs, and imprecision of the results. Overall, this comparative efficacy data is mixed and does not favor one agent over another. In addition to these concerns the Work Group acknowledges the potential for publication bias.(160- 171)

#### **_Comparative Efficacy_**

Comparative effectiveness evidence for various pharmacological interventions used for OA was primarily drawn from a NMA by Beaudart et al. (2020).(160) Across nearly all comparisons, the overall GRADE of evidence is very low due to serious limitations, indirect comparisons, and imprecision. Individual comparisons are discussed in detail below.

Various comparisons were made for NSAIDs vs. acetaminophen. Specifically, comparisons between celecoxib, diclofenac, and naproxen against acetaminophen (or paracetamol) generally showed no statistically significant difference for the critical outcomes of function and pain at the 6- week follow-up period. Celecoxib vs. acetaminophen: function (SMD= 0.21) and pain (SMD= -

0.08) did not differ between the groups. Diclofenac vs. acetaminophen: function (SMD= -0.11) and pain (SMD= 0.40) did not differ between the groups. Naproxen vs. acetaminophen/paracetamol: function (SMD= 0.19) and pain (SMD= -0.14) did not differ between the groups. However, the comparison involving piroxicam suggested that it may be less effective than acetaminophen for function. Piroxicam vs. acetaminophen: Acetaminophen had a greater improvement in function scores (SMD= 1.17; 95% CI: 2.05 to 0.29). Piroxicam was also noted to have poorer functional outcomes compared to placebo. Pain did not differ between the two groups.(160)

When considering direct NSAID comparisons, head-to-head comparisons among celecoxib, diclofenac, naproxen, and piroxicam generally showed no difference in pain outcomes, but highlighted several functional differences where piroxicam performed less well. Celecoxib, diclofenac, and naproxen: When compared against each other (diclofenac vs. celecoxib, naproxen vs. celecoxib, and naproxen vs. diclofenac), function and pain did not differ among these three agents at 6 weeks. Piroxicam vs. celecoxib: The celecoxib group had a greater improvement in function scores (SMD= 0.96), though pain did not differ. Piroxicam vs. diclofenac: diclofenac showed a greater improvement in both function scores (SMD= 1.28) and pain scores (SMD= -0.67) compared to piroxicam. This specific comparison of function was the only outcome rated as low quality evidence. Piroxicam vs. naproxen: naproxen had a greater improvement in function scores (SMD= 0.98), while pain did not differ between the two.(160)

#### **_Non-steroidal Anti-inflammatory Drugs_**

Oral NSAIDs are commonly prescribed for OA of the hip or knee and are generally well tolerated. The long- term use of NSAIDs is limited by adverse events (e.g., increased CV events, gastric and duodenal ulceration and perforation, bleeding, and renal impairment). The risks of these complications increase with age, drug-drug and drug-disease interactions, and duration and quantity of use.(161) These risks are especially concerning in the patient population typically affected by OA, who tend to be older, often struggle with other chronic comorbidities, and may be vulnerable to the effects of polypharmacy. Alternative therapies to reduce these risks are limited.

The suggestion to consider NSAIDs for managing pain associated with hip and knee OA is supported by three SRs with NMAs for effectiveness and two SRs with meta-analyses focusing on safety.(162-166) The SR by Osani et al. (2019) focused on NSAID safety and also examined the durability of symptom relief.(165) Additionally, the Puljak et al. (2017) SR compared the effectiveness and safety of celecoxib versus placebo and other NSAIDs.(167) The SR by da Costa et al. (2017)(n=58,451) included 76 trials studying the use of NSAIDs or acetaminophen and found that all active treatments improved pain associated with OA of the hip or knee versus placebo.(163) However, not all treatments met the predefined criteria for a minimally important difference (MID) to be considered clinically significant.(163) Of the interventions meeting the criteria for MID (i.e., rofecoxib 25-50 milligram [mg], etoricoxib 30-90 mg, and diclofenac 150 mg), only diclofenac is available in the U.S. Comparatively, maximum daily doses of naproxen and ibuprofen had an estimated 80% probability of meeting the MID for pain reduction.

The SR by Stewart et al. (2018) evaluated 29 studies of commonly prescribed analgesics (e.g., NSAIDs [n=9 studies], cyclooxygenase-2 [COX-2 inhibitors] [n=9 studies]).(162) The relative reduction in pain from baseline was 34.3% for oral nonselective NSAIDs and 36.9% for COX-2 inhibitors. Both nonselective NSAIDs and COX-2 inhibitors performed statistically better than acetaminophen, which reduced pain by 32.5% (p<0.001). A SR by Jung et al. (2018) (n=44

studies) evaluated the comparative effectiveness of oral analgesics for KOA.(164) For improving WOMAC pain, etoricoxib ranked the highest, followed by naproxen, acetaminophen, and celecoxib. In a subgroup analysis stratified by baseline severity of pain and radiologic severity of OA, etoricoxib, celecoxib, aceclofenac, and meloxicam or aceclofenac were the highest ranked therapies in the more severe pain group. In the group with mild-to-moderate pain, tramadol, celecoxib, diclofenac, and acetaminophen ranked highest. In Jung et al. (2018), ibuprofen was not included in any of the studies.(164) Rofecoxib, etoricoxib, and aceclofenac are not commercially available in the U.S.

In a SR by Puljak et al. (2017) studying celecoxib for the treatment of OA, celecoxib was slightly superior to placebo and various alternative NSAIDs in improving pain and function.(167) However, because of limited and missing data from several studies and industry sponsorship, the authors expressed strong reservations about their findings.(167) In a SR and meta-analysis by Osani et al. (2019), the durability of pain relief from NSAIDs (measured over 2-26 weeks) in patients with KOA was systematically examined from data collected in 72 clinical trials (n=26,424).(165) Osani et al. (2019) noted a moderate effect of NSAIDs in reducing pain (SMD= 0.43) and improving function in patients with KOA, peaking at two weeks with the magnitude of benefit lessening after approximately eight weeks of treatment.(165) In the same SR, the incidence of GI AEs was statistically higher in the NSAID versus placebo groups at four weeks (RR: 1.38, 95% CI: 1.21 to 1.57).(165) CV AEs were not different between NSAIDs and placebo. Most of the AEs were temporary and minor in severity.

A SR by Curtis et al. (2019) (n=40 trials) examined the safety of COX-2 inhibitors.(166) Overall, drug-related AEs were increased in the COX-2 group versus placebo (RR: 1.21, 95% CI: 1.09 to 1.46). Upper gastrointestinal (UGI) AEs, including dyspepsia, gastritis, and heartburn, were also statistically higher versus placebo (RR: 1.19, 95% CI: 1.03 to 1.38). The risk for hypertension, heart failure, and edema was statistically higher for COX-2 inhibitors versus placebo. However, the risk for hypertension was not statistically significant when rofecoxib was removed from the analysis. Further details on considerations for selecting oral NSAIDs as well as information on NSAIDs and the risks of adverse UGI events, CV events, and renal disease can be found in Appendix I.

#### **_Acetaminophen_**

Acetaminophen has both analgesic and antipyretic effects but lacks potent anti-inflammatory activity. Acetaminophen in usual doses (2-4 g [grams] per day) was previously recognized as the preferred initial choice for the management of OA in most patients. Its use may still be preferred by providers for patients with more mild disease or mild-to-moderate symptoms of OA and in patients at risk for NSAID-induced adverse events (e.g., patients with renal disease, cardiovascular disease [CVD] or at risk for CVD, a history of GI ulcers, those receiving oral anticoagulants or corticosteroids [CS], and the elderly [i.e., aged >65 years]) although recent recommendations from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and voluntary labeling changes suggest a maximum daily dose of 3 g which may impact the overall efficacy.

A NMA by da Costa et al. (2017) and a SR by Leopoldino et al. (2019) showed clinically insignificant differences for acetaminophen versus placebo in reducing pain and improving function in patients with OA of the hip and/or knee.(163,168) There was not a dose-dependent effect for acetaminophen in the SR by Leopoldino et al. (2019).(168) However, in the NMA by da

Costa et al. (2017), there was a statistical improvement in pain and function with maximal doses of acetaminophen (3,900-4,000 mg/day) that was not demonstrated with lower doses.(163) However, differences were not clinically significant regardless of the dose of acetaminophen.(163)

A second SR NMA in KOA only, Jung et al. (2018), noted statistically significant benefits of acetaminophen in reducing pain in patients with mild-moderate pain.(164) In Jung et al. (2018), the benefit of acetaminophen ranked higher than some NSAIDs in patients with mild-to-moderate pain associated with KOA.(164) Jung et al. (2018) did not identify any relevant studies in patients with more severe baseline KOA pain.(164) In another SR and NMA by Stewart et al. (2018), the relative reduction in OA pain from baseline was 32.5% for acetaminophen, similar to NSAIDs.(162) Stewart et al. (2018) found that other classes of medications for knee and hip OA reduced pain by a statistically greater degree than acetaminophen (NSAIDs=34.3%, p=0.035; opioids [mostly tramadol]=35.4%, p=0.001; COX-2 inhibitors=36.9%, p<0.001; topical NSAIDs [knee and hand only]=40.9%, p<0.001).(162) The relative benefit of acetaminophen for reducing pain associated with knee and hip OA was greater in Stewart et al. (2018) than da Costa et al. (2017).(162,163)

Although acetaminophen has long been considered a relatively safe analgesic when taken in usual doses (up to 4,000 mg daily), the risk for acute liver injury and liver failure is increased in patients taking doses >4,000 mg daily.(169,170) In the SR by Leopoldino et al. (2019), the incidence of AEs did not differ between acetaminophen and placebo (RR: 1.01, 95% CI: 0.92 to 1.11).(168) The incidence of SAEs and withdrawal because of AEs between acetaminophen and placebo were not statistically different but the number of events was relatively small and the confidence intervals were wide. Abnormalities in liver function tests (LFTs) were higher with acetaminophen versus placebo (RR: 3.79, 95% CI: 1.94 to 7.39); however, the clinical significance of LFT elevation associated with usual doses of acetaminophen is unclear.

While not included in this CPG’s systematic evidence review and, thus, not considered in determining the strength of recommendation, a SR of observational studies by Roberts et al. (2016) explored rates of mortality and CV, GI, and renal AEs in patients taking usual doses of acetaminophen.(171) A dose-dependent statistical increase in mortality (n=1 study), CV (n=4 studies), GI (n=1 study), and/or renal events (n=3 studies) was observed with acetaminophen use versus no use. Although this suggests a potential risk for AEs, uncontrolled observational studies may represent a channeling bias based on use in selected patients with comorbidities and avoidance of NSAIDs.

Because of the potential for unintentional overdose from taking acetaminophen from multiple sources (e.g. prescription and over the counter (OTC) single and combination products >4,000 mg/day), suggested maximum doses have been reduced to <4,000 mg daily (e.g. 3,000 mg, 3,250 mg, ≤3,900 mg). However, professional discretion can be used if doses up to 4,000 mg daily are deemed necessary for individual patients. Maximum doses of acetaminophen should be limited to the shortest possible duration.

Consideration was given to the benefits vs. harms with benefits slightly outweighing harms. The Work Group notes benefits of improvement in pain and function with evidence suggesting that diclofenac, naproxen, acetaminophen, and celecoxib did not differ in efficacy for pain reduction and function. The potential harms associated with oral pharmacotherapy, which include CV, GI,

and renal with NSAIDS and hepatotoxicity with acetaminophen must be considered when utilizing these agents. Some variation was noted in patient preferences for the use of these agents and with consideration given to known potential harms and multiple daily dosage requirements. The Work Group notes other considerations of cost, acceptability, and subgroup considerations. The cost to the system of oral pharmacotherapy is generally low (most are generic and so low cost per unit, but large volume of use). Individual patient acceptance of any given oral therapy may be limited by side effects or preference, and subgroup consideration plays a large role in managing risk. The risk/benefit profile of various medications may differ depending on an individual patient’s comorbidities (for example, differing CV and GI risks with NSAIDs, pregnancy status, etc.). Finally, the Work Group notes that the efficacy studies for acetaminophen were originally conducted at moderate to high doses of acetaminophen, up to 4g/day. Recent guidance has lowered the maximum daily dose to 3g/day, and this may impact efficacy.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(160- 171) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, Amended_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low. The body of evidence had some limitations including the methodological quality of the included RCTs, indirectness of evidence derived from the single included NMA Beaudart et al. (2020) (55 RCTs in 1 NMA; n=13,633)(160) and imprecision of the results. Overall, this comparative efficacy data is mixed and does not favor one agent over another. The benefits of oral therapies of NSAIDs and acetaminophen improved outcomes of pain and function, based largely on evidence supplied from the prior guideline recommendation and slightly outweighed the potential harm of AEs which requires careful consideration of risk to the patient based on baseline characteristics. Patient values and preferences varied somewhat with consideration given to known potential harms and multiple daily dosages. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

#### **_Recommendation_**

13. For osteoarthritis of the knee, we suggest duloxetine depending upon patient characteristics and preferences.

#### **(Weak for | Reviewed, New-replaced)**

#### **_Discussion_**

The Work Group agreed on a _Weak for_ recommendation for the use of duloxetine in select patients with OA of the knee. Duloxetine is a serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor (SNRI) and FDA indicated for the treatment of major depressive disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, diabetic peripheral neuropathic pain, fibromyalgia, and chronic musculoskeletal pain. The evidence for this recommendation is based on eight RCTs, seven of which were included in the previous evidence review for the 2020 VA/DOD CPG. All studies were ≤18 weeks in duration and evaluated KOA. Only one trial by Wang et al. (2017)(172) also evaluated HOA. Most studies allowed concomitant NSAID use.

A 12-week RCT (n=66) by Illeez et al. (2022) investigated the effects of duloxetine and pregabalin in patients with KOA.(173) Significant improvements were noted in pain, function, QoL, depression, anxiety, and sleep disturbance for both drugs when compared with pre-treatment values. There was no statistically significant difference between the duloxetine and pregabalin groups in Short Form-36 (SF-36) bodily pain scores (duloxetine mean [M]) (standard

deviation [SD])= 63.75(29.41) vs. pregabalin M(SD)= 66.83(25.02); p=0.911), VAS daytime scores (duloxetine M(SD)= 2.7(2.1) vs. pregabalin M(SD)= 2.1(2.12); p=0.179), VAS nighttime scores (duloxetine M(SD)= 2.6(2.14) vs. pregabalin M(SD)= 1.9(2.02); p=0.152), or Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) scores for sleep (duloxetine M(SD)= 6.27(5.09) vs. pregabalin M(SD)= 5.9(3.52); p=0.522). Pregabalin was associated with better WOMAC pain (duloxetine M(SD)= 7.2(5.61) vs. pregabalin M(SD)= 4.2(4.29); p=0.020) and WOMAC function scores (duloxetine M(SD)= 22.93(17.08) vs. pregabalin M(SD)=14.5(14.98); p=0.043) than duloxetine. Patients randomized to duloxetine had higher baseline BMIs which may partially explain some of the better outcomes noted with pregabalin. Fewer side effects were noted in the duloxetine-treated group than in the pregabalin-treated group.

Six RCTs evaluated in a SR by Chen et al. (2019) (n=2,059) demonstrated that duloxetine achieved significant reductions in pain outcomes, including 24-hour pain scores, weekly 24-hour average pain scores, and a ≥50% reduction in weekly mean pain scores when compared to placebo at 10-16 weeks follow-up.(174) The same SR found a statistically significant improvement in physical function for patients in the duloxetine treatment arm at 10-16 weeks when compared to placebo. Five of the RCTs suggested that duloxetine increases the incidence of mild treatmentemergent AEs (e.g., GI toxicity, sexual dysfunction, mild insomnia).(174)

The evidence review only included short-term studies (≤18 weeks) of duloxetine’s safety and efficacy in managing pain associated with OA; as such, one must consider the risk of discontinuation syndrome after long-term use.(174,175) Patients should be educated that the medication is to be taken daily (not as needed) and discontinued only after close consultation with their prescribing provider. Slow tapering will almost always be required. Duloxetine prescriptions should start at doses ≤30 mg daily and be increased to a goal of 60 mg daily. If a patient is taking another class of antidepressant medication (e.g., selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor [SSRI]) for a comorbid mood disorder, providers should consider transitioning to an equivalent dose of duloxetine using a cross-tapering strategy to avoid the risk of serotonin syndrome. As an initial consideration for providers, duloxetine therapy should not be initiated if a patient’s estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) is determined to be <30 milliliters (ml)/minute (min)/1.73 m2 . Of note, no other literature was reviewed looking at the use of different SNRIs for the treatment of OA of the hip or the knee.

While not included in our CPG evidence review, Leite et al. (2025) conducted a SR and metaanalysis which included the eight aforementioned duloxetine studies and one additional study on hand OA. This review found duloxetine 60 mg/day to be superior to placebo in pain and function in adults over the age of 40.(176)

In practice, duloxetine will likely be used as an adjunctive or alternative treatment after a trial of nonpharmacologic therapies and NSAIDs. It may be considered for earlier use in select patients with comorbid conditions (e.g., depression, anxiety, fibromyalgia, neuropathic pain) and in patients with central sensitization of pain. It is worth noting that the prevalence of depression is two to three times higher in patients with OA.(173) A large variation in patient values and preferences is likely given this medication’s development for and primary use as an antidepressant, need for scheduled administration, side effect profile (e.g., sexual side effects) and withdrawal potential.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(172-176) Therefore, it is categorized as a _Reviewed, New-replaced_ recommendation. The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of evidence was Very low. The body of evidence had a few limitations, including that most RCTs were short-term. This intervention’s benefits, including improvement in pain and function, slightly outweighed the small potential for harm. A large variation in patient values and preferences is likely given this medication’s classification as an antidepressant and its side effect profile. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

#### **_Recommendation_**

14. For osteoarthritis of the hip and knee, we suggest against initiating opioids (including tramadol). For patients already on long-term opioid therapy, refer to the current VA/DOD CPG for the Use of Opioids in the Management of Chronic Pain.**

#### **(Weak against | Reviewed, Not changed)**

#### **_Discussion_**

Over the past two decades, increasing recognition of opioid-related harms has led to a reassessment of opioid prescribing for chronic non-cancer pain. Contemporary guidelines have emphasized multimodal and non-opioid approaches to pain management to improve safety; this includes the 2020 OA CPG, which suggested that opioids (including tramadol) offer no clinically meaningful advantage for pain related to OA of the hip or knee. In a recent comparative effectiveness study, Thorlund et al. (2022) confirm that the analgesic effect of opioids was equivalent to NSAIDs for KOA, translating to a 0.3-mm difference on a 100-mm VAS, which was below the threshold of clinical importance.(177)

All active treatments in the NMA produced only small to moderate improvements vs controls, and differences between interventions were not clinically relevant. Additionally, confidence in these estimates was low due to study limitations and indirectness.

These findings align with prior SRs by da Costa et al. (2014), Fuggle et al. (2019), and Toupin et al. (2019) which compared various opioids to no treatment, placebo, or active control for the treatment of hip or knee OA.(178-180) These studies demonstrated that opioids reduce pain intensity more than placebo but with relatively small effect sizes.

Similarly, treatment with opioids (except for tapentadol) improved physical function more than placebo, but the improvements were relatively small (KOA [SMD: -0.22, 95% CI: -0.41 to -0.04], HOA [SMD: -0.33, 95% CI: -0.93 to 0.28], and knee and hip OA combined [SMD: -0.29, 95% CI: - 0.38 to -0.20]). Opioids also consistently produced higher rates of AEs, including withdrawal symptoms, across all trials that were studied.

In a SR and meta-analysis of seven RCTs by Fuggle et al. (2019), tramadol was also associated with a higher risk of AEs compared to placebo.(179) A RCT by Bianchi et al. (2003) compared tramadol to acetaminophen and showed no statistically significant differences in pain reduction or study withdrawal due to AEs at seven days.(181)

> ** See the VA/DOD Clinical Practice Guideline for the Use of Opioids in the Management of Chronic Pain. Available at: https://www.healthquality.va.gov/guidelines/pain/cot/

RCTs by Pavelka et al. (1998)(182) Beaulieu et al. (2008)(183) and DeLemos et al. (2011)(184) compared NSAIDs to tramadol at 4 and 12 weeks demonstrating that NSAIDs reduce pain and improve function over tramadol. Tramadol was also associated with more AEs and more study withdrawals than NSAIDs.

Given the lack of clinically meaningful benefits and well-recognized risks of opioid therapy (including misuse, overdose, dependence, endocrine dysfunction, sleep-disordered breathing, falls, and fractures), the Work Group did not change the 2020 OA CPG recommendation.

However, the Work Group acknowledged that severe knee and/or hip pain, that has not responded to non-pharmacologic and non-opioid therapies, represents a particularly challenging subgroup. In such cases, opioid therapy may be considered on a selected basis, using a patientcentered approach that prioritizes safety when anticipated benefits outweigh risks. If pursued, treatment with opioids should be restricted to refractory, severe pain after failure of first-line strategies and administered at the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration feasible. The Work Group further recommended reevaluation of on-going therapy focusing on benefits, risks, and continued need.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(177-184) Therefore, it is categorized as a _Reviewed, Not changed_ recommendation. Confidence in the evidence was Low due to limited active-comparator or head-to-head trials and the predominance of short-term studies. Although opioids demonstrated modest short-term improvements in pain and function compared with placebo, the durability of these effects is unknown, and the observed benefits are unlikely to be clinically significant. In contrast, substantial risks, including withdrawal, misuse, opioid use disorder, overdose, endocrine dysfunction, sleep-disordered breathing, and falls were documented. Given this unfavorable risk-benefit profile, the Work Group suggested against initiating opioids for hip or knee OA, while acknowledging that selected patients may warrant consideration. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak against_ recommendation.

### **_c. Intra-articular Injections_**

#### **_Recommendation_**

15. For patients with persistent pain due to osteoarthritis of the knee inadequately relieved by other interventions, we suggest an intra-articular corticosteroid injection. **(Weak for | Reviewed, Amended)**

16. For patients with persistent pain due to osteoarthritis of the hip inadequately relieved by other interventions, we suggest an image-guided intra-articular, corticosteroid injection. **(Weak for | Reviewed, Amended)**

#### **_Discussion_**

Intra-articular corticosteroids injections (CSIs) are commonly used as a non-surgical intervention for OA, aimed at reducing synovial inflammation and providing symptomatic pain relief. Given their widespread availability, rapid onset of action, and frequent use in clinical practice, particularly for patients who are not candidates for or are awaiting more definitive interventions, the Work Group evaluated CSIs as a potential recommendation despite uncertainty regarding durability of benefit

and long-term outcomes. A small RCT by Paskins et al. (2022) compared ultrasound-guided intraarticular injections of triamcinolone and lidocaine vs placebo (lidocaine alone) for HOA and found short-term improvement in function at 2 and 4 months, but with no sustained benefit at 6 months.(185) A SR by Mojica et al. (2022) which included 33 RCTs comparing CS and placebo for KOA, similarly demonstrated clinically meaningful short-term improvements in function that regressed towards baseline over time.(186) In contrast, a small RCT by Malone et al. (2024) compared extended-release CS for KOA and found no clinically important difference from placebo.(187) A SR by Pereira et al. (2025) included 10 RCTs comparing CS to placebo for either hip or knee OA, reported heterogenous and largely neutral findings, including significant pain effect favoring placebo over betamethasone at 12 weeks, but no significant difference between methylprednisolone and placebo.(188) Overall, these studies suggest that intra-articular CSIs provide modest short-term functional benefit in some patients, but most highquality evidence demonstrates no sustained advantage over placebo.

When comparing differences in SAEs for CS compared to placebo. Paskins et al. (2022) compared triamcinolone and placebo for HOA and reported one possible treatment-related SAE in the CS group (death from subacute bacterial endocarditis), while two members of the CS group and two members of the control group had non-treatment-related SAEs.(185) Malone et al. (2024) compared extended-release CS and placebo for KOA and found no significant difference in SAEs between intervention and control.(187) A SR with NMA found no clear difference in SAEs between CS and placebo.(188)

Only one study, a RCT by Paskins et al. (2022), compared CS with placebo and incorporated a measure of pain interference, finding a significant improvement in the Stanford Presenteeism Scale for the intervention group compared to the control group at two months but not at six months.(185)

The CPG systematic evidence review did not identify any new SRs or RCTs examining important outcomes identified by the Work Group (sleep and radiographic change). Additionally, the CPG systematic evidence review did not identify any new SRs or RCTs examining if CS should be avoided in the 3 months preceding knee or hip joint replacement surgery. However, it is common practice to delay surgical intervention with recent CSIs to the hip or knee.

Despite general consistency in the evidence supporting CS for knee and hip pain associated with OA, patient preferences and clinician practices vary. The Patient Focus Group noted inconsistencies in clinician practice regarding the frequency and total number of CSIs (some received injections every 3 months, while others received one injection throughout their lifetimes). Additionally, the Work Group and Patient Focus Group noted that some clinicians heavily relied on CSIs when a patient did not qualify for more invasive treatments. The Work Group recognized other implications, including resource use, with considerable variability in products (short versus extended release) available on the market at varying costs, variable clinician skill levels, the general need for imaging availability for hip intra-articular injections, and the increased feasibility of CSIs.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to these recommendations.(185- 188) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, Amended_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low. The body of evidence had some limitations, including risk of

bias (185,188), a single RCT with a low number of events (185,187), and a lack of analysis details.(186,187) The benefits of intra-articular CSI to the hip or knee for OA primarily improve short-term pain, are accessible to almost all clinicians, and easy to perform compared to other interventions and procedures, slightly outweigh the potential harms of generally low SAEs, lack of demonstrated disease-modifying effects (i.e., prevention of structural progression or reversal of cartilage damage), and no clear long-term or sustained improvement in pain or functioning. Patient values and preferences varied somewhat due to varying levels of understanding, different products on the market, and potential avoidance of injections/needles or a desire for less invasive interventions. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

#### **_Discussion_**

Viscosupplementation injections (VSIs) (which refers to all synthetic HA preparations) are intended to improve joint lubrication in the setting of OA.(186,188-191) The CPG systematic evidence review identified 5 new SRs or RCTs examining the intervention of HA in hip or knee OA.

(186,189) Migliorini et al. (2024) found no significant improvement in WOMAC compared with placebo at either 2-4 or 5-8 weeks.(189) Mojica et al. For HOA, the evidence base was more limited. Kubo et al. Patient understanding of VSI products, prior treatment experience, and willingness to pursue repeat or procedure-based interventions may also vary. ## **F. Orthobiologics**

#### **_Recommendation_**

20. For patients with persistent pain due to knee osteoarthritis, there is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against platelet-rich plasma. **(Neither for nor against | Reviewed, Amended)**

#### **_Discussion_**

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) is an autologous blood-derived product created by processing whole blood to concentrate platelets into a small volume of plasma at levels above baseline. Following injection and activation, these concentrated platelets release growth factors and signaling molecules that are believed to help modulate inflammation, support tissue healing, and potentially facilitate repair processes within damaged tissues. Given the limited number of effective non-surgical treatment options available for patients with knee and hip OA, therapies such as PRP have generated considerable interest among clinicians and patients seeking additional strategies to improve pain, function, and QoL.

The Work Group recognized that PRP has a biologically plausible mechanism of action; however, its primary focus was on patient-centered outcomes, including pain, function, QoL, and safety. In one SR of 42 RCTs comparing PRP with HA, Xu et al. (2025) reported improvements in function, QoL, and pain favoring PRP, although PRP did not consistently outperform HA by a statistically significant margin.(197) Similarly, Pelluri et al.(2025), in a SR of 21 RCTs, found that PRP may improve function compared with CSI and placebo, with subgroup analyses suggesting potential benefit for both leukocyte-rich (LR) and leukocyte-poor (LP) PRP and a possible signal favoring LR preparations.(198) The Work Group acknowledged that some reported improvements in pain and function approached or met minimum clinically important difference (MCID) thresholds.

However, confidence in these findings was limited by several important methodological and clinical concerns. The study populations were generally younger, had lower BMI, and had earlier-stage KOA than many patients treated in VA and DOD settings, limiting generalizability. In addition, blinding was likely incomplete in many trials because of visible or physical differences between PRP and comparator injections, creating the potential for performance bias, expectation effects, or inadvertent signaling. Review of the individual RCTs also suggested that studies at higher risk of bias tended to report larger treatment effects. This concern was supported by a NMA within the evidence base, which found that higher-risk PRP trials produced treatment effects approximately four times larger than the single lower-risk PRP trial.(188) Further, substantial heterogeneity in PRP preparation, dosing protocols, comparators, patient selection, and outcome assessment further reduced confidence in the pooled estimates. Taken together, these limitations suggest that the apparent benefits of PRP may be overstated, and the current evidence remains insufficient to support a recommendation for its use in knee or hip OA.

In addition, patient values and preferences varied, with some patients favoring minimally invasive procedures while others preferred more definitive surgical intervention. Nevertheless, most patients expressed interest in having additional treatment options available to improve pain and function. These considerations must also be viewed within the context of broader feasibility and implementation challenges across the VA and DOD healthcare systems. Important practical considerations include the lack of standardized PRP formulations and dosing protocols, the need for specialized equipment and staff training, and variable treatment availability across VA and DOD facilities. Compared with other non-surgical interventional procedures, PRP may also result in higher costs to both patients and the healthcare system, while utilization patterns and the economic impact of widespread adoption remain uncertain. In addition, adverse effects were reported more frequently with PRP compared with placebo;

although, these events were generally mild, self-limited, and consistent with the expected physiologic response to the intervention.

The Work Group systematically reviewed the evidence related to this recommendation. (188,197,198) Therefore, it is categorized _Reviewed, Amended_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of evidence was rated as Very low due to substantial heterogeneity across studies, including variability in methodology, outcome measures, dosing protocols, and PRP formulations.(198) Additional limitations included inconsistent stratification by OA severity and significant variability in reported clinical outcomes.(197) Although some studies suggested potential improvements in pain, function, and QoL, these potential benefits were weighed against the risks of post-injection pain, inflammation, and uncertainty regarding the durability and generalizability of treatment effects. Consideration was also given to the variability in patient preferences, as some individuals favor less invasive treatment options whereas others prefer more definitive interventions. Given the limitations of the available evidence and the balance of potential benefits and harms, the Work Group decided upon a _Neither for nor against_ recommendation.

21. For osteoarthritis of the hip, there is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against platelet-rich plasma injections.

**(Neither for nor against | Reviewed, Amended)**

#### **_Discussion_**

The Work Group systematically reviewed the evidence for PRP use in HOA. A SR by Mirghaderi et al. (2025),(199) found no difference in function outcomes on Harris Hip Score (HHS) or WOMAC scores comparing PRP versus HA or comparing PRP+HA versus HA alone in patients with HOA across 6 total RCTs. NSAIDs are avoided around the time of PRP treatment because they inhibit platelet activation and suppress the inflammatory signaling necessary for platelet-derived growth factors to stimulate tissue repair. #### **_Recommendation_**

22. For osteoarthritis of the knee, there is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against the following intra-articular injections (listed alphabetically):

 - Amniotic tissue allograft suspension (e.g. BioDRestore™)

 - Autologous conditioned serum

 - Dextrose prolotherapy

 - Micro fragmented adipose tissue

 - Plasma rich in growth factor

 - Polynucleotides

 - Stem cell injections (e.g., mesenchymal, adipose- derived, and bone marrowderived)

#### **(Neither for nor against | Reviewed, New-replaced)**

#### **_Discussion_**

Orthobiologic interventions are increasingly utilized for musculoskeletal conditions and are commonly described using regenerative or stem cell-based terminology. However, most currently available orthobiologic preparations are believed to exert their proposed effects primarily through paracrine signaling, immunomodulation, and modulation of the local inflammatory environment rather than through direct tissue engraftment or durable structural regeneration.

Most orthobiologics preparations share these theoretical mechanisms, paracrine signaling (secretion of cytokines/growth factors rather than tissue engraftment), immunomodulation (shifting pro- vs anti-inflammatory balance), matrix/scaffold support (providing extracellular matrix fragments), controlled inflammatory stimulation (prolotherapy model), and limited true regeneration evidence (differentiation claims exceed current in-vivo data).

Additionally, substantial heterogeneity exists in product sourcing, processing methods, cellular composition, concentration of bioactive factors, and administration techniques. These factors limit mechanistic consistency and complicate interpretation and generalizability of findings across studies.

#### **_Autologous Conditioned Serum_**

Autologous conditioned serum (ACS) is derived from a patient’s own blood incubated with a stimulatory surface to increase anti-inflammatory cytokines, particularly interleukin-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1RA). Its primary effect is reduction of IL-1 mediate cartilage catabolism. ACS functions primarily through anti-inflammatory modulation rather than tissue regeneration.

#### **_Amniotic Tissue Allograft Suspension_**

Amniotic tissue allograft suspension is placenta membranes or fluid from donor tissue. The primary mechanism is that it contains extracellular matrix proteins, growth factors, and antiinflammatory mediators, acts as a biologic scaffold and paracrine signaling source, theoretical

immunomodulatory and antifibrotic effects, and most commercially processed products do not contain viable stem cells, with the key concept being scaffold + signaling, not true stem cell therapy in most preparations.

Allograft suspensions are sourced from donor cartilage, bone, or connective tissues micronized or suspended. The primary mechanism is that it provides matrix fragments and biochemical signals intended to stimulate host repair, no cellular engraftment expected, and works through host-mediated remodeling, not donor tissue integration, with the key concept being structural + signaling support, not cell replacement.

Evidence from one RCT by Gomoll et al. (2021)(200) with 200 patients suggests amniotic suspension allograft (ASA) improved function better than either HA or saline however the same study found no difference in safety and SAEs between ASA and either HA or saline for safety/SAEs. Additionally, evidence from one RCT in one NMA by Pereira et al. (2025) suggests that ASA improves function more than placebo.(188) Both of these RCTs were limited by very serious methodological limitations and serious imprecision.

In 1 RCT by Pill et al. (2025)(201) comparing an amniotic tissue allograft (BioDRestore™, a morcellized injectable amniotic tissue allograft) versus steroid KOOS symptom score, KOOS activities of daily living (ADL) score, Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation (SANE) score, and Lysholm score for function improved in the BioDRestore™ group. The study findings suggest that KOOS pain score improved, but no difference was found in the VAS score. This study findings suggest that KOOS QoL score improved in the BioDRestore™ group, but no difference was found in the Veterans RAND 12-Item Health Survey (VR-12) physical component score (PCS) or mental component score (MCS). Interpretation of the results is limited by very serious methodological limitations, serious imprecision, and a small sample size (n=81).

#### **_Dextrose Prolotherapy_**

Dextrose prolotherapy is a hypertonic dextrose injection. The primary mechanism is that it creates a localized osmotic irritation to mild inflammatory cascade, intended to trigger fibroblast proliferation and collagen deposition, primarily used for ligament/tendon laxity models, not cartilage regeneration, with the key concept being controlled inflammatory stimulus to induce connective tissue strengthening.

No difference between Dextrose prolotherapy and all other treatments for function was found in one SR by Khateri et al. (2024)(202) including 11 RCTs. Evidence from six studies in one SR by Arias-Vazquez et al. (2022)(203) found hypertonic dextrose prolotherapy improved function better than HA (Effect size [SMD]: -1.31 (-1.05 [95% CI: -2.03 to -0.08]) and P-value: 0.03). No difference was found between dextrose prolotherapy and HA in this SR. Both SRs have very serious limitations in study quality and risk-of-bias, and serious imprecision.

#### **_Micro-fragmented Adipose Tissue_**

Micro-fragmented adipose tissue (MFAT) is a patient’s lipoaspirate mechanically processed. The primary mechanism of MFAT is that it contains stromal vascular fraction (SVF) including mesenchymal stem/stromal cells (MSCs), pericytes, and cytokines, function largely through paracrine signaling rather than differentiation, anti-inflammatory and angiogenic modulations are

primary theoretical effects. The key concept of MFAT is the cell-assisted anti-inflammatory signaling more than cartilage rebuilding.

Evidence from 75 patients in 1 RCT by Richter et al. (2025)(204) suggests that MFAT improved function more than CSI or saline. This RCT has very serious limitations in study quality and riskof-bias, and serious imprecision.

#### **_Plasma Rich in Growth Factor_**

PRP/plasma rich in growth factors (PRGF) is sourced by centrifuged autologous blood. The primary mechanism is the concentrated platelet α-granules release platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), promotes angiogenesis, chemotaxis, and matrix synthesis signaling, LR vs LP formulations alter inflammatory profile. The key concept being growth-factor mediated signaling, not stem cell therapy.

Evidence from 3 RCTs in 1 SR by Migliorini et al. (2025)(205) showed mixed results for PRGF vs. HA for function. This SR demonstrated serious imprecision.

#### **_Polynucleotides_**

Polynucleotides (PN)/polydeoxyribonucleotides (PDRN) are sourced by purified deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) fragments, often derived from salmon. The primary mechanism involves adenosine receptor subtype 2A (A2A) activation, resulting in anti-inflammatory effects and promotion of fibroblast activity and angiogenesis. The therapy contains no cellular component and acts through biomechanical modulation. The key concept being receptormediated anti-inflammatory and tissue support signaling.

Among 60 patients in one RCT by Kim et al. (2023)(206) comparing intra-articular polynucleotide (IA PN) to high molecular weight hyaluronic acid (HMWHA), no difference was noted for function or safety. Evidence from another RCT by Stagni et al. (2021)(207) suggests Polynucleotides Highly Purified Technology (PN-HPT) and HA improved function better than HA alone. These RCTs have very serious limitations in study quality, risk-of-bias, and serious imprecision.

#### **_Stem Cell Injections_**

Allogeneic umbilical cord derived allografts are donor derived biological products containing extracellular matrix components and bioactive molecules that may modulate inflammation and support tissue repair through paracrine signaling.

Adipose-derived stem/stromal cell injections are sourced by enzymatically or mechanically isolated SVF. The primary mechanism is that immunomodulation via secretion of interleukin-10 (IL-10), TGF-β, VEGF, etc., potential chondrogenic differentiation is theorized but not consistently demonstrated in vivo, predominantly paracrine trophic support rather than direct tissue replacement, with the key concept being immune modulation plus trophic signaling to structural regeneration.

Bone marrow aspirate concentrate (BMAC) is sourced from iliac crest bone marrow aspirate, centrifuged. The primary mechanism of BMAC is that it contains MSCs, hematopoietic cells, platelets, and cytokines, functions through paracrine signaling and immune modulation, true stem cell differentiation into cartilage is theoretical and limited in clinical evidence. The key concept being mixed cellular plus growth factor milieu with signaling predominance.

In a SR comparing stem cells to placebo, evidence from 7 RCTs in one SR by Whittle et al. (2025)(208) suggests that stem cells improve function better than placebo at up to 6 months, but at later time points no between group difference was demonstrated. Four RCTs from this SR suggest that autologous stem cells improve function better than placebo, but there is no difference between allogenic stem cells versus placebo. Seven RCTs from Whittle et al. (2025) did not show any difference between stem cells or placebo for safety or SAEs.(208)

In one SR comparing stem cells to no treatment, usual care (analgesia, weight loss, exercise), a short-term benefit in function was noted, but this effect was not demonstrated at every point between 3 months to 1 year.(208) Evidence from one RCT in one SR by Whittle et al. (2025) was unable to find a difference between stem cells and no treatment/usual care for safety and SAEs.(208)

One RCT in one SR (208) comparing stem cells to glucocorticoids failed to show difference between stem cells and glucocorticoids. Evidence from this study was unable to find a difference between stems cells and glucocorticoids for safety or SAEs.

Evidence from 5-7 RCTs in one SR (208) suggest that stem cell injections are better than HA for improving function after three months. Functional outcomes were assessed at different follow-up time points. 5 RCTs reported function at >3 to 6 months follow-up, and 7 RCTs reported function at >6 months to 1 year follow-up. Five RCTs in this SR failed to show any difference between stem cells and HA for safety or SAEs. (2025)(208) one RCT did not show difference between stem cells and NSAIDs/HA/Physical Therapy/HA for safety or SAEs. This SR had very serious limitations in study quality, risk-of-bias and serious imprecision.

Evidence from 8 RCTs in this SR comparing BMAC versus other treatments including HA was unable to find a difference between BMAC and any other treatment for safety/SAEs. This SR had serious limitations in study quality, and serious imprecision.

In one SR by Dhillon et al. (2025)(210) evidence from 9 RCTs comparing autologous adiposederived mesenchymal stem/stromal cells (ADMSC) versus a variety of other treatments (analgesia, exercise and weight management) suggest that autologous ADMSCs were better than other treatments for improving function. Two RCTs from this SR found mixed results for allogenic ADMSCs for function.

In one SR (210) comparing autologous bone marrow derived (BMD)-MSC versus control, two RCTs found mixed results for function, and no difference for pain interference. One RCT from

Allogeneic umbilical cord derived allografts are donor derived biological products containing extracellular matrix components and bioactive molecules that may modulate inflammation and support tissue repair through paracrine signaling. Evidence from two RCTs in one SR by Dhillon et al. (2025)(210) found mixed results for allogeneic umbilical cord derived analysis versus control for function. 16 RCTs in this SR found no difference in safety/SAEs when comparing all types of stromal cells injections to control. Very serious imprecision was present, with wide confidence intervals in this study.

Six studies in one SR by Ishak-Samrin et al. (2025)(211) suggest Wharton’s jelly MSCs improve function better than placebo versus HA; no difference between Wharton’s jelly MSCs and HA was found for safety/SAEs. Very serious limitations and serious imprecision were present in this SR largely due to reliance on narrative reporting and insufficient quantitative data.

Riggle et al. (2024)(212) in one SR, including studies from 11 RCTs, was unable to find a difference between stem cell and non-stem cell treatments for safety. This SR has very serious limitations in study quality, risk-of-bias, and serious imprecision.

MSC improved function and pain more than comparator treatments in one SR by Xie et al. (2024).(213) The grade of evidence for these outcomes was moderate. This study has serious limitations in quality.

Mixed results were found in one NMA by Zhang et al. (2022)(214) including 10 studies comparing stem cells versus cell-free methods for safety/SAEs. This NMA has serious limitations in study quality, and serious imprecision.

#### **_Summary of Findings for Stem Cell Injections_**

Studies provided very low-quality evidence. Benefits and harms appear balanced with potential improvements in pain and function. AEs may include post injection pain, inflammation, or infection. Patient preferences vary with some expressing ethical concerns or reservations about experimental treatments. Some patients express interest in biologic or orthobiologic injections when conventional options offer limited relief. Others prioritize interventions with robust safety and efficacy evidence and may be less willing to pursue emerging

therapies. Additional considerations include high cost, limited insurance coverage, treatment availability, and the need for guidance methods such as US or fluoroscopy for needle placement. Feasibility and resource considerations also influenced this recommendation. Orthobiologic products are not widely available in VA/DOD clinical settings, and access may require referral outside the health system, potentially introducing cost burden or inequity. Subgroup considerations for Veterans and Service Members include high rates of joint overuse, traumatic joint injury, and physically demanding occupational roles; however, the included studies did not address these specific populations, limiting applicability. Exploratory mechanistic studies were not included in the evidence review and therefore did not influence the strength of this recommendation.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(188,200- 214) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-replaced_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low. The body of evidence had some limitations including small samples. The benefits of intra-articular orthobiologics were pain relief and improvement in function balanced with the potential harm of post-injection pain and inflammation which was small. Patient values and preferences

varied somewhat because some patients may have ethical concerns with stem cell treatment and experimental treatments. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Neither for nor against_ recommendation.

## **G. Interventional Procedures**

#### **_Recommendation_**

24. For the treatment of osteoarthritis of the knee, we suggest against geniculate artery embolization.

#### **(Weak against | Reviewed, New-added)**

#### **_Discussion_**

Geniculate artery embolization has been proposed as a non-surgical, procedural intervention for the treatment of osteoarthritis of the knee. Geniculate artery embolization is a minor, nonsurgical outpatient procedure that blocks the blood supply to the knee’s synovial via cauterization or injection of an embolic material (e.g. permanent microspheres or temporary agents such as imipienem and cilastatin). Reducing this blood flow is hypothesized to reduce local inflammation, neoinnervation, and neovascularization. The SR reviewed for the CPG included 14 studies utilizing a variety of techniques for geniculate artery embolization with variable outcomes.(217) The three RCTs in this SR failed to show a significant improvement in functional outcomes of geniculate artery embolization over sham procedures.(217) Considering pain, one of the RCTs reported a significant improvement at one month but not at longer intervals, and the other two RCTs did not show significant improvements in pain.(217) Notably, subgroup analysis showed a disproportionate response in patients with central sensitization, suggesting that geniculate artery embolization may have a role for pain conditions other than OA. This finding reinforces the recommendation in Appendix I to distinguish nociceptive from nociplastic pain prior to selecting interventional or procedural treatments. Patients whose pain is primarily centrally mediated are unlikely to derive sustained benefit from peripherally targeted procedures such as geniculate artery embolization.

No SAEs were reported in any of the 14 studies included in this SR.(217) However, providers should be aware of risks for geniculate artery embolization, which may include infection, postprocedural pain, and significant activity restrictions following the procedure.

Patients and providers are interested in additional non-surgical interventional procedures in the treatment of OA of the hip and knee. Compared to other non-surgical interventional procedures, geniculate artery embolization may incur higher costs to the healthcare system and patient, require more strict and longer activity restrictions, and may not be widely available.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(217) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-Added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low. The body of evidence had some limitations including lack of clarity in blinding procedures, high placebo response rates, varying outcome measures,

overlapping interventions, variability in embolization technique, and inclusion of noncomparative studies.(217) The harms and burdens of geniculate artery embolization for OA of the knee slightly outweigh the potential benefits. Patient values and preferences were similar. Patients may be interested in additional non-surgical procedural interventions to treat OA but would want a clearly demonstrated benefit before agreeing to such procedures. Given that geniculate artery embolization may be more burdensome than other non-surgical procedures for the treatment of OA of the hip and knee, the harms outweigh the potential benefits. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak against_ recommendation.

25. For the treatment of osteoarthritis of the knee, we suggest against geniculate nerve block.

#### **(Weak against | Reviewed, New-added)**

#### **_Discussion_**

Geniculate nerve blocks (GNB) have been studied both as a direct treatment for KOA and as a predictor of response to radiofrequency ablation (RFA) of the geniculate nerve. GNBs are minor, nonsurgical outpatient procedures where a local anesthetic is injected into the geniculate nerve. Blocking the innervation to the knee may reduce pain by disrupting pain signals from the knee. Here, we focus solely on GNB’s role in the treatment of KOA. The Work Group reviewed one SR that included eight studies by Almeida et al. 2025.(218) Six studies failed to show a clinically significant improvement in function or pain over comparators (i.e. standard of care, physical therapy, or alcoholic neurolysis).(218) One study showed short-term benefits in pain and function compared to placebo that was not sustained past 4 weeks.(218) Similarly, one study that compared GNB to intra-articular CS reported a temporary improvement in pain but no clinically significant difference in functional outcomes.(218) No SAEs were reported in the eight studies included in this SR.(218)

GNB for KOA appears to be a relatively safe procedure, however the evidence base is variable with the majority failing to show efficacy in multiple outcome measures including function, pain, fatigue, sleep, or QoL. The Work Group acknowledges that interventionalists may perform GNB when considering if RFA may benefit a patient. Commenting on that practice is beyond the scope of this CPG. The Work Group suggests against GNB as a treatment for KOA on its own.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(218) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-Added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low. The body of evidence had some limitations including heterogeneous patient populations, varying outcome measures, overlapping interventions, variable comparators, and inclusion of non-comparative studies.(218) The harms and burdens of GNB for KOA slightly outweighed the potential benefits. Patient values and preferences were similar because patients may be interested in additional non-surgical procedural interventions to treat OA but would want a clearly demonstrated benefit before agreeing to such procedures. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak against_ recommendation.

#### **_Recommendation_**

26. For the treatment of osteoarthritis of the knee, there is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against the use of radiofrequency ablation. **(Neither for nor against | Reviewed, New-added)**

#### **_Discussion_**

The Work Group reviewed two SRs of 39 studies by Almeida et al. 2025, Liu et al. 2024 (218,219) and one NMA of 12 RCTs by Park et al. 2024(220) that reported on RFA of the geniculate nerve for KOA. RFA is a minor, nonsurgical outpatient procedure that uses electrical stimulation to destroy sensory nerves. Ablating the geniculate nerve may reduce pain signals from the knee. RFA for KOA showed mixed results for improvement in function. The NMA by Park et al. (2024)(220) and one of the SRs (219) favored RFA over a variety of comparators including sham procedures, stem cells, HA, intra-articular CS, oral NSAID, PRP, and dextrose prolotherapy. However, the effect size was not clinically significant in this SR,(219) and the other SR failed to find a significant difference in function between the RFA and comparator groups.(218) Pain outcomes also showed mixed results. Liu et al. (2024) reported improvements in pain at 2, 4, 12, and 24 weeks follow up.(219) Almeida et al. (2025) reported a significant improvement in pain at 4-12 weeks, but not at 24-48 weeks.(218) Park et al.(2024) found no difference in pain or QoL outcomes.(220)

No SAEs were reported in one of the SRs.(218) SAEs were not discussed in the other SR (219) nor in the NMA.(220) However, providers should be aware of risks for RFA, which may include infection, procedural pain, and post-procedural pain. Notably, while RFA protocols are variable, this procedure is longer than others (e.g. interventional ablation [IA] or GNB) with protocols lasting as long as 15 minutes.(219)

Increasing options for non-surgical interventional procedures in the treatment of OA interests’ patients and providers alike. Compared to other non-surgical interventional procedures, RFA may incur higher costs to the healthcare system and patient, entail more procedure-related discomfort given the duration of the procedure, and may not be widely available.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(218-220) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-Added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low. The body of evidence had some limitations including multiple comparators, a lack of clarity around blinding procedures, randomization or allocation concealment, and the measurement of the outcomes. Analysis of the results also suffered from the overlapping of the interventions,(218-220) serious indirectness and sparse connections in the NMA,(220) using the global WOMAC score to assess function,(219) and heterogeneity in RFA protocols.(218-220) The benefits and harms/burden of RFA for OA are balanced. While there’s mixed evidence of its efficacy, safety appears to be comparable to other recommended non-surgical interventions (e.g. intra-articular injections). Patient values and preferences were similar because patients may be interested in additional non-surgical procedural interventions to treat OA but would want a clearly demonstrated benefit before agreeing to such procedures. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Neither for nor against_ recommendation.

#### **_Recommendation_**

27. For the treatment of osteoarthritis of the hip and knee, there is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against extracorporeal shockwave therapy. **(Neither for nor against | Reviewed, New-added)**

#### **_Discussion_**

The Work Group reviewed a SR by Silva et al. (2023)(221) of 12 studies evaluating extracorporeal shockwave therapy (ESWT) for OA of the hip and knee. ESWT is a minor, nonsurgical outpatient procedure that applies pressure waves and shock waves locally, which are hypothesized to reduce pain by stimulating tissue repair, promote healing mechanisms, and reduce inflammation. The evidence suggested that ESWT may improve function and pain scores compared to sham or standard of care, particularly in the short term (3-10 weeks). However, the strength of evidence was rated very low due to methodological limitations across studies, including unclear blinding, randomization, and outcome measurement procedures. Additionally, high heterogeneity in pooled analyses and overlapping interventions reduced confidence in the findings. While individual studies in the >10-week meta-analysis showed statistically significant effects favoring ESWT, inconsistency across studies limited the reliability of these results and, collectively, the confidence intervals were wide and not statistically significant. Safety outcomes were not reported, and postprocedure restrictions on NSAID use may affect recovery.

Patient values and preferences were considered. ESWT may offer temporary improvements in pain and function, which some patients may find appealing. However, the lack of safety data and the requirement to avoid NSAIDs for six weeks post-procedure may cause hesitation in patients. Feasibility is limited by patient access and the need for specialized training for treatment providers. Acceptability may vary due to differences in technique and frequency, and the unpredictable benefit may reduce patient confidence. Resource use is also a concern, as ESWT requires costly equipment and setup, which may not be available in all clinical settings, particularly within VA/DOD systems.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this new recommendation.(221) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low, due to multiple limitations in study design and reporting. These included unclear blinding and randomization procedures, lack of allocation concealment, and inconsistent outcome measurement. Additionally, the meta-analyses showed high heterogeneity, and many studies included overlapping interventions or non-comparative designs, further reducing confidence in the findings. While the evidence suggested that ESWT may improve pain and function scores in the short term (3-10 weeks), the durability of these effects remains uncertain. Safety data were not reported, and the requirement to avoid NSAIDs for six weeks post-procedure may interfere with recovery or patient comfort. The benefits of ESWT were therefore considered balanced with potential harms and burdens. Patient values and preferences were similar, with some patients potentially interested in non-invasive options, but others may be deterred by the lack of clarity around expected outcomes and procedural variability. Feasibility and resource use were also concerns, as ESWT requires specialized equipment, training, and may not be widely available in all clinical settings. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Neither for nor against_ recommendation.

28. For the treatment of osteoarthritis of the hip and knee, there is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against radiation therapy. **(Neither for nor against | Reviewed, New-added)**

#### **_Discussion_**

The Work Group reviewed one RCT evaluating low-dose radiation therapy for KOA by van den Ende et al. (2020).(222) Low-dose radiation therapy administers low-doses of radiation, typically 2 gray (Gy) or less, locally to the affected joint. Its mechanism of action in reducing pain is not fully elucidated, but it has been proposed to reduce inflammation in the targeted joint.(223) The study included 55 participants and compared radiation therapy to a sham intervention over 12 months. Across critical outcomes of function, pain, and SAEs, no statistically significant differences were observed between groups. The study reported no SAEs in the radiation group and three SAEs in the sham group, though none were deemed related to the intervention. The small sample size and lack of study for HOA contributed to a low strength of evidence. Given the limited data and absence of demonstrated benefit, the Work Group concluded that the evidence is insufficient to recommend for or against the use of radiation therapy for OA of the hip and knee.

Patient values and preferences were considered in the recommendation. While some patients may be open to non-invasive interventions, others may be deterred by the term “radiation” due to its negative connotation. The time burden associated with six treatments over two weeks, without demonstrated benefit, was also noted as a potential barrier. Resource use is significant, requiring specialized equipment and personnel, which may limit accessibility and equity, particularly in underserved or rural populations. Acceptability is further challenged by unclear benefits and patient apprehension. These factors collectively support the decision to refrain from recommending radiation therapy currently.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this new recommendation.(222) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Low, primarily due to the limited number of studies, small sample size, and lack of data for HOA. The only available study, a single RCT, did not demonstrate significant differences in pain, function, or SAEs between low-dose radiation therapy and sham treatment. The benefits of radiation therapy were balanced with potential harms, which were minimal but included concerns about radiation exposure and treatment burden. Although no AEs were attributed to the intervention, the lack of efficacy and the need for multiple treatments over a short period raised concerns about resource use and feasibility. Patient values and preferences were similar, with some patients potentially interested in non-invasive options, while others may be deterred by the term “radiation”. Given the absence of demonstrated benefit, the logistical burden, and the potential for patient apprehension, the Work Group concluded that the evidence is insufficient to recommend for or against the use of low-dose radiation therapy for OA of the hip and knee. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Neither for nor against_ recommendation.

29. For the treatment of osteoarthritis of the hip and knee, we suggest against the use of cannabidiol (CBD oil). **(Weak against | Reviewed, New-added)**

#### **_Discussion_**

Having access to nutraceuticals, specifically including CBD, which was mentioned by the Patient Focus Group, is important to both Veterans and Active-Duty Service Members with painful OA. The Work Group reviewed the evidence to determine whether there are any benefits or harms associated with CBD for OA. There was a single RCT by Pramhas et al. This could result in significant adverse outcomes including termination from federal service or tapering of long-term opioid therapy.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(224) Therefore, it is categorized as a _Reviewed, New-added_ recommendation. The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of evidence was Very low. The body of the evidence had some limitations including being limited to a single small study. The harms of CBD for OA of the hip or knee outweighed the benefits as evidenced by harms that were present (e.g., GGTA elevations and employment risks), and benefits of interest (e.g. pain and function) were absent. Patient values and preferences varied largely because of out-of-pocket costs, variability in formulations, uncertainty of purity of formulations, and potential adverse employment consequences. Finally, for all nutraceuticals, a more rigorous approach to product development and purity standards, dose ranging studies, and replication of RCTs by non-vested parties would be helpful in making more certain recommendations about the role nutraceuticals, including CBD, can or cannot play in the management of OA. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak against_ recommendation.

## **I. Complementary and Integrative Health**

#### **_Recommendation_**

31. For osteoarthritis of the knee, we suggest yoga or tai chi. **(Weak for | Reviewed, New-replaced)**

#### **_Discussion_**

Yoga and tai chi are distinct yet related mind-body practices that incorporate physical postures (yoga), slow and intentional movements (tai chi), breathing techniques, and mental focus to promote physical and psychological well-being. The evidence indicates that yoga and tai chi are generally safe interventions and may provide small but clinically meaningful short-term improvements in pain and function for patients with KOA. There is insufficient evidence to support a recommendation for or against yoga or tai chi for osteoarthritis of the hip.

#### **_Yoga_**

A SR by Lauche et al. (2019)(252) of nine RCTs found that for individuals with KOA, yoga improved physical function (SMD 0.64) and reduced pain intensity (SMD -0.75) compared to non-exercise controls. More recently, a RCT by Bennell et al. (2022)(253) evaluating a 12-week unsupervised online yoga program found a statistically significant improvement in function at 12 weeks compared to an educational control; however, this effect was not sustained at the 24week follow-up after completion of the intervention, during which adherence declined. The Bennell et al. (2022) study reported no difference in pain between the groups.(253)

Regarding safety, the Lauche et al. (2019) review noted limited reporting of AEs.(252) The Bennell et al. (2022) trial reported no SAEs, although participants in the yoga group reported

more non-SAEs (primarily musculoskeletal pain) than those in the education only control group (13% vs. 1%).(253)

#### **_Tai chi_**

A RCT by Chen et al. (2021)(254) found that for individuals with KOA, a 12-week tai chi program resulted in significant improvements in multiple measures of functional fitness (including the 2-minute step test, chair sit-and-reach test, and single-leg stance test) when compared to an educational control group. A SR by Hall et al. (2017), which was considered in the 2020 OA CPG, found statistically significant reductions in shortterm disability (SMD -0.66) and pain (SMD -0.66) versus no treatment or usual care.(255)

Regarding safety, the Hall et al. (2017)(255) review did not specifically address SAEs, noting only that few AEs were reported in earlier reviews on the topic. Similarly, Chen et al. (2021) did not address AEs.(254)

The Work Group also considered patient values and preferences, feasibility, and subgroup considerations. Findings from the VA/DOD Patient Focus Group indicate that many Veterans and Service Members value managing the symptoms of OA through lifestyle and nonpharmacologic interventions. Participants reported familiarity with a range of complementary and integrative health (CIH) interventions and have experienced symptom relief though the benefits were often described as temporary or variable. Participants also expressed concerns about potential out-of-pocket costs.

From a feasibility standpoint, the Work Group noted that VA already supports yoga, tai chi, and related CIH modalities, and that many VA facilities and military treatment facilities offer these or similar programs at no cost to beneficiaries. However, availability and regular access may vary, including the availability of clinicians or instructors trained in specific CIH modalities.

Subgroup considerations include that yoga and tai chi obtained in community settings are generally not covered by TRICARE, which may limit access for some DOD beneficiaries. Although some individuals may be able to use healthcare flexible spending accounts to offset costs, this option is not universally available. However, many communities offer free or low-cost yoga or tai chi classes, and virtual options may also improve access.

The Work Group systematically reviewed new and previously reviewed evidence related to this new recommendation.(252-255) Therefore, this recommendation is categorized as _Reviewed, New-replaced_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Low for yoga and Very low for tai chi. The body of evidence was limited by a small number of studies, small sample sizes, and a lack of data on long-term effectiveness. The benefits of yoga and tai chi in improving function, pain interference, and pain were judged to outweigh the potential harms, which consisted primarily of small increases in non-serious AEs. Patient values and preferences varied somewhat, with many expressing interest in alternative treatment modalities while also expressing concern about out-of-pocket costs and long-term effectiveness. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

#### **_Discussion_**

Integrative health brings conventional and complementary approaches together in a coordinated way. Integrative health also emphasizes multimodal interventions, which are two or more interventions such as conventional health care approaches (like medication, physical rehabilitation, psychotherapy), and complementary health approaches (like acupuncture, yoga, and probiotics) in various combinations, with an emphasis on treating the whole person rather than, for example, one organ system. Integrative health aims for well-coordinated care among different providers and institutions by bringing conventional and complementary approaches together to care for the whole person. Patient interest in these approaches (previously termed complementary and alternative medicine) is high. The use of complementary health approaches by American adults has surged, especially for pain treatment in the past several years.(256) Due to the popularity of these approaches we focused an entire key question on their safety and efficacy.

This section includes our review of and recommendation for 10 CIH interventions. The modalities reviewed included: acupressure, acupuncture, balneotherapy, dry needling, massage, meditation, mindfulness, moxibustion, mud therapy, and neurofeedback. While some promising evidence emerged, there was insufficient evidence to recommend for or against the use of any of these CIH interventions for the treatment of OA of the hip or knee.

#### **_Acupressure_**

Acupressure is a treatment in which a practitioner applies, or a patient can self-administer physical pressure to specific points on the body to relieve symptoms.(257,258) The practice is based on traditional Chinese medicine meridian theory and uses the same acupoints as acupuncture but using fingers, hands, or elbows instead of acupuncture needles. The putative mechanisms for acupressure include stimulation of self-healing, relief of muscular tension, endorphin release, suppression of peripheral inflammation, and modulation of pain signals.(258)

The evidence reviewed for acupressure in KOA included two RCTs, a pilot study by Cheung et al. (2020)(259) and a fully powered trial by Yeung et al. (2024)(258) from the same research group. Yeung et al. (2024)(258) compared self-administered acupressure (n=174) vs an education

control arm (n=175). There were no differences between self-administered acupressure and control for function and pain interference. Participants in the acupressure group showed improved pain and QoL at the end of treatment compared to control participants in the larger trial but not in the pilot trial. Because no SAEs were reported in either study, acupressure is generally considered safe.

#### **_Acupuncture_**

<mark>Acupuncture involves a practitioner inserting needles into the skin to stimulate nerves, muscles, and connective tissues throughout the body. Conceptually, acupuncture is believed to stimulate the body's meridians, or energy-carrying channels, to alleviate pain, tension, and stress.(2</mark> 60 <mark>) The needles may be manipulated manually or stimulated with small electrical currents (electroacupuncture) or heated (fire needle, moxibustion).</mark>

<mark>The evidence reviewed for acupuncture in KOA included a SR by Kwak and colleagues.(2</mark> 61) <mark>Their review found six RCTs and involved more than 900 participants. Included studies evaluated the comparative effectiveness of acupuncture combined with NSAID treatment versus NSAID treatment alone. There were significant effects on function and pain severity favoring acupuncture plus NSAID treatment compared to NSAID alone at treatment end and at follow-up. A relatively small comparative effectiveness trial by Atlay et al. (2021)(2</mark> 62 <mark>) assessed the efficacy and safety of acupuncture (n=50) versus physical therapy (n=50). There was no significant difference between the treatment arms on function, pain severity, fatigue, and QoL.</mark>

<mark>Related to safety, the largest RCT (n=652) by Scharf et al. (2006)(</mark> 26 <mark>3) reported more hematoma</mark> <mark>events in the acupuncture plus NSAID group than the NSAID only group. In the Atlay et al. (2021) study (</mark> 262 <mark>) no significant AEs were noted in either treatment arm.</mark>

#### **_Balneotherapy_**

Balneotherapy is a therapeutic practice that involves bathing in mineral-rich baths or pools and water immersion for health purposes and has been recognized by the World Health Organization as a complementary therapeutic approach.(264) Balneotherapy has been used for hundreds of years to treat musculoskeletal conditions such as OA, dermatological diseases, and chronic respiratory disorders. Its therapeutic benefits are believed to stem from anti-inflammatory, analgesic, antioxidant, cartilage protection, and immunomodulatory effects.(265)

Two relatively small RCTs by Adigüzel et al. (2022) and Cantista et al. (2020)(266,267) were reviewed. Adigüzel and colleagues (266) compared balneotherapy (n=28) versus hydrotherapy (n=25) with sham peloid therapy (mud therapy) in participants with KOA. In a slightly larger trial, Cantista et al. (2020)(267) compared balneotherapy (n=60) versus usual care (n=60) for KOA. Overall, results showed improved function, pain severity, and pain interference compared to usual care. However, outcomes (function, pain severity, and pain interference) were not statistically different between balneotherapy and hydrotherapy with sham peloid therapy. There was no difference between balneotherapy and usual care for QoL. Balneotherapy is considered safe with minimal side effects.(268)

#### **_Dry Needling_**

Dry needling is defined by the American Physical Therapy Association as an intervention using a thin filiform needle to penetrate the skin and stimulate underlying myofascial trigger points, muscular, and connective tissues for the management of pain and movement impairment. Dry needling results in both peripheral and central effects. Peripheral effects include decreased spontaneous electrical activity and acetylcholine at myofascial trigger, increase in vasoactive substances. Central effects include activation of supraspinal mechanisms namely diffuse noxious inhibitory control.(269)

We reviewed a SR by Jiménez-Del-Barrio et al. (2022)(270) that included seven RCTs of participants with knee or HOA. Five of the seven trials compared dry needling versus sham (n=191), and two trials compared dry needling to exercise (n=100). Note, all trials were small (less 50 participants), assessed only short-term effects (1 to 3-weeks), and evaluated dry needing in isolation rather than combined with other commonly used treatment modalities used in physical medicine/physical therapy settings. In KOA, there may be short-term benefits for pain severity.(271) For HOA, dry needling outperformed sham needling or exercise in three trials for short-term improvements in pain severity.

Only one of the seven RCTs (n=62) included AEs, reporting no SAEs in either the dry needling or exercise arms. Potential adverse effects include bruising, hematoma, infection, or nerve injury.

#### **_Massage_**

In a 2011 study, 82% of Veterans reported use of at least one CIH treatment and nearly all were willing to try such approaches for their pain.(272) Of all CIH approaches, massage was the most preferred by Veterans.(272) Massage is theorized to work through a variety of mechanisms to relieve pain.(273-275) These mechanisms include: 1) increased local blood circulation; 2) improved muscle tone; 3) increased joint flexibility; 4) heightened relaxation response; and 5) changed neuroendocrine and inflammatory status implicated in pain generation and sensitivity.

The evidence reviewed for massage for KOA included two small RCTs by Döner et al. (2024) and Sansila et al. (2019).(276,277) Döner et al. conducted a 3-arm trial that tested aromatherapy massage combined with physiotherapy (n=20) versus massage with sham aromatherapy (n=20) versus physiotherapy alone (n=20). In this trial, aromatherapy massage with physiotherapy improved function and pain severity compared to massage with sham aromatherapy or physiotherapy alone. There were no differences between study interventions for sleep. A comparative effectiveness trial (277) compared traditional Thai massage (n=30) versus diclofenac (n=30) in patients with KOA. While function improved in the massage arm compared to diclofenac at 6 and 12 weeks, there were no differences seen in pain severity and pain inference.

In the 2020 OA CPG, the trial by Perlman et al. (2019)(278) was highlighted. This trial of 200 individuals showed that Swedish massage improved WOMAC global scores (pain and function) in patients with KOA compared to light touch and usual care.(278) In all these studies of different forms of massage, massage had a very low risk of harm or side effects.

#### **_Meditation_**

We did not review any new studies of meditation treatment for OA of the knee and hip. However, we reviewed mindfulness-based interventions below and acknowledge that meditation and mindfulness are related concepts and that mindfulness meditation is a popular type of meditation practice.

#### **_Mindfulness_**

Mindfulness-based interventions are complementary treatments used to treat pain.(279) Mindfulness derives from the Buddhist spiritual tradition and manifests as a present-moment, nonjudgmental awareness and acceptance of one's thoughts, emotions, and experiences.(280) Mindfulness is fostered through various meditation practices that have shown to improve attention regulation, body and self-awareness, and emotional regulation.(281)

The evidence reviewed for mindfulness-based interventions for knee or hip OA included two small RCTs by Shah et al. (2024) and Marais et al. (2022).(282,283) Shah et al. (2024) compared a telehealth-delivered mindful exercise intervention (n=21) versus telehealth exercise only (n=19) in patients with KOA.(275) There were no differences between study interventions for function, pain interference, or pain severity at 8 or 14 weeks. In the trial by Marais and colleagues,(283) guided mindfulness exercises (n=20) were compared to usual care (n=20) in knee or hip OA. There were no differences between mindfulness exercises and usual care in function, pain severity, or QoL at 3 or 6 months. However, mental health improved more in the mindfulness arm at the end of treatment, yet benefits did not persist at the 6-month follow-up. Mindfulness is considered safe; there were no differences in reported SAEs between study arms.

#### **_Moxibustion_**

Moxibustion is a Traditional Chinese Medicine treatment that involves burning dried mugwort (moxa) on or above the skin at acupuncture points to warm meridians, stimulate Qi (energy) flow, and promote healing.(284) The burning moxa can be rolled in stick form, placed on the skin, set on acupuncture needles, or delivered by specialized machines that radiate heat.(285) Moxibustion is believed to work via heat conduction and radiation that penetrates tissue and increase blood flow as well as pharmacologically active compounds in moxa and its combustion products.(286)

A small three-arm trial by Kang et al. (2020)(287) tested electronic moxibustion (n=45), traditional moxibustion (n=45), and usual care (n=46) for patients with KOA. Results showed no differences in function, pain severity, or QoL between electronic and traditional moxibustion, yet all outcomes were improved relative to usual care. In this trial, no SAEs were reported. While considered safe, burns, allergic reactions, infection, and respiratory symptoms from smoke exposure have been reported.(284)

#### **_Mud Therapy_**

Mud therapy (also called peloid therapy or mud-bath therapy) is a treatment that uses mineral-rich mud often combined with high temperature, water enriched with organic and inorganic minerals that are applied to the different body parts to relieve pain, facilitate blood circulation, and improve skin conditions. Treatment typically involves 10 daily sessions for acute treatment where heated mud is applied to affected joints, often followed by mineral water baths.(288) The therapeutic

mechanisms are believed to derive from hyperthermia, mechanical effects from mud application, and anti-inflammatory effects from mineral absorption.(289)

We reviewed a SR by Raza et al. (2021)(290) that included 6 RCTs and more than 500 participants with KOA. Two of the trials compared mud therapy (mud bath or mud pack) versus hot pack therapy and three of the trials compared mud therapy to usual care. Overall, mud therapy showed improved function and pain severity compared to control.

#### **_Neurofeedback_**

Neurofeedback is a technique to train individuals to learn to self-regulate specific patterns of brain activity through real-time feedback, typically using electroencephalography (EEG) or functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI).(291) Neurofeedback targets brain areas involved in pain modulation, emotional processing, and sensory components of pain (e.g. anterior cingulate cortex and somatosensory cortex).(292) Neurofeedback has been investigated for various chronic pain conditions including fibromyalgia, neuropathic pain, migraines, chronic low back pain, and KOA.(293)

A single RCT was identified and reviewed by Mathew et al. (2022)(294) for neurofeedback. In a very small trial with brief follow-up, Mathew and colleagues (294) compared 9 sessions of neurofeedback over 3 weeks in 11 participants compared to sham neurofeedback (n=10) in patients with KOA. There were no differences between study arms in functional improvement, pain interference, or pain severity. No SAEs were reported in either arm.

#### **_Additional Considerations_**

The Work Group also considered patient values and preferences, feasibility, and subgroup considerations. Findings from the VA/DOD Patient Focus Group indicate that Veterans and Service Members value a holistic and individualized approach to treatment of OA and associated pain.

Patient preferences likely vary for these CIH interventions. Some treatments may be preferred (e.g. massage) relative to other treatments such as acupuncture since some individuals may have anxiety related to use of acupuncture needles. Similarly, providers’ preferences may differ on whether to recommend them or not. Furthermore, clinicians may not be aware of certain treatments such as balneotherapy, moxibustion, mud therapy, and neurofeedback.

From a feasibility standpoint, several complementary and integrative therapies are available at VA medical centers and military treatment facilities. However, access to these therapies is not universal and can vary by geographic location. Some therapies such as balneotherapy, moxibustion, and mud therapy have limited (if any) availability currently for Veterans and military Service Members. For example, balneotherapy, moxibustion, and mud therapy are typically delivered in specialized spas or health resort settings rather than health care settings. Availability of these treatments also varies due to the lack of appropriately trained and skilled clinicians or practitioners.

Focus group participants reported having tried various CIH interventions and highlighted their benefits and challenges. The primary benefit mentioned was pain relief, however the relief experienced was only temporary, which was frustrating to participants. Participants also

expressed the following challenges with CIH interventions; 1) transportation barriers; 2) scheduling conflicts (i.e. lack of after-hour treatment options); 3) potential out-of-pocket costs; 4) multiple visits for treatment; and 5) lengthy referral and renewal of service processes that can lead to gaps in treatment and worsening symptoms.

The Work Group systematically reviewed new and previously reviewed evidence related to this new recommendation.(256-270,272-296) Therefore, this recommendation is categorized as _Reviewed, New-replaced._ The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Low. The evidence was limited by few studies, small sample sizes, modest effects sizes, short-term follow-up, various treatment protocols, and minimal reporting of side effects. The benefits of these interventions (improved function, reduced pain interference, and pain relief) were judged to outweigh the potential harms, which were considered minimal. Patient values and preferences varied somewhat, with many expressing interest in alternative treatment modalities while also expressing concern about out-of-pocket costs and only short-term relief. The Work Group also considered the challenge that some of these therapies are delivered in specialized spas or resort settings in a few locales rather than medical settings. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Neither for nor against_ recommendation.

# **X. Research Priorities**

There are several areas that require more focused research to provide stronger evidence for further recommendation development across the spectrum of care. In summary, the Work Group recommends further research on the long-term safety, effectiveness and comparative effectiveness of interventions for OA with attention to patient subgroups, sustained outcomes, cost-effectiveness and guidance for clinical decision-making.

- **A. Distinguishing Pain Phenotypes to Improve Treatment Targeting in Osteoarthritis**

 - Nociplastic pain mechanisms are estimated to be a prominent feature in up to 40% of knee and hip OA cases, where reported pain severity is disproportionate to observable structural damage. This represents a large subgroup of patients who may respond poorly to tissue-directed treatments.(297,298)

 - The prevalence and clinical profile of nociplastic pain among Veterans and Active-Duty Service Members with OA is not well characterized. Research is needed to define how frequently this phenotype occurs in this population and to describe its clinical features.(299)

 - Validated screening instruments exist but require feasibility testing. Research should help establish practical, mechanism-informed screening pathways to enable frontline providers to distinguish nociplastic from nociceptive pain in patients presenting with hip or knee OA, and to direct patients toward interventions most likely to address the underlying pain mechanism.(300,301)

- Mechanism-matched treatment trials are needed to determine whether phenotype-guided therapy improves clinical outcomes compared with current non-phenotype approaches.(302)

## **B. Long-term Studies and Comparative Effectiveness Research**

- OA is a chronic condition; therefore, studies of a longer duration (>12 weeks) should be conducted to determine long-term effectiveness and safety.

- There is a paucity of research on the comparative effectiveness of different treatment options for OA of the knee and hip. Additional studies are needed to examine the comparative effectiveness of pharmacologic and non-pharmacologic options.

- Overall, limited studies address the treatment of HOA. Future studies should focus specifically on the management of HOA to better inform clinical decision-making.

## **C. Lifestyle-Based Interventions**

- Additional studies are needed on weight loss interventions, alone or combined with other interventions, to evaluate their impact on OA outcomes.

- Exercise is a key component of OA management, but further research is needed on the comparative effectiveness of different exercise types (non-weight-bearing, weight-bearing, aerobic) and on controlling dose and loading parameters.

## **D. Physical Therapy**

- While physical therapy is a safe and effective intervention for patients with hip and knee OA, research is needed on the long-term effectiveness (beyond 6-12 months), including sustainability of benefits, safety, and impact on healthcare cost and utilization.

- Additional research is recommended to help determine the optimal timing and strategies for the delivery of physical therapy across the disease course of hip and knee OA, including approaches to improve patient adherence and long-term engagement.

- Further studies are also recommended on how to more effectively engage patients in physical therapy interventions. Given changes in demand and healthcare utilization, studies comparing alternate modes of delivery for physical therapy should be performed comparing telehealth-supported, self-directed, group, and in-person treatment for both utilization and treatment effectiveness.

## **E. Devices**

- While bracing appears to improve pain and function compared with usual care, its effectiveness compared with control orthoses is variable. Additional research is needed using more uniform comparators, standardized controls, and consistent protocols for wear time and activity-specific use. Long-term, high-quality comparative effectiveness studies of different brace designs and optimizing knee braces are needed.

- Further research is needed to standardize outcome measures and assess potential harms of bracing, including objective measures of functional performance, muscle strength, and muscle cross-sectional area, to better understand risks such as disuse atrophy.

- There is a paucity of well-powered comparative effectiveness studies of orthopedic insoles, with substantial heterogeneity in insole type, materials, and fabrication methods. Future studies should control for wear time, account for daily activity patterns, and identify patient subgroups most likely to benefit.

- Additional studies are recommended to evaluate the effectiveness of TENS and other electrotherapeutic devices, particularly for patients with OA and comorbidities that limit participation in exercise-based interventions.

## **F. Pharmacotherapy**

 - Given limited pharmacologic options for hip and knee OA, larger, well-designed trials with longer follow-up are needed to evaluate the efficacy, safety, and recommended delivery of capsaicin, acetaminophen, topical NSAIDs, and selective versus non-selective COX-2 inhibitors.

 - Persistent gaps remain in understanding optimal use of opioids and combined pharmacotherapies. Future studies should clarify the role of buprenorphine, identify populations who may benefit from short- or long-term opioid use, and establish safe multimodal sequencing strategies while assessing opioid-related risks.

 - More standardized, high-quality research is needed for intra-articular injections, including expanded outcome measures (e.g., QoL, sleep, pain interference), clearer guidance on timing, frequency, and patient selection, and evaluation of both local and systemic AEs.

 - Overall, higher-quality, more standardized studies and improved clinical decision-making tools are needed, particularly for patients with comorbidities and for HOA, which remains underrepresented in literature.

- **G. Evidence to Support the Safe and Effective Use of Orthobiologic Therapies and Other Emerging Biologic Treatments for Osteoarthritis**

 - Research should prioritize standardization of orthobiologic preparation and administration, including product type, dosing, timing, frequency, and injection techniques, to reduce variability and improve comparability across studies.

 - Well-designed RCTs are needed to assess the safety, effectiveness, and comparative effectiveness of orthobiologics (e.g., PRP, stem cells, amniotic products, polynucleotides) versus placebo, sham, and established injectable therapies for improving outcomes and OA progression.

 - Additional studies should better define patient populations most likely to benefit, including subgroup analyses relevant to Veterans and Service Members (e.g., age, disease severity, comorbidities, prior joint injury, occupational demands), and

incorporate cost-effectiveness, implementation considerations, and patient education and shared decision-making.

## **H. Interventional Procedures**

- Standardization of protocols and techniques is needed for procedures such as GNB, RFA, ESWT, and low-dose radiation therapy, including dosing, frequency, type, administration, and procedural parameters.

- Well-designed studies are needed to evaluate safety, effectiveness, and long-term outcomes versus sham or other non-surgical treatments, with attention to patient subgroups, hip versus knee OA, and Veteran and Service Member populations.

- Research is needed to assess long-term, patient-centered outcomes including sustained pain relief, function, QoL, and AEs, and clarify which patients are most likely to benefit from each procedure.

## **I. Dietary Supplements and Nutraceuticals in Osteoarthritis**

- The use of OTC dietary supplements and nutraceuticals is common among patients with OA but for more robust, non-industry funded studies are needed to determine the dose, frequency, effectiveness, and safety of supplements. More rigorous evaluation of product development and purity standards would also be beneficial in clarifying their role in managing OA.

## **J. Complementary and Integrative Health**

- Further research is needed to determine the effectiveness and role of CIH interventions for knee and hip OA, including timing, combination with other therapies, and integration into current management paradigms. Larger, well-designed trials are needed to address methodological limitations, identify optimal frequency, duration, and style, and isolate specific intervention effects.

- Long-term outcomes and cost-effectiveness remain unclear, particularly for hip OA and combination therapies, requiring further evaluation of sustained benefits, safety, and economic impact.

## **K. Psychological Interventions**

- The WG acknowledged a limitation that this CPG did not review psychological interventions for the treatment of osteoarthritis of the knee and/or hip.

- For the next CPG update, the WG recommends including a psychologist as a participant in the Work Group development team.

- The evidence review should include the use of psychological interventions, such as CBT, ACT, and cognitive reprocessing therapy for the improvement of symptoms and function related to OA.

## **B. Conducting the Systematic Review**

Literature searches identified 6,315 citations potentially addressing the KQs of interest for this evidence review. After 94 duplicates were removed, there were 6,221 citations. Of these, 4,427 were excluded upon title and abstract review for clearly not meeting inclusion criteria (e.g., not pertinent to the topic, not published in English, published prior to the study inclusion publication date, or not a full-length article). A total of 1,794 full-length articles were reviewed. Of these, 1,685 were excluded for the following reasons: comparator not of interest (298), intervention not of interest (277), superseded by another SR (252), not a country of very high Human Development Index (HDI) (231), population not of interest (130), study design not of interest (121), publication type not of interest (99), outcomes not of interest (97), other (e.g., SR did not include risk of bias, relevant SR with no useable data, too short follow-up period [87]), study included in an included SR (35), published before January 1, 2015 (24), does not address any KQ (19), inadequate sample size (10), or not English language (5).
