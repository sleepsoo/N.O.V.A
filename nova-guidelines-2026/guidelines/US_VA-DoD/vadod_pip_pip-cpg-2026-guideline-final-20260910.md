##### **QUALIFYING STATEMENTS**

The Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) and the Department of Defense (DOD) guidelines are based on the best information available at the time of publication. The guidelines are designed to provide information and assist decision making. They are not intended to define a standard of care and should not be construed as such, nor should the guidelines be interpreted as prescribing an exclusive course of management.

This clinical practice guideline (CPG) is based on a systematic review of both clinical and epidemiological evidence. Developed by a panel of multidisciplinary experts, it provides a clear explanation of the logical relationships between various care options and health outcomes while rating both the quality of the evidence and the strength of the recommendation.

Variations in practice will inevitably and appropriately occur when providers consider the needs of individual patients, available resources, and limitations unique to an institution or type of practice. Therefore, every health care professional using these guidelines is responsible for evaluating the appropriateness of applying them in each unique clinical situation using a patient-centered approach.

These guidelines are not intended to represent VA or DOD policies. Further, inclusion of recommendations for specific testing, therapeutic interventions, or both, within these guidelines does not guarantee coverage of civilian sector care.

## **I. Introduction**

The Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) and Department of Defense (DOD) Evidence-Based Practice Work Group (EBPWG) was established and first chartered in 2004, with a mission to advise the “…Health Executive Council (HEC) on the use of clinical and epidemiological evidence to improve the health of the population across the Veterans Health Administration (VHA) and Military Health System (MHS),” by facilitating the development of clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) for the VA and DOD populations.(1) The development and updating of VA/DOD CPGs is funded by VA Evidence Based Practice, Office of Quality and Patient Safety. The system-wide goal of evidence-based CPGs is to improve patient health and well-being.

The VA/DOD EBPWG initiated the creation of the VA/DOD Pressure Injury Prevention and Management CPG in 2024. This CPG provides an evidence-based framework for evaluating and managing adult patients, 18 years or older, who are eligible for care in the VA and/or DOD healthcare systems and may benefit from pressure injury prevention and management. Successful implementation of this CPG will:

- Emphasize the use of patient-centered care using risk factors and event history;

- Minimize preventable complications and morbidity;

- Optimize each patient’s health outcomes and improve quality of life; and

- Assess the patient’s condition and in collaboration with the patient, family, and caregivers, determine optimal treatment.

## **II. Background**

### **A. Importance and Impact of Pressure Injuries**

Pressure injury (PI) represents a medical condition that produces a significant human and financial burden on healthcare systems, causing patient harm, driving up costs, and extending hospital stays.(2) To incentivize quality and safety in healthcare, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) ceased reimbursement for Stage 3 and higher hospital-acquired pressure injuries (HAPIs) in 2008. This financial pressure was further intensified by the 2015 Hospital-Acquired Condition (HAC) Reduction Program, which penalizes facilities with the highest HAPI rates. Beyond these CMS penalties, these injuries present an immense risk to patient health and well-being.(3)

Most PIs are avoidable; unavoidable PIs may still develop, and existing PIs may become worse despite the timely implementation of prevention and intervention strategies. Some common highrisk populations include those with spinal cord injury/disorders, those who are frail, and those who are living with immobility, dementia, prior history of PIs, neurological impairment, prolonged surgical procedure/anesthesia, obesity, malnutrition, and many other health conditions.(4) Formerly known as “pressure ulcers”, “bed sores,” or “decubitus ulcers”, PIs are often seen as a marker of neglect and poor quality of care, which may be complicated by pain and/or infection, and have a profound impact on the patient’s and caregiver’s quality of life. Throughout this document, we will use the term ‘pressure injury’ rather than the historical term ‘pressure ulcer’.

Historically, avoidable PIs occur when there is a delay in identifying a patient as being high risk. This leads to late or no implementation of PI prevention strategies. These strategies are repositioning, incontinence management, reduction in shear forces, and monitoring medical devices. Patients with dark skin tone, in particular, may have late recognition of PI incidence. Treatment, prevention, and management of PIs is costly to a healthcare system(5) and the patient, but can be devastating to a caregiver, family member, or significant other who may bear the responsibility for managing and implementing the patient's treatment and plan of care.

Assessing, preventing, identifying risk, and managing PIs is crucial, not only to healthcare systems, but also to clinical practice professionals. Regardless of whether pressure injuries are avoidable or unavoidable, the treatment and management of the PI impacts quality of life. Additionally, it is important to increase awareness of PI prevention and decrease the potential risk of unnecessary harm, delay in care, and death.

### **B. Pathophysiology/Etiology/Biological Factors of Pressure Injury and Staging**

In patients without mobility, cognitive, or sensation deficits, prolonged pressure elicits a sensory feedback response that prompts a change in body position. When the feedback response is absent or severely impaired, sustained external pressure ultimately leads to tissue ischemia, subsequent injury, and necrosis. In addition, the return of blood supply to an area with chronic ischemia (reperfusion) has been documented as contributing to tissue damage and pressure injuries.(6) The highest pressures often occur at the interface of bone and muscle, causing necrosis at this level while leaving the epidermis with no clinical manifestations.(7) Literature shows that after five to seven days of initial injury, when skin ulceration is observed, a deep tissue pressure injury has already occurred and is evolving.

Shear forces, particularly encountered when lying at an incline, may impair local capillary beds and contribute to tissue hypoxia.(8) When lying at an angle, the downward force of gravity is countered by friction, which prevents the person from sliding down in the bed. Though the skin may not move down the bed, internal structures like muscle and bone that are not in contact with an external surface are displaced downward because of gravity. These shear forces can disrupt blood flow as vessels caught between the skin and bone are distorted or compressed.(9)

##### _Etiology_

In 2016, the National Pressure Ulcer Advisory Panel (recently renamed the National Pressure Injury Advisory Panel [NPIAP]) revised the definition and staging of pressure injuries. The revision was undertaken to incorporate the current understanding of the etiology of pressure injuries and to clarify the anatomical features present or absent in each stage. Staging systems describe the extent of tissue loss and the physical appearance of the injury caused by pressure and/or shear forces. In 1975, J.D. Shea developed a staging system for the classification of pressure injuries, and in 1988, the International Association of Enterostomal Therapy (now the Wound, Ostomy and Continence Nurses Society) created a four-stage system based on these classifications.(10) In 2016, the revised NPIAP Pressure Injury Staging System included the term injury, but not ulcer. It was determined that the term ulcer is not accurate because pressure injuries may not result in a break in the skin.(11)

Pressure injuries can occur in various settings in which individuals are subjected to sustained mechanical loads. These injuries are especially common in individuals bed or wheelchair confined, or who wear a prosthesis for ambulatory purposes.(12) In addition, the numerical standard for capillary closure pressure of 32 mmHg is frequently used as a threshold for tissue damage.(13) External pressure must exceed the arterial capillary pressure of 32 mmHg to impede blood flow. The pressure must be greater than the venous capillary closing pressure of 8 to 12 mmHg to impair venous blood return. Sustained pressures above these values can lead to tissue ischemia and progress to localized necrosis. This significant pressure can be a result of compression from a firm mattress, hospital bed railings, or any hard surface in contact with an individual. Pressures at the junction between the skin and a contact surface that exceed this value will most likely produce localized ischemia, which, over a prolonged period, may lead to tissue breakdown.(14) Although it is acknowledged that pressure injuries are primarily due to sustained mechanical loading of the soft tissues of the body, prevention of injury remains difficult to accomplish. This is primarily attributed to a lack of data regarding how loading conditions are transferred to local stress and strains inside the soft tissues and how these conditions may lead to tissue breakdown.(12) Although not clinically validated, computer models capable of capturing the transition of external loads to local internal stresses and strains may provide detailed and relevant insight into the mechanical conditions of various tissue layers from the epidermis to the underlying muscle.(12) Other common causative factors for the development of pressure injuries include:

#### _Shear and Friction Forces_

Recent pressure injury research indicates that friction-induced skin injuries are not true pressure injuries, whereas shearing forces can cause a decrease in localized blood flow and therefore are important in the development of such injuries.(15) While it is accepted that friction can contribute to pressure ulcerations in deeper soft tissues, friction itself does not readily provide pathological changes to the epidermis.

#### _Poor Perfusion_

Poor perfusion includes factors that alter oxygen delivery to tissues. Factors such as atherosclerosis, hypovolemia, heart failure, and infection can cause injury to dermal and muscular cells, ultimately contributing to localized edema and cell demise.(16) Therefore, adequate tissue perfusion must be maintained to provide healing to pressure injury areas.

#### _Moisture_

Moisture-associated skin damage is produced by chronic exposure to sources such as perspiration, wound exudate, urine, and stool. It affects the epidermis initially, leading to inflammation and potential erosion of the skin. Excess moisture to the skin, particularly urine, perspiration, and stool, can increase the pH level of the epidermis, leading to irritation and further breakdown.(17)

#### _Biological_

Wound healing represents various interrelated biological events occurring over a temporal sequence. They entail tightly regulated interactions between cells, growth factors, the extracellular matrix, and a variety of proteases and inhibitors.(18) Natural phases of healing, which overlap in time, include the initial inflammatory phase, proliferative phase, and the final remodeling and

maturation phase. Unlike acute wounds, pressure injuries do not often follow the normal phases of healing; instead, they become arrested in the inflammatory phase.(19)

Endothelial dysfunction can contribute to the pathway of inflammatory phase arrest for pressure injuries. Injury to endothelial cells, the cells that line the inside of blood vessels, along with a lack of adequate blood supply to tissues in the body, can trigger the immune system to release substances that directly impact the actions of the blood vessels. In a typical short-term injury, vasoactive cytokines are released and help to heal the injured area without causing overall harm to the body. When an injury to endothelial cells becomes chronic, the vasoactive cytokines can cause permanent damage, resulting in a change in cellular structure that prevents cells from functioning. The most common cytokines responsible for this are the pro-inflammatory cytokines interleukin-1 beta and tissue necrosis factor alpha. This permanent change often leads to decreased production of vasodilators, causing vasoconstriction and reduced blood flow to the tissues.(20)

Anemia can be another biological contributor to pressure injuries. Sufficient hemoglobin is required for tissue oxygenation. In anemic patients, the oxygen-carrying capacity of blood is reduced, and hence, there is a decreased supply of oxygen to the tissues. This will precipitate tissue necrosis in ischemic tissues because of mechanical pressure. Therefore, patients with appropriate hemoglobin levels will be able to tolerate the deleterious effects of pressure better in comparison to anemic patients.(21)

Edematous tissue contains compromised circulation and is also low in albumin. Increased tissue fluid decreases tissue oxygenation and increases the likelihood of pressure injuries and ulcerations. It is generally believed that edema fluid should have a cushioning effect and decrease localized pressure. However, increased interstitial tissue fluid causes increased pressure over blood vessels, decreases the pressure difference between the capillary blood and the tissue fluid, and hence decreases blood flow and oxygenation to the tissues. Ultimately, edema does not prevent but rather enhances the deleterious effects of pressure on the body.(21)

### **C. Pressure Injury Prevention in the Department of Veterans Affairs and the Department of Defense**

The Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) and Defense Health Agency (DHA) represent two of the largest healthcare agencies in the world. Higher rates of pressure injuries in this population have been documented compared to civilian populations. In 2020, Congress reported the incidence of facility-acquired pressure injuries and the associated $1,300,000,000 to $3,600,000,000(22) in annual costs for the treatment of hospitalized patients with pressure injuries in VA facilities.

Hospital-acquired pressure injuries (HAPIs) represent a significant and often preventable source of patient harm, particularly among hospitalized military personnel. These injuries cause pain, increase morbidity and mortality, and contribute to higher healthcare costs. While the ultimate goal is HAPI elimination, current rates remain concerning, necessitating improved prevention strategies and prompt management protocols for Veterans and U.S. Military Service members and their families. This requires a concerted, multidisciplinary effort to identify at-risk patients, implement best practices, accurately document present on admission (POA) status, and utilize data-driven performance improvement initiatives to reduce HAPI incidence and severity.

As the largest integrated health care system in the United States, the VA’s commitment to prevention and management of PIs embodies its mission and Core Values, which inform every interaction with Veterans, caregivers, family members, or significant others. These Core Values are Integrity, Commitment, Advocacy, Respect, and Excellence, also known as “I CARE”.(23) These values have been the foundation for the varied policies, programs, training, and education developed for high-risk Veteran populations such as spinal cord injury and disorder (SCI/D), and those with immobility, incontinence, motor/sensory impairment, rehab/long-term care, altered cognitive function, dementia, poor nutritional status, and other medical and health-related conditions.

Historically, the Veterans Health Administration has been at the forefront of implementing innovative strategies to improve access and meet the health care needs of Veterans. In 2006, the VHA implemented the Assessment and Prevention of Pressure Ulcers Handbook, which provided mandated procedures for assessment and prevention of pressure injuries in all clinical settings, including assessment of patients at the time of admission to a VHA health care facility, upon interor intra-facility transfer, discharge, and other times as appropriate. In 2011, the VHA expanded its guidance for preventing pressure injuries and provided a detailed overview for a comprehensive assessment for all relevant clinical care settings to include Acute Care, Inpatient Mental Health, Spinal Cord Injury (SCI) Centers, Community Living Centers (CLC), Home-Based Primary Care (HBPC), and Outpatient Primary Care. The VHA standardized a directive format to provide a policy and procedures for the prevention, assessment, and management of pressure injuries across all clinical settings.

DOD has set a goal of eliminating avoidable pressure injuries. In 2023, DOD published Practice Recommendations (PRs) on hospital-acquired pressure injuries utilizing the best information available at the time of publication, and in instances where definitive, well-designed randomized controlled trials were absent, the PR guidance was based on expert opinion.

Military personnel are at risk of developing a pressure injury during military aeromedical evaluation, prolonged field care (PFC), and during inpatient admission. During evacuation and PFC, Veterans and wounded US Military Service members may be at increased risk for PIs due to prolonged time periods on unpadded litters while awaiting transport. Furthermore, use of support straps over the occiput, scapula, sacrum, or buttocks, and heels can increase skin interface pressure, temperature, and moisture, as well as body repositioning.(24)

In DOD, and throughout the health care industry, maintaining skin integrity begins with each staff member identifying patients with, or at risk for, skin breakdown. Using best-practice strategies, DOD staff work diligently to prevent pressure injuries. Pressure injury prevention and management across the continuum of care is a priority for DOD. The development of this evidence-based clinical practice guideline (CPG) addresses the unique needs of Veterans, U.S. Military Service members, and their families, ensuring shared decision-making regarding pressure injury prevention practices and treatment.

##### _Problem_

A pressure injury (PI) is localized damage to the skin and/or underlying soft tissue, usually either over a bony prominence or related to a medical or other device. The injury can present itself as intact skin or an open ulcer and may be painful. The injury occurs as a result of intense and/or

prolonged pressure, or pressure in combination with shear. The tolerance of soft tissue for pressure and shear may be affected by microclimate, nutrition, perfusion, co-morbidities, and the condition of the soft tissue.(25) Pressure injuries are a cause of higher morbidity and mortality rates among hospitalized, institutionalized, mobility-compromised, as well as malnourished/undernourished individuals. Pressure injury prevalence and incidence rates, as well as other measurements, may be important indicators of patient safety. Most pressure injuries are avoidable; however, unavoidable pressure injuries may develop, and existing injuries may worsen despite appropriate care in certain high-risk individuals.

##### _Population_

The target population for this CPG is adults with or at risk for pressure injury. Maintaining skin integrity begins with each staff member identifying patients with or at risk for skin breakdown; this includes providers who give direct or indirect care to patients at an MTF. Pressure injury prevention and management across the continuum of care is a priority for DHA, VHA, and throughout the health care industry.

##### _Desired Outcome_

Eliminating avoidable pressure injuries is the ultimate goal of prevention. It is the care team’s objective to implement appropriate best-practice strategies to prevent pressure injuries and pressure-related complications. If a patient develops a pressure injury during an inpatient visit, a Patient Safety Report (PSR) is filed, treatment rendered, and appropriate consults (e.g., wound care nurse, registered dietitian) are submitted. Pressure injury data should be continuously monitored for trends and evaluated to identify opportunities for performance improvement.

### **D. Epidemiology of Pressure Injuries**

Pressure injuries affect nearly 3 million people in the United States.(26) Prevalence varies by health care setting: 25.2% in long-term acute care, 11.8% in long-term care (i.e., nursing homes), 9.7% in acute care, 12% in rehabilitation centers, and 0.02% to 10.8% in the community.(27) Annually, approximately 60,000 deaths are attributed to complications from pressure injuries, with an estimated mortality rate of 7.6%.(28)

Certain populations are disproportionately affected. The Intensive Care Unit (ICU) reports the highest incidence (41%) and prevalence (33%) among all healthcare settings.(29) Individuals with neurologic impairments have a pressure injury incidence of seven to eight percent, with a lifetime risk ranging from 25-85% due to impaired mobility status and lack of sensation.(29)

Regarding age, pressure injury prevalence demonstrates a bimodal distribution with younger males at higher risk due to traumatic Spinal Cord Injuries (SCIs).(29) Within the Veterans Health Administration, approximately 27,000 Veterans with SCIs receive care annually.(30) A recent study by Champagne et al. 2024(30) reported that among Veterans with SCIs, half of Stage 3 and Stage 4 PIs did not achieve healing at one month. Older adults present with multiple risk factors for PI development, and two-thirds of cases occur in adults aged 70 years and older, a figure expected to rise with the aging population.(29) This demonstrates the need for proactive prevention and management strategies.

Racial disparities are also evident. Patients with darker skin tones are more likely to be diagnosed with a severe-stage pressure injury compared to patients with light skin tones, in part due to

limited clinician training and resources to accurately identify early-stage skin changes in darker skin tones.(31,32) Harms et al. 2014(33) found that Black residents in nursing homes had a 1.7 times higher rate of pressure injuries than White residents. These racial disparities are prevalent in the acute care setting too.(34) As noted by Black et al. 2023, patients who identify as African American experience higher rates of pressure injuries and are more likely to present with advanced-stage injuries compared to those patients who identify as White. Furthermore, African Americans were more likely to be discharged with a pressure injury compared to their non-African American counterparts.(34) Collectively, these findings highlight the need for clinical screening tools validated across diverse racial and ethnic populations to support early detection and equitable care.

Malnutrition or risk of malnutrition is a well-established risk factor for PI development. In a systematic review and meta-analysis by Chen et al. 2023(35), the incidence of pressure injuries was 3.66 times higher in malnourished patients compared to patients who were well-nourished. Malnutrition as a result of inadequate nutrient intake, excess or imbalance of nutrients, or impaired assimilation or utilization negatively affects skin integrity and delays wound healing.

In acute care settings, the prevalence of PIs has remained relatively stable from 2008 (1.8%) to 2019 (1.9%); however, the severity has increased.(26) Using the US Nationwide Inpatient Sample from 2008-2019, among hospitalized patients with a pressure injury diagnosis, the proportion of severe pressure injuries (Stage 3 and Stage 4) increased from 32.4% in 2008 to 36.6% in 2019, highlighting the clinical burden of advanced-stage pressure injuries over time.(26) Similarly, Kayser et al. 2019(36), using data from the International Pressure Ulcer Prevention database, reported a decrease in superficial HAPIs between 2011 and 2016, while the prevalence of severe pressure remained unchanged.

### **E. At-risk Populations (e.g., SCI, PVD, etc.)**

Pressure injuries (PI) can occur in health care settings (e.g., hospital, nursing home), where care is provided by formal caregivers, or occur in the community (e.g., home, group home, assistive living), where care is provided by self, formal, or informal caregivers. The risk for health careacquired (HAPI) and community-acquired pressure injuries (CAPI) differs based on certain chronic, acute, and situational conditions, as well as history of a previous PI and skin strength/tissue tolerance. Each at-risk condition is described in the section below.

##### _Spinal Cord Injury and Disorders_

Damage to the nerve fibers and subsequent symptoms in spinal cord injury (caused by an event) or disorders (caused by a chronic disease process) are contingent on the completeness of the injury, where some function may be maintained, and the level of injury, affecting the nerve fibers below the injury. Individuals living with spinal cord injury are particularly at risk for pressure injuries due to reduced mobility as well as altered, or a lack of, sensation.(25) PIs can occur during the initial hospitalization, but more frequently occur in the community, where the individual is primarily responsible for CAPI prevention.

##### _Incontinence_

Individuals with incontinence have increased moisture in the skin. Excess moisture increases the risk of friction, and moist skin that experiences pressure is more prone to pressure injuries.(37)

##### _Peripheral Vascular Disease_

The narrowing of peripheral arteries that carry blood from the heart to other parts of the body can lead to reduced blood flow to the skin and reduced oxygenation in the tissues. This increases the risk of PIs as less pressure is required to cut off the blood supply to those areas.(37) In addition, edema associated with venous peripheral vascular disease causes structural changes to the skin layers and increases the risk of the development of pressure injuries.(37)

_Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD)_

Decreased oxygenation associated with COPD and other chronic pulmonary diseases causes tissue hypoxia and, therefore, increased risk of PIs. In addition, the immobility associated with COPD increases risk as well.(38)

_Dementia and Other Neurodegenerative Disorders_

In individuals living with advanced dementia, the associated immobility, spasticity, flexion, and lack of sensation all contribute to increased risk of PIs. Similarly, the rigidity associated with Parkinson’s disease and related disorders also contributes to increased risk of PI development.(38)

##### _Cardiac Disease_

Low cardiac output associated with advanced cardiac disease results in hypotension, decreased blood perfusion, and peripheral ischemia, which increases the risk of PI development.(38)

##### _Amputations_

Individuals with amputations who subsequently use prosthetic devices are also at high risk for CAPIs due to poorly fitted equipment and intermittent edema.(39)

##### _Vascular Procedures_

Individuals who undergo vascular surgery are at a higher risk for hospital-acquired pressure injuries due to their underlying vascular disease, the stress of the surgery(40), and altered mobility.

##### _Surgery/Operating Room_

Intraoperative pressure injuries are common due to immobility, positioning, equipment, and the length of surgery.(41,42)

##### _Intensive Care Unit_

Individuals receiving care in the intensive care unit (ICU) who have a higher risk of a PI are those who are intubated, sedated, hypotensive, have generalized edema, or have experienced urgent surgery.(43)

##### _Burns_

Risk factors previously discussed, including malnutrition, incontinence, edema, and mobility limitations, are present in most patients with significant burn injuries and are higher in those who are more critically ill, elderly, obese, immobilized, and those with amputations or recent fractures. In addition, treatment modalities for burns further worsen the PI risk, including large volume resuscitation, repeat operations, splinting, and immobilization.(44)

##### _Medical Devices_

Individuals with a medical device are two to four times more likely to develop a PI. Medical devices can put external pressure on the skin, causing a PI in the pattern or shape of the device. Common pressure injury-causing medical devices, such as nasal cannula, oxygen tubing, masks, Foley catheters, tubes, lines, splints, etc., are often necessary for the care of the patient; therefore, the healthcare team must be vigilant of the increased risk of PI with these devices.(45)

##### _Malnutrition_

Malnutrition includes a variety of disorders, including undernutrition, obesity, and abnormal micronutrient levels.(46) Other nutrition-related disorders that impact an individual’s nutrition status include frailty, sarcopenia, and cachexia, which have multifaceted pathogenic origins.(47,48) Inadequate nutrition and fluid intake affect skin integrity and pressure injury healing.

##### _Sedentary Lifestyle_

Pressure injuries are associated with prolonged pressure on the skin in one position, particularly over bony prominences. The risk of pressure injuries is higher when coupled with other chronic conditions.(49)

##### _Aging_

Age-related changes in skin integrity increase the risk of PI in this population. The skin becomes drier, less elastic, and has more wrinkles. There is also a loss of subcutaneous fat to protect the skin from bony prominences. All of these changes increase the risk of pressure injury development.(50)

### **F. Financial Impact (Medicare)**

The cost of treating pressure injuries (PIs) in the U.S. is substantial. Separate from communityacquired pressure injuries, approximately 2.5 million hospital-acquired pressure injuries occur every year, which are largely preventable.(51,52) Pressure injuries can increase hospitalization costs by lengthening the duration of a hospital stay by 57% and are associated with a 22% higher rate of 30-day readmissions.(51) On average, a single stage 3, 4, or unstageable pressure injury costs $75,000 to $100,000 per patient to treat.(51,52) Estimates of the total cost of treating hospital-acquired pressure injuries in the U.S. have ranged from $3.3 billion to $11 billion every year, although Medicare claims for chronic pressure injury care (which includes both inpatient and outpatient care) are much higher at $22 billion.(52) The Veterans Affairs hospital system also estimates higher costs to the U.S. healthcare system for HAPI at $26 billion annually, or in other words, $1 million every 20 minutes.(53) The Veterans Affairs (VA) healthcare system, in particular, serves a large number of patients with spinal cord injuries, of which a third will develop pressure injuries within a year of their spinal cord injury.(53) Veterans with spinal cord injuries and pressure injuries have higher rates of hospitalization and healthcare costs than those without, costing about $70,000 more per patient and extending hospitalizations by nearly 52 days.(54) This adds up to an extra $89 million in costs to the VA system.(54)

Due to the preventable nature of hospital-acquired pressure injuries, the U.S. Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) has reduced reimbursement for treating these injuries, leaving hospitals to incur the cost.(52) CMS uses the Hospital-Acquired Condition (HAC)

Reduction Program to encourage hospitals to improve safety and implement best practices to reduce their rates of hospital-acquired pressure injuries associated with health care.(55) The program reduces payments to hospitals based on performance and quality measures of HACs, including pressure injury rate.(55)

In terms of the financial burdens and cost to the patient, Medicare Part A will help cover the costs of pressure injuries if the patient is receiving care for their pressure injury in an acute care hospital, skilled nursing facility, inpatient rehabilitation facility, or long-term care hospital (with a deductible).(56) For patients with Medicare Part B, some help with the costs of pressure injury care may be provided as an outpatient or in a clinic, but individuals are expected to cover 20% of the approved amount plus the deductible.(56) Patients with Medicare Part A and Part B can receive home health care services, such as part-time or intermittent skilled nursing care or home health aide care, to cover PI care as long as certain conditions are met.(56)

### **G. Morbidity and Mortality**

Many complications can develop for patients with pressure injuries. The most common major complication is infection of the local wound site. Other complications may include: spreading cellulitis, deep soft tissue infection, osteomyelitis, bacteremia, sinus tract abscess, septic arthritis, and, for patients with chronic pressure injuries, squamous cell carcinoma.(29) Osteomyelitis and infection should be considered for any pressure injuries that fail to heal, as sepsis occurs in approximately 26% of patients with non-healing wounds secondary to osteomyelitis.(29) Additionally, there is a negative impact on quality of life, and many patients with pressure injuries will have pain, depression, increased psychological stress, and may experience loss of work or increased burdens on their family, who already often participate in the patient’s care.(29,51)

Mortality rates are approximately five percent higher for patients with hospital-acquired pressure injuries than those without, and in some studies, the odds of dying in the hospital are 2.8 times higher for elderly patients with pressure injuries.(51,52) While most patients will die from medical conditions other than their pressure injuries, pressure injury complications can be a contributing factor and are believed to be the direct cause of 60,000 deaths annually.(29,30,51-53) Furthermore, pressure injury-associated mortality rates, even adjusting for age, were four times higher for Black populations than White populations, highlighting healthcare disparities impacting mortality rates as well.(57)

## **III. Scope of This Guideline**

This CPG is based on published clinical evidence and related information available from January 1, 2015, to March 31, 2025. Although the CPG is intended to improve the quality of care and clinical outcomes (see Introduction), it is not intended to define a standard of care (i.e., mandated or strictly required care).

### **A. Guideline Audience**

This CPG is designed to assist providers in the healthcare team in the Department of Defense and Department of Veterans Affairs healthcare systems, and healthcare team partners in the community who assess and manage patients at risk for pressure injuries.

The target population for this CPG is adult patients aged 18 years or older with or at risk of a pressure injury who are users of the VA and DOD healthcare systems. ## **VI. Summary of Guideline Development Methodology**

The methodology used in developing this CPG follows the Guideline for Guidelines, an internal document of the VA/DOD EBPWG updated in January 2019 that outlines procedures for developing and submitting VA/DOD CPGs.(58) The Guideline for Guidelines is available at http://www.healthquality.va.gov/policy/index.asp. This CPG also aligns with the National Academy of Medicine’s (NAM) principles of trustworthy CPGs (e.g., explanation of evidence quality and strength, management of potential conflicts of interest [COI], interdisciplinary stakeholder involvement, use of SR and external review).(59) Appendix A provides a detailed description of the CPG development methodology.

### **A. Evidence Quality and Recommendation Strength**

The Work Group used the GRADE approach to craft each recommendation and determine its strength. Per the GRADE approach, recommendations must be evidence-based and cannot be made based on expert opinion alone. The GRADE approach uses the following four domains to inform the strength of each recommendation (see Determining Recommendation Strength and Direction)(60):

- Balance of desirable and undesirable outcomes

- Confidence in the quality of the evidence

- Patient or provider values and preferences

- Other implications, as appropriate (e.g., resource use, equity, acceptability, feasibility, subgroup considerations)

Using these four domains, the Work Group determined the relative strength of each recommendation ( _Strong or Weak_ ). The strength of a recommendation is defined as the extent to which one can be confident that the desirable effects of an intervention outweigh its undesirable effects and is based on the framework above, which incorporates the four domains.(61) A Strong recommendation generally indicates High or Moderate confidence in the quality of the available evidence, a clear difference in magnitude between the benefits and harms of an intervention, similar patient values and preferences, and understood influence of other implications (e.g., resource use, feasibility). A recommendation’s strength (i.e., Strong versus Weak) does not reference its clinical importance (e.g., a Weak recommendation is evidence-based and still important to clinical care).

In some instances, the systematic evidence review might have found little or no relevant evidence, inconclusive evidence, or conflicting evidence for a particular therapy or intervention. The way this finding is expressed in the CPG might vary. The Work Group might include a statement among its recommendations acknowledging insufficient evidence for or against a commonly practiced intervention, particularly if it lacks supporting clinical evidence and poses potential risks (e.g., high opportunity cost, misallocation of resources). In other cases, the Work Group might choose to remain silent in cases where evidence is lacking for a rarely used intervention or when an intervention, despite the absence of recent evidence, is considered the standard of care and has a favorable balance of benefits and harms.

Using these elements, the Work Group determines the strength and direction of each recommendation and formulates the recommendation with the general corresponding text as shown in Table 2. The strength of each recommendation is shown in Recommendations.

**Table 2. Strength and Direction of Recommendations and General Corresponding Text**

|**Recommendation Strength and Direction**|**General Corresponding Text**|
|---|---|
|Strongfor|We recommend . . .|
|Weak for|We suggest …|
|Neither for nor against|There is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against . . .|
|Weak against|We suggest against . . .|
|Strong against|We recommend against . . .|

This CPG’s use of GRADE reflects a more rigorous application of the methodology than previous iterations; the determination of the strength of the recommendation is more directly linked to the confidence in the quality of the evidence on outcomes that are critical to clinical decision-making. The confidence in the quality of the evidence is assessed using an objective, systematic approach independent of the clinical topic of interest. Therefore, recommendations on topics for which designing and conducting rigorous studies (e.g., randomized controlled trials [RCTs]), might be inherently more difficult, are typically considered lower quality evidence and, in turn, are usually Weak recommendations. Recommendations on topics for which rigorous studies can be designed

and conducted (e.g., randomized controlled trials [RCTs]) may more often be Strong recommendations. Per GRADE, if the quality of evidence differs across the relevant critical outcomes, the lowest quality of evidence for any of the critical outcomes determines the overall quality of the evidence for a recommendation.(62,63) This stricter standard provides a consistent approach to determining recommendation strengths. For additional information on GRADE or CPG methodology, see Appendix A.

## **VII. Approach to Care in the Department of Veterans Affairs and the Department of Defense**

### **A. Patient-Centered Care**

VA and DOD encourage providers to be sensitive to demographic, cultural, and other differences that affect patients’ values, needs, and preferences, aimed at treating the condition while also optimizing the individual’s overall health and well-being. Regardless of the care setting, all patients should have access to individualized evidence-based care. Patient-centered care can decrease patient anxiety, increase trust in providers, and improve treatment adherence.(69,70) A holistic health approach (https:/www.va.gov/wholehealth/) empowers and equips individuals to meet their personal health and well-being goals. Good communication is essential and should be supported by evidence-based information tailored to each patient’s needs. Guideline recommendations should be applied in a holistic approach to care that is patient-centered, culturally appropriate, and available to people with limited literacy skills and physical, sensory, or learning disabilities.

### **B. Shared Decision-Making**

This CPG encourages providers to practice shared decision-making (SDM), a process in which providers, patients, and patient care partners (e.g., family, friends, caregivers) consider clinical evidence of benefits and risks as well as patient values and preferences to make decisions regarding the patient’s treatment.(71) Shared decision-making is emphasized in “Crossing the Quality Chasm”, an Institute of Medicine, now NAM, report in 2001 (72) and is a core component of a patient-centered, whole health approach. Providers must be adept at presenting information to their patients regarding individual treatments, expected risks, possible outcomes, and levels and/or settings of care, especially where patient heterogeneity in weighing risks and benefits might exist. The VA and DOD have embraced SDM. Providers are encouraged to use SDM to individualize treatment goals and plans based on patient capabilities, needs, values, and preferences.

### **C. Patients with Co-occurring Conditions**

Co-occurring conditions can modify the degree of risk for pressure injuries, impact assessment and diagnosis, influence patient and provider treatment priorities and clinical decisions, and affect the overall approach to pressure injury prevention and management. Many Veterans, active duty Service members, and their families have one or more co-occurring conditions that may increase susceptibility to skin breakdown, delay healing, or complicate care planning. Because pressure injuries are often associated with complex medical, functional, and psychosocial factors, collaborative management with interdisciplinary care providers is often beneficial. Some cooccurring conditions may require early specialist consultation to guide prevention and treatment strategies or establish a shared understanding of how care should be coordinated.

##### **_Recommendation_**

1. We suggest the use of a validated risk assessment tool to predict the risk of pressure injuries.

**(Weak for | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

Several risk assessment tools demonstrated very low quality of evidence for predicting pressure injuries in one umbrella review (74) and one meta-analysis.(75) The Braden Scale demonstrated fair to good accuracy for predicting pressure injuries across many studies with large sample sizes, but these studies had limitations in study quality and risk for bias(74), in addition to heterogeneity.(75) Within the Hillier et al. 2025 umbrella review, the Saleh study indicated no difference between the Braden score and clinical judgement.(74) Pang and Wong added two measures to the Braden Scale, which demonstrated good predictive accuracy in one systematic review and two studies with large sample sizes, but also demonstrated serious limitations in quality and risk for bias.(74) Song and Choi added two additional measures to the original Braden Scale, demonstrating excellent predictive accuracy in a large sample size, but also demonstrated serious limitations in the quality of the study and risk for bias.(74) Within the Hillier umbrella review, Webster reported that the Waterlow scale had poor to good predictive accuracy across three systematic reviews with large sample sizes, but had very serious limitations in study quality, risk for bias, inconsistency, and imprecision (74), and also demonstrated no difference with clinical judgement.(74) Within the Hillier umbrella review, Lospitae-Gomez reported that the Norton Scale demonstrated fair to good predictive accuracy in two systematic reviews and one large cohort study for use in the intensive care unit, but serious limitations in study quality, risk for bias, and imprecision.(74) The Cubbin and Jackson scale had good to excellent predictive accuracy in three systematic reviews with large sample sizes, but also demonstrated very serious limitations in study quality and risk for bias, and serious limitations in imprecision.(74)

A focus group was conducted with five Veterans or their caregivers to gain a better understanding of the patient perspective. Results indicated that participants want early detection and prompt treatment of pressure injuries, including regular skin assessments. Additional meta-synthesis studies of qualitative research outside the scope of this evidence report indicated that patients rely on providers to prevent pressure injuries, and nurses indicated that competing demands on workload impact their ability to prevent pressure injuries (76) affecting the feasibility of implementing risk assessment without recognition of workload. Also, nurses need training and retraining to accurately use the tools.(77)

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(74,75) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low. The body of evidence had some limitations, including very serious limitations in study quality and risk for bias, and serious limitations in imprecision.(74,75) The benefits of using a risk assessment tool to identify risk and initiate preventive activities outweigh the lack of training potentially leading to inaccurate results. Patient values and preferences were similar because they do not want a pressure injury, and it is a non-invasive provider-initiated assessment. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

##### **_Recommendation_**

2. We suggest the use of sub-epidermal moisture measurement to identify patients at risk for pressure injuries and/or early diagnosis of pressure injuries. **(Weak for | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

Subepidermal moisture measurement is a non-invasive tool that uses biocapacitance technology to detect localized tissue damage/edema before visual skin changes are apparent.(78) Elevated sub-epidermal moisture numbers have been shown to be an indicator of early pressure injuries, and can allow healthcare team members to identify patients at risk for pressure injuries, enabling early interventions.

Evidence from one systematic review (SR) by (79) evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of subepidermal moisture scanners versus standard visual skin assessment (VSA). Three studies found the sensitivity of sub-epidermal moisture to be >80% as compared to 50% with VSA. In addition, one non-RCT study found sub-epidermal moisture (intervention) vs. standard VSA (control) resulting in a 93% decrease in pressure injuries in the sub-epidermal moisture group. Two studies reported that sub-epidermal moisture readings indicated a pressure injury on average four days earlier than VSA. One study found that pressure injury detection using sub-epidermal moisture was on average 8.2 days before VSA.

In addition, evidence from four studies in one SR (80) favored sub-epidermal moisture over VSA. Four studies in one SR (81) found that sub-epidermal moisture measurement demonstrated good interrater reliability, lending to the tool’s consistency in ability to predict/measure skin status. Of note, all these studies were rated as having a Very low quality strength of evidence.

The Work Group found that the benefits outweigh potential harm or burdens. The benefits include early detection/prevention of pressure injuries. The potential harm/burdens found were that subepidermal moisture can generate false positives, leading to unnecessary and costly interventions.

Patient values and preferences were evaluated and found to be similar. Subgroup considerations across studies consistently found that sub-epidermal moisture eliminated bias in detecting early tissue damage across all skin tones (though the Work Group notes that more studies are needed in darker skin tones). While the cost of sub-epidermal moisture scanners needs to be evaluated when considering resource use, it is worth noting that the cost of a sub-epidermal moisture scanner must be weighed against the potentially enormous savings in pressure injury care and management. The feasibility of implementing sub-epidermal moisture scanners in the daily workflow by healthcare staff was also considered. Nurses and other healthcare team members would need to be trained to use sub-epidermal moisture scanners, but studies found that nursing staff found the tool quick to use and interpret.(80)

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(78-85) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low. The body of evidence had some limitations, including small sample sizes in single-center studies, limited patient mix, and wide standard deviations in available

studies.(78-85) The benefits of the use of sub-epidermal moisture to identify patients at risk for pressure injuries and/or early diagnosis of pressure injuries outweighed the potential harm/adverse events, which were small. Patient values and preferences were similar because patients do not want to develop pressure injuries, and patients want a non-invasive risk identification and diagnostic tool. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

##### **_Recommendation_**

3. We suggest thermography for risk assessment and/or early diagnosis of pressure injuries. **(Weak for | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

Thermography is a non-invasive imaging technique that detects temperature changes in the skin. Infrared radiation emitted from the skin surface is detected as temperature measurements. Temperature changes in the skin can be the result of poor blood flow, inflammation, or tissue damage.(86) The variations in temperature can be used to identify early pressure injuries, even prior to visual detection.

Evidence from one systematic review (SR) by (87) found that thermography had good predictive accuracy (sensitivity 0.85, specificity 0.90) for predicting pressure injuries.

Oohasi et al. 2025 (88) followed patients for eight weeks and found that thermography had good predictive accuracy for recurrent pressure injuries (sensitivity 0.80, specificity 0.94), lending to the tool’s consistency in its ability to predict/measure skin status. In addition,(86) Burke et al. 2025 found that thermography had a positive predictive value of 75.5% and a negative predictive value of 88.9% in predicting new and existing pressure injuries. Of note, all these studies were rated as having a Very low strength of evidence.

The Work Group found that the benefits outweigh potential harm or burdens. The benefits include early detection and prevention of pressure injuries, thereby decreasing complications such as pain and improving quality of life. The potential harm is that thermography can generate false positives, leading to unnecessary and costly interventions.

Patient values and preferences were evaluated and found to have some variation. Patients do not want to develop pressure injuries, and they would like a non-invasive tool to detect/diagnosis pressure injuries. However, patients may be fearful of new technology, and it can be timeconsuming for the patient/family. Subgroup considerations across studies consistently found that thermography eliminated bias in detecting early tissue damage across all skin tones (though the Work Group notes that more studies are needed in darker skin tones). While the cost of thermography needs to be evaluated when considering resource use, it is worth noting that the cost of thermography must be weighed against the potentially enormous savings in pressure injury care and management. Resources may also be limited in certain geographic locations. The feasibility of implementing thermography in the daily workflow by healthcare staff, as well as the training of healthcare staff, was also considered.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(81,86-89) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low. The body of evidence had some limitations, including study design, small sample sizes in single-center studies, limited patient mix, and wide standard deviations in available studies.(81,86-89) The benefits of the use of thermography to identify patients at risk for pressure injuries and/or early diagnosis of pressure injuries outweighed the potential harm of adverse events, which were small. Patient values and preferences had some variation, as patients do not want to develop pressure injuries, want a non-invasive risk identification and diagnostic tool, and are fearful of new technology. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

##### **_Recommendation_**

4. We suggest educating the healthcare team to enhance pressure injury identification and classification.

##### **(Weak for | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

The Work Group came to this conclusion based on the studies by Kim et al. 2020 (90) and Yuan et al. 2022.(91) This was based on the reasoning that, while the strength of evidence was Low, the benefits outweighed the harms/burdens. The benefits noted were that it is a noninvasive intervention and improves outcome identification of pressure injuries. The potential harm or burdens were that training attendance was a burden to staff with busy schedules, along with staff shortages and struggles to provide coverage. Kim et al. 2020 (90) showed that with training, there was improved visual discrimination and improved clinical judgement. Yuan et al. 2022 (91) showed improvement in classification and integrating preventative measures into clinical practice post-intervention.

Factors that led to this recommendation include benefits outweighing the harm or burdens as discussed above. Patient values and preferences aligned with this recommendation in that they indicated they valued knowledgeable and skilled providers. There is a cost to establish training, but once this training is established it will become a low-resource action. It is easy to implement but does require time on behalf of the healthcare team/provider. A barrier to implementation is acceptability - the provider may have preconceived or outdated strategies and knowledge from prior trainings. Medical providers may also feel that identification and classification are not their priority.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(90,91) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Low. The body of evidence had some limitations, including a small sample size, limited to the nursing staff studied. The benefits of educating the healthcare team to enhance pressure injury identification and classification improved outcomes of visual discrimination of pressure injury, improved clinical judgement, post-intervention classification skills, and improvement in integrating preventative care, outweighed the potential harm, which was primarily related to staff availability and coverage. Patient values and preferences were similar because

patients indicated that they valued knowledgeable and skilled providers. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

##### **_Recommendation_**

5. There is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against the use of serum biomarkers in assessing the risk of pressure injuries.

 - **(Neither for nor against | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

Individual non-specific serum biomarkers had low predictive accuracy, including C-reactive protein in the study by Hatanaka within the SR (92), serum hemoglobin in the study by Hatanaka and Anthony within the SR (92), and albumin in the study by Montalcini, Anthony, and Hatanaka within the SR.(92) Combined serum biomarkers with demographics (Hb, CRP, Alb, age, gender) demonstrated a moderate predictive accuracy for pressure injuries in the study by Hatanaka within the SR.(92)

A focus group was conducted with five Veterans or their caregivers to gain a better understanding of the patient perspective. Results indicated that participants want early detection and prompt treatment of pressure injuries. Additional meta-synthesis studies of qualitative research outside the scope of this evidence report indicated that patients rely on providers to prevent pressure injuries, which includes providing the appropriate information to detect a pressure injury.(76) If readily available, the serum blood biomarkers may provide insight into the management of pressure injuries, but alone, these biomarkers do not demonstrate good predictive accuracy - therefore, isolated lab draws are not recommended.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(92) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low. The body of evidence had some limitations, including poor predictive accuracy.(92) The benefits of using a bundle of serum biomarker values as part of risk assessment outweighed the potential harm if the lab values are available. Patient values and preferences were similar because patients do not want a pressure injury and prefer non-invasive treatments. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Neither for nor against_ recommendation.

##### **_Recommendation_**

6. We recommend implementation of patient centered pressure injury prevention programs to reduce pressure injury incidence. **(Strong for | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

The evidence reviewed included two randomized controlled trials (RCTs) by Tayyib et al. 2015 (93) and Chaboyer et al. 2016.(94) Both of these RCTs were initiated with patients in inpatient settings and had a follow-up of 28 days. In the Tayyib et al. 2015 (93) article, the team implemented a pressure ulcer prevention bundle that included risk assessment, skin assessment, skin care, nutrition, repositioning, support surface, education and training, and care of medical

devices. They had 70 patients in the intensive care unit (ICU) in each of the study arms and followed them every two days until death, discharge, or 28 days maximum. They found that the pressure ulcer cumulative incidence was higher in the control group (32.86%) compared to the intervention group (7.14%). The Chaboyer et al. 2016 (94) article described two components of their intervention that were delivered by either experienced nurses or experienced registered dietitians. One component was focused on nurses with additional education on pressure injuries, and the other focused on patients. The patient component included a five-minute DVD, information brochure, and poster with key messages to 1) keep moving, 2) look after skin, and 3) eat a healthy diet. This RCT had 799 patients in each arm, who were followed throughout the study. In the intervention arm, 31 did not receive the full intervention because of being too sick or tired, and some were lost to follow-up/withdrawal of consent in both arms; however, all 799 in each arm were analyzed based on their last skin exam and therefore included in their final study numbers. The pressure ulcer incidence rate was higher in the control group (10.5%) compared to the intervention group (6.1%). Both RCTs demonstrated a statistically significantly lowered incidence in the intervention groups.

These interventions have very limited burdens, and in addition to the lower incidence of pressure ulcers there are very likely unmeasured benefits not accounted for in these RCTs. These interventions are patient-centered and focused on the unique variables affecting each patient, which aligns them well with patient values and preferences. While some patients may be unable to participate meaningfully in a patient-centered initiative (i.e., too ill, major neurocognitive disorder, serious mental illness), the majority of patients would still benefit from an initiative focused on their unique needs, as discussed in the Tayyib et al. 2015 (93) RCT even if unable to meaningfully participate. Some health systems may find it difficult to provide education or find experienced staff to lead these types of initiatives; however, the evidence demonstrates that there is a benefit to the initial investment of time and resources for this education.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation Tayyib et al. 2015 (93) and Chaboyer et al. 2016.(94) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Moderate. The body of evidence had some limitations, including the inclusion of patients lost to follow-up in the final sample size.(94) The benefits of patient centered pressure injury prevention programs, reduction in pressure injury incidence, outweighed the potential harm, which was small. Patient values and preferences were similar because these initiatives are designed to be uniquely tailored to the patients. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Strong for_ recommendation.

##### **_Recommendation_**

7. We suggest early mobilization for decreasing the incidence of pressure injuries in the intensive care unit.

##### **(Weak for | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

The Work Group came to the recommendation of suggesting early mobilization based on low strength of evidence from the evidence review and clinical rationale. The evidence came from one

systematic review and meta-analysis Wang et al. 2020.(95) This study found that early mobilization and standard care in intensive care units (ICUs) resulted in decreased pressure injury rates compared to no early mobilization or delayed early mobilization and standard care. Overall, the study was rated as having a low strength of evidence, as there was noted to be bias due to lack of blinding, small sample sizes, and pressure injury rates were not a primary outcome. The benefits of early mobilization, including decreasing delirium, DVT, and incidence of pressure injuries (95) outweighed the harm of patients being vulnerable to injuries from early mobilization. This harm can be amplified in populations where a decrease in sensation can cause difficulty in determining the limits of range of motion or proprioception; however, it can be minimized as mobilization can be performed by skilled health care professionals, such as physical and occupational therapists or an interdisciplinary rehabilitation team.

Other factors that led the Work Group to the recommendation of early mobilization for decreasing the incidence of pressure injuries in the intensive care unit were the discussion of patient values and preferences. Most patients, if able, prefer engaging in their care through rehabilitation and early mobilization, which provides them with the ability to control their participation within their medically evolving environment. Considerations for ICUs to implement, such as feasibility, resource use, and subgroup considerations, were discussed as a Work Group. Feasibility for each hospital to incorporate the appropriate rehabilitation staff members to ensure the patient is maximizing independence and mobility, versus utilizing bedside nursing to perform these tasks with the support of safe patient handling equipment. Facilities should consider incorporating equipment (for example, mechanical lifts and friction-reducing devices) into routine repositioning and mobilization of patients who cannot be safely moved with manual techniques alone. Resource considerations were discussed as hospitals will need to train appropriate staff in safety when performing early mobilization with patients requiring ICU-level care. Geriatric, spinal cord injury, and critically ill patients were discussed as various subgroups to consider when performing early mobilization within the ICU.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation Wang et al. 2020.(95) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Low. The body of evidence had some limitations, including small sample sizes, high bias due to lack of blinding, and pressure injury rates were reported as a secondary outcome. The benefits of early mobilization outweighed the potential harm of patients being injured from early mobilization, which was small. Patient values and preferences were similar because patients would like to reduce their risk of developing a pressure injury and have the desire to be an active participant in their medical care if they are able. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation for early mobilization for decreasing the incidence of pressure injuries in the intensive care unit.

##### **_Recommendation_**

8. For patients at risk of pressure injury, we suggest family/caregiver pressure injury prevention education. **(Weak for | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

The evidence underpinning this recommendation was derived from one systematic review and two RCTs supporting family/caregiver pressure injury prevention education. In one RCT, education directed at relatives of palliative care patients had improved knowledge of pressure injury prevention better than routine discharge teaching.(96) There were mixed results regarding the impact of educating family/caregivers in preventing pressure injuries across several studies. In one quasi-experimental study with patients with spinal cord injury who received telephone support over 12 weeks post-rehabilitation discharge by Bloemen Vrencken within the Cogan study, there was no significant difference in the prevalence of pressure injuries.(97) In a clustered RCT of a 12-week educational intervention that included pressure injury prevention with persons with a new stroke and their families, there was no significant difference in pressure injury incidence.(98) However, Rintala in the Cogan review conducted a small RCT with Veterans with spinal cord injury post-surgical stage 3 or 4 PI repair and found participants who completed an individualized enhanced educational intervention consisting of four one-hour sessions before discharge with monthly phone calls after discharge for 24 months, in comparison to standard education with monthly or three-month contacts, demonstrated a significant decrease in the rate of pressure injury recurrence.(97) This suggests that individualized education with telephone follow-up for two years post-discharge of a surgical PI repair may be effective in reducing PI recurrence. A family educational intervention post stroke demonstrated a significant increase in patient functional status at four and twelve weeks, suggesting more family education post-stroke better prepares family for caregiving. The Deepradit et al. 2023 study also demonstrated a decrease in post-stroke patient depression at 12 weeks, but no change in caregiver stress or burden.(98) This suggests that a family educational intervention may increase patient quality of life after an initial injury.

Little research exists exploring the patient perspective of informal caregiver education, other than improving patient quality of life. The majority of the literature relates to the need for preventive care education for formal hospital-based caregivers. The clinical expert opinion of the review team suggests that family education promotes empowerment and improved care at home, and that patients value family efficacy and control. Informal education can ease patient stress, knowing there is help at home in pressure injury prevention practices if family is available. Family education requires staff time, equipment, and workflow integration.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(96-98) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Moderate. The body of evidence had some limitations, including variations in educational strategies (telephone follow-up, face-to-face intervention, or combination) and time (four to twelve weeks), as well as differences in patient populations (spinal cord injury, palliative care, stroke). The benefits of caregiver education, including reduced pressure injury recurrence and improved patient quality of life, outweigh the time commitment for training. Patient values and preferences were similar because families want to be empowered and have efficacy to prevent pressure injuries at home. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

##### **_Recommendation_**

9. For patients at high risk of pressure injuries in hospital and residential care settings, we suggest the use of reactive air surfaces and alternating pressure (active) air surfaces compared to foam surfaces for the prevention of pressure injuries. **(Weak for | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

The four RCTs and one meta-analysis (99) included here evaluated the use of different support surfaces and devices for the prevention of PIs in the hospital and surgical setting. The overall quality of evidence from the four RCTs was limited due to small sample size,(100,101) lack of intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis (101-103), and risk for bias due to device manufacturer sponsorship.(103) Shi et al. 2021 (99) included six Cochrane reviews (81 RCTs total) in its body of evidence. Support services for the prevention of pressure injuries were evaluated in 68 of the 81 included RCTs. Their network meta-analysis compares the effectiveness of PI prevention between different types of interventions/support surfaces, described as contrasts. The inclusion criteria were any patients in any clinical setting. However, Shi et al. 2021 (99) describes that the quality of evidence from their review was rated as Low or Very low due to the fact that the included RCTs in their review were also limited by high risk of bias, heterogeneity, limited followup duration, small sample sizes, and sparse data. Therefore, the overall quality of evidence was rated as Very low.

Of the 68 RCTs in Shi et al. 2021 (99) that evaluated the use of different support surfaces for the prevention of pressure injuries, only 40 were included in the network meta-analysis. The network focused on five types of support surfaces: alternating pressure (active) air surfaces, reactive air surfaces, foam surfaces, reactive sheepskin surfaces, and reactive gel surfaces (as classified in the NPIAP S3I, 2007). Foam surfaces were defined as non-powered reactive foam surfaces, which could include a foam mattress replacement, solid foam overlay, viscoelastic foam mattress/overlay, or others. “Standard hospital surfaces” were occasionally listed in RCTs as the control surface; however, these were poorly defined or not defined at all, and the authors attempted to specifically label what type of mattress or surface was being used, as NPIAP S31 2007 discourages using the term. The primary outcome of PI prevention was evaluated by pressure injury incidence, defined as either the proportion of participants developing a new pressure injury or the time to development of injury. Most of the RCTs included patients at risk of developing a PI, as assessed by the Waterlow, Norton, or Braden Scales. Shi et al. 2021 (99) describes the heterogeneity of the included RCTs (due to varying primary outcomes, outcome assessments, definition of standard of care, etc.); the high risk for bias due to lack of blinding of participants, personnel and outcome assessments; sparse data (42% of contrasts between different types of interventions were based on only one study); small sample sizes (majority of contrasts had <500 participants); a limited follow up duration (14 days on average); funding for studies coming from industry; and small number of events (PIs) per contrast. The evidence in their analysis was therefore downgraded for within-study bias, heterogeneity, and imprecision. They break down findings into overview findings/direct findings from the included studies, and network meta-analysis findings.

In terms of direct evidence, four Cochrane reviews evaluated 21 direct comparisons between different types of mattresses and support surfaces for preventing PIs; only seven had moderate or low quality evidence. This evidence suggests that reactive air surfaces and alternating pressure (active) air surfaces may reduce pressure ulcer risk compared with foam surfaces for people in acute and long-term care settings; alternating pressure (active) air surfaces and reactive sheepskin surfaces may reduce pressure ulcer risk compared with standard hospital surfaces for people in acute and long-term care settings; and reactive water surfaces may reduce pressure ulcer risk compared with 'standard hospital surfaces' for people in acute care settings only (an acute care setting such as a hospital wards unit or intensive care unit).

Additionally, there was evidence that alternating pressure (active) air surfaces applied on both operating tables and hospital beds may reduce pressure injury risk compared with reactive gel surfaces used on operating tables followed by foam surfaces applied on hospital beds; and in operating rooms, reactive gel surfaces probably reduce pressure ulcer risk compared with the 'standard hospital surfaces' that were not well described. The network meta-analysis overall found that there is low-certainty evidence that use of reactive gel surfaces may prevent PIs compared to foam surfaces, particularly in patients in the operating room or long-term care setting, but the confidence interval for this analysis crossed 1 (CI: 0.22 to 1.01), and therefore the WG did not make these recommendations. The authors of Shi et al. 2021 (99) also noted that there was Moderate quality evidence involving the use of reactive gel surfaces on operating tables followed by the use of foam surfaces on ward beds, but they deemed that the sequential use of two different support surfaces was relevant specifically to operating rooms only and therefore was not considered as a key intervention for the network meta-analysis.

They found that there was low-certainty evidence that the use of reactive foam surfaces or alternating pressure surfaces may reduce the risk of developing new PIs compared to foam surfaces. There was also low-certainty direct evidence that the use of reactive air surfaces may reduce the hazard of developing a new PI over 14 days compared to alternating pressure surfaces in the nursing home setting. This is different from international guidelines (25) and other Cochrane reviews (104) that recommend the use of foam surfaces for preventing PIs. Additionally, the network meta-analysis found that the use of reactive air surfaces may increase complete ulcer healing compared to foam surfaces. However, the WG did not make this recommendation as the CI crossed 1 (CI: 0.96 to 1.80).

It was felt that in the surgical setting, where patients are sedated, they likely would be accepting of any of the support surfaces and devices studied here. However, in terms of resource use, it was unclear whether the cost of these support surfaces was high or what the availability of these support surfaces is at different facilities. In terms of the perfusion enhancement system, there was also the concern that the use of this device may not be accepted by the healthcare team, as it places a burden on the healthcare team to have to learn how to operate and manage the device. Shi et al. 2021 (99) note that there may be higher costs when using reactive air surfaces compared to foam surfaces in the long-term care setting, but that the use of alternating pressure surfaces for PI prevention may be a more cost-effective choice compared to foam surfaces based on their limited cost-effectiveness analysis. Additionally, Shi et al. 2021 noted that there was little reported on the use of different support surfaces and patient comfort, adverse events, or impact

on quality of life. However, the data that were reported suggest there was no difference in quality of life between the different support surfaces evaluated.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(99-103) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low. The body of evidence had some limitations, including small sample size(100,101), small number of RCT studies evaluating a specific intervention(103), lack of ITT analysis(101-103), and risk for bias due to device manufacturer sponsorship.(99,103) The benefits of potentially preventing pressure injuries outweighed the potential harm of increased costs to the healthcare system and lack of available resources. Patient values and preferences were similar because of the studies that reported patient quality of life; there appeared to be no difference between the different support surfaces. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

##### **_Recommendation_**

10. In patients at risk for pressure injuries, we suggest the use of foam dressings for the prevention of sacral pressure injuries, based on patient characteristics and preferences. **(Weak for | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

Six RCTs informed this recommendation, and while most of the studies found that the use of sacral foam dressings in addition to standard care significantly reduced the incidence of pressure injuries compared to standard care alone, one study found different findings in different settings (105), and one study found that the use of sacral foam dressings was associated with increased risk for pressure injuries (106) and was stopped early due to that risk. However, that study, Gazineo et al. 2020 (106), has significant limitations. This study was conducted in post-operative patients who had recently had hip replacement surgery. For any patients who did not have a bowel movement within 48 hours, they were given daily enemas. Additionally, more patients within the control group were given urinary catheters than those in the intervention group. Because of these significant confounders in Gazineo et al. 2020 (106), the WG felt that the overall collection of evidence from the remainder of the RCTs favored the use of sacral foam dressings in addition to standard care in at-risk patients for the prevention of sacral pressure injuries.(105,107-110) There were limitations in the other remaining RCTs, including small sample size (106), lack of ITT analysis (107,108), lack of blinding (105-110), the use of block randomization (108), randomized patients at the facility level, limited follow-up duration (105-107,109,110) and risk for bias due to funding from dressing or device manufacturers.(107,108) There were several larger studies identified in this evidence base (105,107-110), but due to the limitations noted above, the overall quality of evidence was rated as low.

Standard of care was often poorly described, or described as per hospital protocol (105,107,108,110), but Beeckman et al. 2021(109) defined standard of care as ongoing risk assessment, regular repositioning, skin care, and use of available support surfaces. The locations and outcomes assessed also varied between studies, as some studies not only evaluated the use of dressings for sacral PI prevention, but heel PI prevention or trochanter PI prevention as well,

and at different PI stages. While Forni et al. 2022 (105) and Oe et al. 2020 (110) evaluated sacral PI incidence of any stage, Beeckman et al. 2021 evaluated the incidence of sacral, heel, and trochanter PIs at stage II and above, but found no difference in heel or trochanter PI incidence with the use of foam dressings in addition to standard of care at these sites. Santamaria et al. 2018 (108) evaluated the incidence of sacral and heel PIs in the community (aged care facilities), while Santamaria et al. 2015 (107) evaluated patients in the ICU setting. While Santamaria et al. 2018 (108) did not find any difference in heel PI incidence between groups in the community setting, Santamaria et al. 2015 (107) did find a significantly lower incidence in both sacral and heel PIs in ICU patients who used dressings in addition to standard care. Gazineo et al. 2020 (106), as mentioned, evaluated the incidence of sacral PIs in post-operative patients after hip replacement surgery. It was also the smallest study and did not find a statistical difference in PI incidence between groups, with the CI being very wide (0.91 to 53.87), partly because the study was stopped early. Additionally, the definition for at-risk patients varied in all included studies, with atrisk patients defined as having Braden scores ≤12 (108), ≤16 (105), ≤17 (109), all critically ill patients (107) or patients with persistent diarrhea and/or fragile skin.(110)

The potential benefit of the use of foam dressings is that there is evidence that they prevent the incidence of sacral PIs, which can be debilitating, costly, and impact patients’ quality of life. The potential harms include skin tears or irritation from the foam dressing adhesives (109,110), device failure from rolled-up edges or poor adhesion (109), or the risk for incontinence-associated dermatitis or PIs if soiled dressings are not promptly removed.(106) The skin also needs to be evaluated below the dressing daily, which can place a burden on the care team. As seen in most RCT study protocols, these dressings should be removed promptly if soiled or dislodged.

Patients are likely tobe in favor of using foam dressings in addition to the standard of care for the prevention of sacral PIs unless they have uncontrolled urinary or fecal incontinence, as the dressings can then be easily soiled. There were also several subgroup considerations identified. Forni et al. 2022 (105) evaluated PI incidence in the ICU, medical unit, and surgical unit settings, and only noticed a significant difference in PI incidence in the medical and surgical unit settings but not in the ICU, which is different from the findings from Beeckman et al. 2021 (109), which also evaluated patients in both medical units and the ICU and found a significant difference in both settings, and Santamaria et al. 2015 (107) that evaluated patients only in the ICU and also found a significant difference. Additionally, there were no studies conducted on patients at home, although Santamaria et al. 2018 (108) was conducted in aged care facilities/community settings, somewhat limiting its generalizability. Furthermore, patients who are prescribed foam dressings for sacral PI at home require education in dressing replacement and use.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(105-110) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Low. The body of evidence had some limitations, including significant confounders (106), small sample size (106), lack of ITT analysis (107,108), lack of blinding (105- 110), the use of block randomization (108) (Santamaria et al. 2018 randomized patients at the facility level), limited follow-up duration (105-107,109,110) and risk for bias due to funding from dressing/device manufacturers.(107,108) The benefits of sacral PI prevention outweigh the potential harm of skin irritation, skin tears, incontinence-associated dermatitis, and device failure

from poor adhesion or rolled-up edges. Patient values and preferences were similar because likely most patients would be okay with using sacral foam dressings in addition to the standard of care for the prevention of PIs, whether in the hospital or community setting, as long as caregivers and patients were instructed on use. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

##### **_Recommendation_**

11. There is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against the use of disposable underpads and/or reusable underpads for the prevention of pressure injuries. **(Neither for nor against | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

Three RCTs (111-113) and one quasi-experimental study (114) were reviewed, all of which were rated poor quality due to a lack of blinding (111-114), no intention-to-treat analysis (111-114), small sample sizes (111,113,114), lack of effective randomization/presence of potential confounders (111,112,114), and only one study found a significant difference in pressure injury incidence between intervention and control groups.(112) Choi et al. 2021 (111) studied the use of uncoated WYPALL paper (a multitask cleaning wipe/cloth) applied in the sacral region for the prevention of sacral PIs compared to standard care and found no difference between groups. This study was limited by the potential confounder that patients in the intervention group were turned more frequently, as the paper had to be removed immediately if soiled from urine, feces, or sweat. Additionally, this study was conducted outside of the U.S., and while WYPALL materials appear to be available in the U.S., they are primarily used as general cleaning supplies/wipes; there is no evidence that it is used in direct patient care in this population. Francis et al. 2017 (112) evaluated disposable versus reusable underpads for the prevention of pressure injuries in hospitalized patients and found a significant difference that favored the use of disposable underpads. It was also the largest study, enrolling over 400 patients. The study utilized cluster randomization wherein entire Med/Surg units were randomized, however, which led to significant baseline differences between groups. The study was also limited by bias, in that the hospital performing the study was using reusable underpads at that time for patient care, and staff wanted to switch to the use of disposable underpads, so this study was conducted to justify their request. Kim et al. 2023 (114) was removed from this recommendation as this study evaluated the use of a smart automatic defecation treatment system for the treatment of pressure injuries in nursing home patients in Korea, and at present, this device does not appear to be used for patient care within the United States. Studies were also limited by short follow-up duration, ranging from five days to two weeks (111,114), or were not specified.(112)

Potential benefits found by these studies included a reduction in pressure injury risk with the use of uncoated paper, and reduced pressure injury incidence with the use of disposable pads compared to reusable pads. Significant adverse events with the use of uncoated paper were not reported, but the Work Group had concerns that if the uncoated paper became soiled and was not removed in a timely manner, it would increase the risk for dermatitis. There was no difference in incontinence-associated dermatitis with the use of disposable or reusable underpads.

While quality of life metrics and patient satisfaction were not evaluated in these studies (111,112), the Work Group concluded that patients, family members, and care team members would likely be in favor of the use of disposable pads over reusable pads due to ease of use, although there may be a higher associated cost. In terms of feasibility, it was thought that most hospitals and care facilities likely already used disposable pads over reusable pads in patient care. However, regarding uncoated paper use, the study explicitly stated that the paper could not be wrinkled during use and had to be removed immediately if it became soiled with feces, urine, or even sweat. There were concerns about whether this was feasible and the burden this would place on the care team. There were also subgroup considerations identified and concerns for generalizability in that Choi et al. 2021 (111) was performed only on neurological ICU patients.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(111-114) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low. The body of evidence had some limitations, including a lack of blinding (111-114), no intention-to-treat analysis (111-114), small sample sizes (111,113,114), and lack of effective randomization/presence of potential confounders.(112,114) Additionally, only one study found a significant difference in pressure injury incidence between intervention and control groups.(112) The benefits of a possible reduction in pressure injury risk and incidence outweigh the potential harm of dermatitis, as no difference in incontinence-associated dermatitis was seen in patients who used disposable or reusable underpads. Patient values and preferences varied somewhat because while most patients and care team members would likely prefer disposable underpads over reusable underpads, it is unclear if they would be in favor of uncoated paper use. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Neither for nor against_ recommendation.

##### **_Recommendation_**

12. We suggest the use of heel offloading devices in addition to standard care for the prevention of heel pressure injuries. **(Weak for | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

##### _Offloading Devices_

Several studies evaluated the use of offloading devices for heel PI prevention, including four RCTs and one systematic review.(115-119) It should be noted that the systematic review included here, Greenwood et al. 2022 (115), would classify boots and pillows as offloading devices, and therefore some of the RCTs included here (116,118,119) evaluated the use of boots vs. pillows for the prevention of heel PIs and would be considered a comparison of one offloading device to another offloading device. Additionally, the systematic review by Greenwood et al. 2022 (115) is a review of 15 RCTs, nine of which evaluate the use of dressings for heel PI prevention, and only five of which evaluate the use of offloading devices for heel PI prevention. Three of those five RCTs evaluated the use of offloading devices in addition to standard of care compared to standard of care alone for heel PI prevention, while the other two RCTs compare one offloading device to a different offloading device. These five RCTs within the systematic review will be discussed in further detail here, as it impacts the recommendation. These studies have several

limitations impacting the strength of the recommendation, including small study size (117-119), lack of blinding (117,118), risk for bias due to funding from device manufacturers (117,118), lack of ITT analysis (116-119), potential confounders (116), failure to meet sample size targets,(117) and confidence intervals included in the study that crossed 1.(119) It should also be noted that Greenwood et al. 2022 (115) observed that of their 15 included RCTs, only five performed ITT analysis, and it is unclear if patients lost to follow-up were random across treatment arms or free from bias because reasons for loss of follow-up were not reported. There was also a general lack of blinding. They also performed sensitivity analyses (of best care/worst case scenarios) that changed the direction of effect for all but one trial, suggesting that the findings from the RCTs’ primary analyses were not robust. Therefore, the overall strength of evidence is Very low.

Of the four RCTs from our evidence review, three are a comparison of one type of offloading device compared to another. Both Arslan et al. 2024 (119) and Meyers et al. 2017 (118) compared the use of an offloading boot compared to standard of care with a pillow for the prevention of heel PIs as well as plantar flexion contractures in ICU patients. Barakat-Johnson et al. 2022 (116) also evaluated the use of an offloading boot (Prevalon) compared to standard of care for the prevention of heel PIs and plantar flexion contractures in ICU patients; however, the control group in Barakat-Johnson et al. 2022 (116) had two ICUs use a foam pillow applied under the calves of patients as standard of care, while one ICU used Posey Heel protector boots as standard of care. Therefore, Barakat-Johnson et al. 2022 (116) compared an offloading boot to both pillows and a different type of boot, creating a possible confounder, as not all members of the control group received the same treatment. Of these studies, while Barakat-Johnson et al. 2022 (116) and Meyers et al. 2017 (118) found a significantly lower incidence of heel PIs in the groups that used boots over pillows, Arslan et al. 2024 (119) also found no difference between treatment and control groups (CI crossed 1). Two RCTs from the systematic review by Greenwood et al. 2022 (115) are also comparisons of different types of offloading devices. Of these two RCTs, one compared an offloading device (EHOB foot waffle) to pillows, and the other evaluated three treatment arms, comparing bunny boot, eggcrate heel device, and an EHOB device, for the prevention of heel PIs. They did not find any difference between groups. Greenwood et al. 2022 (115) noted several limitations in these two RCTs, including a lack of ITT analysis, and one study had a small sample size. Therefore, when combining this data with the other RCTs in this evidence review, there is overall conflicting data on whether offloading boots are more effective than pillows or other offloading devices in preventing heel PIs. Greenwood et al. 2022 (115) also found no difference between heel PI incidence when comparing offloading devices to constant low-pressure devices.

As mentioned previously, three RCTs within the systematic review evaluated offloading devices in addition to standard of care compared to standard of care alone for the prevention of heel PIs. They did find that there was a lower incidence of heel PIs of both Stage 1 and above and Stage 2 and above for patients using offloading devices compared to standard care (this systematic review separated findings by PI stages in this manner). Of these three RCTs, two were smaller studies, and one did not perform an ITT analysis.

Lastly, Cleves et al. 2022 (117) evaluated the use of low-friction booties in addition to standard of care compared to standard of care alone (which included alternating pressure mattresses) for the

prevention of heel PIs. The Cleves et al. 2022 study (117) had a small sample size and did not find a significant difference between groups.

##### _Dressings_

As mentioned previously, there were several studies that evaluated not only the use of dressings for sacral PI prevention but also heel PI prevention.(107-109) The Greenwood et al. 2022 (115) systematic review also included several RCTs that evaluated the use of dressings for heel PI prevention. The majority of studies found no significant difference between groups and also had several limitations, including lack of blinding (107-109), risk for bias due to funding from dressing/device manufacturers (107,108), lack of ITT analysis (107,108), and the use of block randomization (108) (Santamaria et al. 2018 randomized patients at the facility level).

Beeckman et al. 2021 (109) evaluated the use of dressings to prevent the incidence of sacral, heel, and trochanter PIs at stage 2 and above, but found no difference in heel or trochanter PI incidence with the use of foam dressings in addition to the standard of care at these sites. Santamaria et al. 2018 (108) evaluated the incidence of sacral and heel PIs in the community (aged care facilities), while Santamaria et al. 2015 (107) evaluated patients in the ICU setting. While Santamaria et al. 2018 (108) did not find any difference in heel PI incidence between groups in the community setting, Santamaria et al. 2015 (107) did find a significantly lower incidence in both sacral and heel PIs in ICU patients who used dressings in addition to standard care.

Greenwood et al. 2022 (115) also evaluated the use of foam dressings and multilayer silicone foam dressings for the prevention of heel PIs and the majority of evidence suggested that there was no difference between groups (no difference in Stage 1 and above or Stage 2 and above heel PIs between foam heel dressings and standard of care; no difference in Stage 2 and above heel PIs between multilayer silicone foam dressings and standard of care; there was a significantly lower incidence of Stage 1 and above heel PIs with multilayer silicone foam dressings compared to standard of care). It should be noted that Greenwood et al. 2022 (115) included Beeckman et al. 2021 (109), Santamaria et al. 2015 (107) and Santamaria et al. 2018 (108) in its analysis. Overall, the majority of evidence does not indicate a significant difference between using dressings and the standard of care for the prevention of heel PIs.

In terms of risks and benefits, there is a potential benefit in that using heel offloading devices in addition to the standard of care may prevent both heel PIs and also reduce flexion contracture risk, especially in critically ill patients. The studies noted that the offloading boots hold the foot and leg in positions to avoid flexion contractures and protect the peroneal nerve from damage.(116) However, there was concern from staff that the boots may increase fall risks in patients.(117)

For heel dressings, there does not seem to be enough evidence to suggest that the use of dressings in addition to the standard of care prevents heel PIs. Furthermore, several studies noted increased risk for harm when using heel dressings. Beeckman et al. 2021 (109) mentioned patients had increased difficulty with walking if they had heel dressings applied. Some studies also mentioned that several patients fell if they had heel dressings on, or that there was staff concern for patients falling if they had heel dressings applied.

Greenwood et al. 2022 (115) noted that several of the included RCTs reported that there were issues with patient compliance, acceptability of the offloading devices, and decreased patient quality of life when using these devices. There was also concern from this working group about the availability of these devices in different treatment facilities, where pillows are likely less expensive. There are several subgroup considerations. One is that several of the RCTs included here (116,118,119) were all performed on ICU patients, which limits the generalizability to other patients.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(107-109,115- 119) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low. The body of evidence had some limitations, including small study size (117-119), lack of blinding (117,118), risk for bias due to funding from device manufacturers (117,118), lack of ITT analysis (116-119), potential confounders (116), did not meet sample size targets (117) and the confidence intervals included in the study crossed 1.(119) The benefits of preventing heel PIs and reducing risk for flexion contractures outweighed the potential harm of increased risk for falls. Patient values and preferences varied somewhat because it was documented in several studies that there was patient noncompliance and issues of acceptability with the devices, and decreased quality of life with device use. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

##### **_Recommendation_**

13. There is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against the use of turning and repositioning reminder systems for the prevention of pressure injuries.

 - **(Neither for nor against | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

The Work Group came to the recommendation of insufficient evidence based on the combination of a very low strength of evidence from the evidence review, and clinical rationale. The evidence base came from two RCTs: Kapp et al. 2023 (120) and De Meyer et al. 2019 (121), and a single cluster RCT: Ziegler et al. 2023 (122), which reviewed the use of various turning systems to assist in performing pressure-relieving repositioning positions within hospital settings, primarily within the intensive care units.

There were concerns with all the studies, as they received a poor study quality rating and demonstrated very serious limitations. All studies took place in hospital or ICU settings, limiting the patient population and use to within hospital settings. Kapp et al. 2023 (120) had a low sample size, with enrollment being discontinued due to the COVID-19 pandemic, and stated that their trial was underpowered, indicating that the “findings do not provide an indication of the clinical effectiveness of the interventions.” De Meyer et al. 2019 (121) had a lack of blinding. Ziegler et al. 2023 (122) study was not powered for pressure injury, but instead for immobility rate, which was assumed to be much lower. This indicates that the study did not have sufficient power to examine the effect of the particular turning system that was utilized.

The Work Group concluded that the benefits of utilizing a turning and repositioning reminder system slightly outweighed the harms/burdens, as medical staff would have reminders to turn and

reposition patients who are immobile and incapable of performing for themselves. These turning and repositioning reminder systems are expensive and could add to hospital waste of current equipment, as some systems are unable to be added onto current equipment despite their potential to prevent pressure injuries. Other factors that led the Work Group to the recommendation of insufficient evidence was the discussion of patient values and preferences. We concluded that there would be some variation in patients’ desire to utilize various turning and repositioning reminder systems. These systems may cause possible irritation to a patient’s skin if adhesive sensors need to be placed for monitoring their position. There is an increased burden associated with being turned/repositioned while the patient is sleeping, changing their sleep preferences, and maintaining hygiene. The last patient value that was discussed was the concern of pain associated with changing positions, affecting their level of comfort. Minimal concerns arise regarding a patient’s privacy as their movement is monitored nonstop by these turning systems. Other patients and caregivers may like the security of these systems, providing external reminders to medical staff to ensure they are being repositioned and turned to hopefully prevent a pressure injury.

Other considerations that were discussed within our Work Group were resource use, encompassing the time it would take to train bedside staff on the appropriate use of equipment to maximize its potential, as well as determining the most appropriate means to identify appropriate patients to utilize this equipment. Turning and repositioning systems could reduce the mental burden on bedside medical staff by eliminating the need to set an individual reminder to reposition patients who are not physically capable. The biggest consideration discussed by the interdisciplinary Work Group was the feasibility and acceptability of turning and repositioning reminder systems in terms of financial burden and other implications for the healthcare facility. The financial implications for the hospital facility to install, utilize, and train staff may outweigh the benefits, as multiple studies indicate there was no difference detected between specialized turning systems, with or without individualized repositioning schedules, and usual methods.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(120-122) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low. The body of evidence had some limitations, including small sample sizes, limited follow-up - Ziegler et al. 2023 (122) no follow-up, Kapp et al. 2023 (120) five days, and De Meyer et al. 2019 (121) eight days - and lack of blinding. The benefits of utilizing a turning and repositioning reminder system slightly outweigh the potential harm, cost of the device in a healthcare facility, or potential irritation from the adhesive if the turning system is used to track patients’ movements compared to pressure injury development. Patient values and preferences varied somewhat because patients may not wish to be monitored continuously, posing privacy issues, as well as the burden of disturbing sleep routines to be repositioned. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Neither for nor against_ recommendation for the use of turning and repositioning reminder systems.

##### **_Recommendation_**

14. There is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against a specific time frequency or specific positioning of head of bed tilt to prevent pressure injuries. **(Neither for nor against | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

The Work Group came to the recommendation of insufficient evidence based on the combination of a very low strength of evidence from the evidence review and clinical rationale. The evidence base came from one systematic review, Gillespie et al. 2020 (123), one cluster RCT, Yap et al. 2022 (124), and one non-randomized controlled trial, Daigné et al. 2023.(125)

The authors of Gillespie et al. 2020 (123) rated the included studies as poor, with a high risk of bias due to lack of blinding of nursing staff, missing data, and standard/routine care being illdefined. Imprecision was also noted within two RCTs that initially reported no difference in the tilt position of 30 degrees bed tilt compared to 90 degrees, while another RCT found an effect favoring 30 degrees bed tilt. The benefits of this study were the large sample size, multinational nature, and analysis within variable practice settings. This study also examined the frequency of turning, comparing two-hourly, three-hourly, and four-hourly repositioning regimens on highdensity mattresses, with no difference found.

Yap et al. 2022 (124) and Daigné et al. 2023 (125) were rated poor quality with serious imprecision, as there was wide confidence intervals were noted for both studies. Daigné et al. 2023 (125) was also rated Very low, as the study was non-randomized, and sample size calculations were not provided with a small sample size.

The Work Group determined that the benefits of repositioning at any frequency or position of head of bed outweighed the harms or burdens on medical staff or caregivers. Repositioning is a common preventive strategy and was determined to have a low risk of injury for the patient or staff when appropriately utilizing safe patient handling equipment and appropriate body mechanics. Most hospitals have a protocol regarding repositioning frequency as a standard of care for pressure injury prevention.

Other factors that led the Work Group to the recommendation of insufficient evidence were the discussion of patient values and preferences. Patients have similar values regarding the desire to prevent pressure injuries; however, they may not wish to participate in repositioning at the medical facility's recommended frequency due to various factors, including sleep hygiene, comfort with fluctuating pain, and difficulty accessing the environment while in various positions. Significant time was spent within our Work Group discussing other implications, including acceptability, resource use, and subgroup considerations. Most medical care settings already have predetermined protocols for frequency/timing of repositioning patients, and the Work Group further discussed the difficulty of facilities obtaining the appropriate number of staff to perform repositioning in various practice settings. Repositioning may require increased staff/caregiver assistance or require the use of safe patient handling equipment such as a mechanical lift to achieve positions. epending on the medical acuity, incontinence issues, or immobility level of the patient, as well as the practice setting in which the patient is residing. Gillespie et al. 2020 (123) identified no difference in pressure injury risk for 30 degrees vs. 90 degrees tilt for head of bed positioning, and no evidence was found for other surfaces, such as wheelchair or chair.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation, including one systematic review, Gillespie et al. 2020 (123), one cluster RCT, Yap et al. 2022 (124), and one non-randomized controlled trial, Daigné et al. 2023.(125) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed,_

_New-added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low. The body of evidence had some limitations, including Daigné et al. 2023 (125) having small sample size, non-randomized study and no sample size calculations; Gillespie et al. 2020 (123) authors identified all trials to be at risk for bias due to lack of blinding and missing data as well as routine care and standard positions were not defined; Yap et al. 2022 (124) with no patient follow up post four-week intervention at nursing home level. The benefits of repositioning in bed at a given frequency and position outweigh the potential harm of pressure injury development, decreased comfort, or medical staff burden. Patient values and preferences were similar because most patients wish to mobilize to prevent pressure injuries. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Neither for nor against_ recommendation for a specific time frequency or specific positioning of head of bed tilt recommendation to prevent pressure injuries.

##### **_Recommendation_**

15. There is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against the use of continuous body pressure mapping for reducing the incidence and prevalence of pressure injuries. **(Neither for nor against | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

The Work Group came to the recommendation of insufficient evidence based on the combination of a very low strength of evidence from the evidence review and clinical rationale. The evidence came from two RCTs, Ho et al. 2023 (126) and Gunningberg et al. 2017.(127) Both trials were conducted only within hospital settings, had small sample sizes, short follow-up time, and noted great heterogeneity. Ho et al. 2023 (126) was a well-conducted RCT, but demonstrated a high risk of bias due to missing data. The Work Group felt the benefits of real-time pressure mapping with identification of peak pressure points/data from a pressure-sensing mat on a mattress outweighed the risks. Some of the risks include preserved ethical concerns regarding continuous data collection on a patient, and potential adverse effects or development of other wounds from the sensor, which is an overlay on the mattress. We determined that the continuous bedside pressure mapping (CBPM) would provide vital real-time data for the health care team to assist with repositioning patients.

Other factors that led the Work Group to the recommendation of insufficient evidence were the discussion of patient values and preferences. We concluded that there would be some variation in patients' desire to utilize CBPM, as patients appreciate objective and visualization of high pressure if utilized as an educational tool to promote mobility and repositioning to prevent sustained high pressure on anatomical areas identified at risk for each patient. Other patients may not wish to be mobilized for the CBPM sensor to be placed under them while lying on the mattress. CBPM may be challenging for hospitals to implement due to the cost of the physical device, as well as allocating resources to train medical staff to appropriately identify patients for use, utilize the physical equipment, and understand and implement change with the real-time data the system is providing.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation Ho et al. 2023 (126) and Gunningberg et al. 2017.(127) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low. The body of evidence

had some limitations, including small sample size for both studies, a lack of blinding for Gunningberg et al. 2017 (127) and Ho et al. 2023,(126) and a lack of intention-to-treat analysis, indicating a fatal flaw in the study due to missing data at the beginning of their study. The benefits of the use of CBPM outweigh the potential harm of adverse events, which were small. Patient values and preferences were similar because patients value objective and visual feedback for themselves and their health care clinicians who are working to prevent pressure injuries while hospitalized. ##### **_Recommendation_**

16. We suggest against the routine use of prone positioning techniques for prevention of pressure injuries.

##### **(Weak against | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

The Work Group came to the recommendation to suggest against the routine use of prone positioning techniques for the prevention of pressure injuries based on the combination of a moderate level of evidence from the evidence review and clinical rationale. The evidence came from one umbrella systematic review, Patton et al. 2022 (128), which examined 10 systematic reviews and the impact of prone positioning on pressure injury in adults in ICUs. Although the strength of evidence was Moderate, there was no follow-up noted, and pressure injuries were reported as a secondary outcome. The Work Group determined from our clinical experience that the harms of increased prevalence of pressure injury slightly outweighed the benefits of offloading other anatomical areas. Prone positions are unavoidable at times due to medical necessity for improvement in respiratory symptoms or off-loading a patient’s anatomy following specific surgical cases; however, it is not recommended for the general population to prevent pressure injuries, as supported by Patton et al. 2022.(128) The evidence showed a higher incidence of pressure injuries in patients who were placed in prone positions, irrespective of their duration or frequency.

Other factors that led the Work Group to this recommendation were the discussion of patient values and preferences. We concluded that there would be some variation in patients' desire to be positioned in the prone position due to limiting their access and ability to interact with their environment, comfort, sleep hygiene, and overall quality of life. We discussed other implications, including the feasibility of achieving prone positioning for patients, which may require increased resources, staff, or the use of safe patient handling equipment to assist with mobilizing patients. Our Work Group discussed at length the potential medical implications that require a patient to achieve a prone position; for example, respiratory considerations and the need for medical staff to “assess patients’ skin and PI risk and proactively implement PI prevention strategies”.(128)

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation Patton et al. 2022.(128) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Moderate. The body of evidence had some limitations, including a single care setting, intensive care units, and systematic review authors rated the majority of the

included RCTs as fair. Patton et al. 2022 (128) noted that no follow-up was performed with the studies, and identified attendant risk of bias and how it affected the study results. The potential harm of increased incidence of pressure injury in prone positioning outweighs the benefits of offloading and repositioning when used with the general population. Patient values and preferences varied somewhat because some patients find prone positioning uncomfortable, and their quality of life may be diminished largely due to the inability to access and interact with their environment. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak against_ recommendation for the routine use of prone positioning techniques for the prevention of pressure injuries.

17. For the prevention and treatment of medical-device related facial pressure injuries, there is insufficient evidence to favor one of these interventions over the other:

 - bandage endotracheal tube fixation compared to a rigid endotracheal tube holder

 - skin barrier film or fatty acid solution

 - under-the-nose face mask or

 - microfoam surgical tape

##### **(Neither for nor against | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

We found seven RCTs that evaluated different interventions and their effect on preventing medical device-related pressure injuries (MDRPIs), and one RCT also evaluated the treatment and healing of MDRPIs. It should be noted that while most of these studies found significant differences in the incidence of MDRPIs or improved healing in MDRPIs favoring the intervention groups, these seven RCTs evaluated MDRPIs caused by different devices, in different locations, in different clinical settings, and evaluated or compared different interventions. Therefore, there is a limitation in consistency in that these interventions have a small number of RCTs (only one each) evaluating their effectiveness in preventing or treating MDRPIs.(129-135) Additionally, these studies were limited by a small sample size (129-133), low incidence of pressure injuries (129-131,133,134), indirectness in not fully describing the intervention or standard of care (129,134), the use of a composite outcome (134), failure to perform an ITT analysis (130,134,135), no finding of a significant difference between groups (133), conflicting findings (129,134) and lack of blinding.(129,130,132,133,135) Therefore, the strength of evidence from these studies was rated as very low.

Three studies evaluated different interventions for preventing facial MDRPIs in patients requiring non-invasive positive pressure ventilation (NIPPV), such as CPAP or BiPAP. Alfuqaha et al. 2024 (130) evaluated the use of hydrocolloid dressings plus repositioning the facial mask every two to four hours compared to standard care (defined as use of a fine mist and repositioning the facial mask every two to four hours) for preventing stage 2 or higher facial MDRPIs. Lemyze et al.2023 (132) evaluated the use of an under-the-nose face mask vs. an over-the-nose face mask for the prevention of facial MDRPIs. Otero et al. 2017 (135) evaluated four different treatment groups for the prevention of MDRPIs: the use of Allevyn thin foam dressing, Askina foam dressing, Linovera fatty acid solution, compared to no skin protectant underneath oronasal face mask for NIPPV. Hashimoto et al. 2023 (131) evaluated the use of 3M microfoam surgical tape compared to no

tape for preventing nasal MDRPIs for patients requiring nasotracheal intubation during various surgical procedures, while Miyashita et al. 2023 (133) evaluated the use of a skin barrier film compared to no film for the prevention of nasal MDRPIs in patients undergoing endoscopic sinonasal surgery. Genc et al. 2022 (129) and Landsperger et al. 2019 (134) both evaluated bandage tube fixation compared to rigid endotracheal tube fixation for the prevention of oral MDRPIs, while Genc et al. 2022 (129) also evaluated the healing/treatment of MDRPIs.

Different harms and benefits were identified depending upon the intervention discussed. The potential benefit of using surgical microfoam, skin barrier films, fatty acid solutions, or dressings is that they may potentially prevent MDRPIs. Miyashita et al. 2023 (133) noted that there was a significant reduction in nasal floor pain in patients who used the skin barrier film before endoscopic sinonasal surgery. The potential harms may include skin irritation from the adhesives, barrier films, or solutions. While the use of under-the-nose face masks may prevent facial MDRPIs in patients who require NIPPV, Lemyze et al. 2023 (132) demonstrated that these patients also experienced significant air leak with the use of this type of mask and needed to switch to a different type of mask/interface more often. Genc et al. 2022 (129) found that the use of bandage tube fixation may help in healing oral MDRPIs compared to rigid endotracheal tube fixation, while Landsperger et al. 2019 (134) found that the use of bandage tube fixation led to an increased risk for endotracheal tube dislodgement. Landsperger et al. 2019 (134) did not find a significant difference in facial skin tears or lip ulcers between groups using either bandage or rigid endotracheal tube fixation.

Likely, most patients would be in favor of using microfoam tape and skin barrier films during surgery. They may also be in favor of the use of fatty acid solutions and dressings if critically ill and require NIPPV, or to make the use of the face mask more comfortable, although patients requiring NIPPV and not critically ill may find the use of these dressings and films cumbersome. It is presumed that patients would not prefer an under-the-nose face mask compared to other types of masks based on the positioning of the mask and frequent air leak observed in the study by Lemyze et al. 2023.(132) Patients unconscious and critically ill in the ICU may not have a preference for either bandage or rigid endotracheal tube fixation, while a conscious patient in the ICU may prefer bandage tube fixation.

In terms of feasibility, most care teams and nurses are likely familiar with the use of hydrocolloid and foam dressings and microfoam tape. For the skin barrier and fatty acid solution, it would likely be easy to teach the application (barrier film and fatty acid solution application appears similar to applying an iodine swab). Additionally, most institutions already likely use either bandage or rigid endotracheal tube fixation techniques. In terms of resource use, bandage tube fixation is likely cheaper than rigid fixation, while the addition of microfoam, dressings, barrier films, and solutions in addition to standard care would likely create an increased cost in terms of requiring additional supplies but may reduce overall healthcare costs in terms of preventing MDRPIs and treatment. Additionally, it is unclear how widely available under-the-nose face masks are. There are several subgroup considerations, including that several of these studies were conducted on surgical and post-operative patients, or in the ICU and in critically ill patients, limiting generalizability.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(129-135) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-added._ The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low. The body of evidence had some limitations including a small number of RCTs evaluating each intervention (129-135), small sample size (129-133), low incidence of pressure injuries (129-131,133,134), indirectness in not fully describing the intervention or standard of care (129,134), the use of a composite outcome (134), did not perform an ITT analysis (130,134,135), did not find significant difference between groups (133), conflicting findings (129,134) and lack of blinding.(129,130,132,133,135) The benefits of possibly preventing or treating MDRPIs were balanced with the potential harms of skin irritation, endotracheal tube dislodgement, and the need to switch to a different NIPPV interface mask. Patient values and preferences varied somewhat because some patients may not prefer the under-the-nose face mask compared to other masks for NIPPV, and patient preferences for these devices or types of endotracheal tube fixation methods may vary depending on whether the patient is critically ill or not. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Neither for nor against_ recommendation.

19. We suggest the use of oral nutritional supplements that combine energy (calories), protein, and micronutrients for pressure injury improvement.

**(Weak for | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

This recommendation is primarily informed by the systematic review by Langer et al. 2024 (137) evaluating energy, protein, and micronutrient supplementation compared with a standard diet on pressure injury improvement and incidence. For treatment of existing pressure injuries, pooled data (n=577) from three RCTs, Ek et al. 1991, Ohura et al. 2011, and Yu et al. 2015 from the SR (137) demonstrated that oral nutrition supplements that combined energy, protein, and micronutrients increased the likelihood of pressure ulcer healing (RR 1.45; 95% CI 1.14 to 1.85; p=0.002). However, the strength of evidence was rated as low due to a very serious risk of bias across studies. For prevention, pooled data (n=1,634) from three RCTs (Ek et al. 1991, Arias et al. 2008, and Bourdel Marchasson et al. 2000) from the SR (137) showed little to no difference in pressure injury incidence between intervention and control groups (RR 0.92; 95% CI 0.71 to 1.19; p=0.52), also with low certainty of evidence. Excluding Ohura et al. 2011, patients in the intervention groups received a standard hospital diet supplemented with a high-calorie, highprotein oral nutrition drink. In the Ohura et al. 2011 study, patients in both the control and intervention group received a polymeric, standard enteral nutrition formula. Study duration ranged from 20 days to 182 days. Three RCTs, Ek et al. 1991, Ohura et al. 2011, and Yu et al. 2015, from the SR (137) excluded high-risk patient populations like individuals with uncontrolled diabetes mellitus (DM), and serious renal, liver, or heart disease. Overall, using oral nutrition supplements that supply energy (calories), protein, and micronutrients demonstrated potential benefit for healing pressure injuries with minimal demonstrated harm, though findings were limited by small sample sizes and methodological concerns.

Patient values and preferences may vary regarding high-calorie, high-protein oral nutritional supplements. The Patient Focus Group participants reported the importance of nutrition, especially protein, and hydration on healing pressure injuries, whether consumed by mouth or through a feeding tube. The Work Group concluded that some patients may find liquid oral

nutrition supplements easier to consume than increasing calories and protein through standard meals, while others may experience taste fatigue that affects adherence. Acceptability may be influenced by dietary restrictions or cultural considerations, as some products are not Vegan, Halal, or Kosher; however, there are increasingly more options commercially available for patients to meet their unique medical and dietary restrictions and preferences. Resource considerations are relevant, as costs are associated with supplementation, and coverage varies between the DOD and VA beneficiaries, potentially limiting access. Equity concerns arise for patients without financial means to obtain supplements when not covered by third-party payors. Despite these considerations, the potential for improved healing and the availability of multiple product options to accommodate varying preferences are in favor of supplementation when aligned with individual patient circumstances and clinical judgment.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation from three randomized controlled trials RCTs Ek et al. 1991, Ohura et al. 2011, and Yu et al. 2015 from the Langer et al. 2024 SR.(137) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-Added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Low. The body of evidence had some limitations, including a very serious risk of bias arising from the randomization process, deviations from the intended intervention (Ek et al. 1991, Ohura et al. 2011), and missing outcome data.(137) The benefits of improvement in the healing of pressure injuries, along with a variety of products to meet patient-specific needs and medical conditions, outweigh the potential harm. Patient values and preferences varied somewhat because of cost, variable insurance coverage, accessibility, acceptance (flavors, taste), and medical or cultural limitations (Kosher, Vegan). Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

21. For the improvement of pressure injuries, there is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against supplementation with:

 - fortified combination of amino acids, arginine, zinc, and antioxidants

 - arginine supplements

 - vitamin C supplements or

 - zinc sulfate supplements

##### **(Neither for nor against | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

This recommendation is primarily informed by systematic reviews by Cheshmeh et al. 2022 (138) and Langer et al. 2024 (137) evaluating micronutrient and amino acid supplementation. Moderate quality evidence (137,138) suggests that, in the absence of a micronutrient deficiency, the potential harm and burdens may slightly outweigh the benefits. This concern may be more pronounced in specific populations, older adults, individuals with diabetes or multiple comorbidities, patients with SCI, and critically ill patients, where some data suggest a potential increase in mortality risk.

Evidence for individual nutrients is limited. In Langer et al. 2024,(137) zinc supplementation did not result in complete pressure injury healing in patients with SCI (n=8). Prolonged high-dose zinc supplementation (e.g., 50 mg elemental zinc) may impair copper absorption. Zinc assessment requires clinical correlation, as it is a negative acute-phase reactant and may appear falsely low in the presence of inflammation or hypoalbuminemia. Clinical signs of deficiency include alopecia, dermatitis, hypogeusia, dysgeusia, diarrhea, anorexia, and impaired wound healing. Prior to supplementation, risk for deficiency (e.g., poor oral intake, diarrhea, certain gastrointestinal conditions), malnutrition assessment, nutrition-focused physical exam, and lab assay should be assessed to weigh benefits versus potential harm.

Vitamin C demonstrated a positive trend in reducing pressure injury size but did not achieve complete healing (Very low quality evidence).(138) Caution is warranted in patients with chronic kidney disease, a history of nephrolithiasis, or hemochromatosis. Deficiency manifestations include gingivitis, scurvy, petechiae, ecchymosis, purpura, and corkscrew hairs. Interpretation of laboratory values should consider inflammatory status (e.g., CRP), dietary intake, smoking status, and findings from a nutrition-focused physical exam.

Cheshmeh et al. 2022 (138) reported that arginine supplementation was associated with reductions in pressure injury size but not complete healing. Generalizability is limited by small sample sizes (eight RCTs, n=197), heterogeneity in dosing (2.5- 18 g), and short intervention durations (2–12 weeks). Arginine may benefit select populations (e.g., trauma or surgical patients) but use in critically ill patients with septic shock or hemodynamic instability may increase mortality risk.

Across studies, the evidence suggests that supplementation with individual micronutrients or amino acids may contribute to reductions in pressure injury size; however, there is insufficient evidence to support complete pressure injury healing. The strongest evidence supports adequate provision of energy, protein, and overall micronutrient intake to promote healing (138), suggesting a synergistic effect.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(137-139) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Moderate. The body of evidence had some limitations, including small sample sizes; inconsistent reporting of pressure injury characteristics and healing outcomes; variable doses of micronutrients and amino acid fortification, making comparisons between studies difficult; lack of dietary intake data; exclusion of key populations (e.g., diabetes, malnutrition); potential industry sponsorship; and short study durations. Potential harm includes adverse effects (e.g., gastrointestinal distress with high doses of Vitamin C), nutrient interactions (e.g., zinc-induced copper deficiency), and possible increased mortality risk in select populations (e.g., arginine in patients with septic shock). Cost and pill burden may outweigh benefits, particularly when nutrients can be obtained through food. Banks et al. 2023 (139) reported adverse events; however, attribution to specific ingredients versus combined formulations was unclear. Patient preferences may vary due to factors such as taste fatigue, supplement burden, cost, side effects, and the perception of “doing something” to support healing. Supplements may displace more nutrient-dense foods and contribute to unnecessary testing or oversupplementation. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Neither for nor against_ recommendation.

22. There is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against the use of one support surface over another for the management of pressure injuries including:

 - reactive air surfaces

 - foam surfaces

 - gel surfaces or

 - alternating pressure surfaces

**(Neither for nor against | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

Shi et al. 2021 (99) was the only study from this evidence base that evaluated the use of different support surfaces for the healing of pressure injuries. Shi et al. 2021 (99) was a network metaanalysis and included 6 Cochrane reviews and 81 RCTs, of which 12 RCTs evaluated different support surfaces for the treatment of pressure injuries.

This study concluded that there was no difference in the complete healing of PIs for foam surfaces (control) compared to reactive air surfaces, alternating pressure (active) air surfaces, or reactive water surfaces. There were several limitations in these 12 RCTs, including sparse data from these studies, small sample sizes, risk for bias due to funding from manufacturers, wide confidence intervals that crossed 1, and overlap between interventions in general. For this reason, the overall quality of evidence was rated as Very low.

The primary outcome of PI treatment was evaluated by the proportion of subjects with healed PIs or time to healing. The 12 RCT studies included a total of 972 participants with a median study sample size of 72. 11 of the 12 studies were either completely or partially funded by manufacturers/industry or received the surfaces/mattresses for free from the manufacturers. Shi et al. 2021 (99) found low-certainty direct evidence that patients using reactive air surfaces may be more likely to have healed pressure ulcers compared with those using foam surfaces in longterm care settings. Likewise, there was low-certainty evidence that more PIs may heal completely in patients using reactive air surfaces compared to foam surfaces, but this CI also crossed 1 (RR 1.32, 95% CI: 0.96 to 1.80). Therefore, the Work Group deemed there to be insufficient evidence to suggest that any support surface is better than any other for PI healing and treatment. There were no significant adverse events reported or evidence to suggest that adverse events occurred more with one support surface type than another.

Shi et al. 2021 (99) noted that there was little reported on the use of different support surfaces and patient comfort, adverse events, or impact on quality of life. However, the data that were reported suggest there was no difference in quality of life between the different support surfaces evaluated. Shi et al. 2021 (99) notes that there may be higher costs when using reactive air surfaces compared to foam surfaces in the long-term care setting, but that the use of alternating pressure surfaces for PI prevention may be a more cost-effective choice compared to foam surfaces based on their limited cost-effectiveness analysis. However, the data that was reported suggests there was no difference in quality of life between the different support surfaces evaluated. There was a concern that certain healthcare facilities may not have access to these different types of support surfaces studied here.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(99) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low. The body of evidence had some limitations, including sparse data, small sample sizes, risk for bias due to funding from manufacturers, wide confidence intervals that crossed 1, and overlap between interventions in general. The benefits of treating pressure injuries

outweigh the potential harm of increased costs or lack of available resources. Patient values and preferences were similar because of the studies that reported patient quality of life; there appeared to be no difference between the different support surfaces. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Neither for nor against_ recommendation.

Although a lower temperature at the pressure ulcer wound edge was significantly associated with the development of undermining one week after the temperature assessment. The strength of evidence for this study was very low because of a small sample size (n=22), and the absence of CI reporting for diagnostic accuracy estimates resulted in very low certainty of evidence.

The Work Group highlighted the importance of the utility of the test to assess delayed pressure injury healing, which supported VA/DOD focus group themes, which emphasized “the critical role of health interventions in supporting wound healing and pressure injury management”. A strategy to assess pressure injury healing introduced by the Work Group is the use of imaging technology. There are varied imaging technologies that can be used to detect underlying skin abnormalities, such as changes in cavity structure (e.g., undermining) and changes in periwound temperature. Some of these modalities can be expensive or unavailable in some clinical settings, which can impact patient routine and timely access to useful technology.(145) Therefore, the use of thermography can serve as a new strategy for real-time 2D imaging temperature measurement on the skin surface, wound, and periwound skin. In addition, the use of this method may help to identify such devitalized tissue. The temperature differences can indicate inflammation without clinical manifestations seen in patients with varied types of wounds (e.g., diabetic, pressure injuries, venous ulcers).

There was a decrease in temperature at the wound edge, as detected by thermography, which may predict the development of undermining, and is defined as tissue destruction or ulceration extending beneath the skin edge; thus, the wound bed of the pressure ulcer appears larger than at the surface. However, most evidence focused on diabetic foot ulcers. Norman et al. 2021 (146) conducted a systematic review and only one of the 21 included studies addressed pressure injuries; however, the review focused primarily on diabetic foot ulcers. Saiko and colleagues (147) conducted a systematic review that evaluated the use of hyperspectral imaging for assessing wound healing; however, only one of the 31 included studies addressed pressure injuries - most of the evidence focused on diabetic foot ulcers or peripheral arterial disease. These studies were outside of the scope of the review and did not influence the strength of the evidence. The Work Group could not determine the evidence for or against the accuracy and clinical utility of testing to assess delayed pressure injury healing due to the majority of available studies being focused on diabetic foot or venous leg ulcer.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation: Kanazawa et al. 2016.(145) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low. The body of evidence had some limitations, including a small sample size and no CI for estimates of diagnostic accuracy.(145) The Work Group found that the benefits slightly outweigh the harms/burden. The benefits include a

decrease in pain or discomfort for the patient, improving quality of life, care, and early identification, and reducing the delay in wound healing. The harms include the time for training and use of technology, which may require additional testing and could result in false positives if not done properly by clinical staff. However, patient values and preferences were similar because family and patients want knowledge about pressure injury and wound healing, time-consuming use of technology as a burden on family or patient, and fear of use of new technology. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Neither for nor against_ recommendation. Additionally, other implications include equity (e.g., generalized to one specific patient population), subgroup considerations (e.g., access), feasibility (e.g., medical resources/supplies impacted due to finances and access limited due to geography and maintenance of medical devices), and resource use (e.g., training of staff and lack of patient/family knowledge in technology).

##### **_Discussion_**

Based on the Work Group's chosen criteria, no evidence was found to support a _Neither for nor against_ recommendation for a surgical procedure for pressure injury management.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation with no evidence to support a _Neither for nor against_ recommendation for the effectiveness of any particular surgical procedure as compared to no surgery. Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-added_ . Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Neither for nor against_ recommendation.

##### **_Recommendation_**

27. For select patients, we suggest offering platelet-rich plasma (PRP) for promotion of healing of pressure injuries. **(Weak for | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

The Work Group came to the recommendation to suggest offering PRP based on eight RCTS within one systematic review.(148) Five of these eight RCTS were published in 2020 or later, indicating up-to-date findings. After anywhere from three weeks to 10 weeks of treatment, the complete healing rate had significantly improved in the intervention group compared to standard care. The wound size had also significantly reduced, and the Pressure Ulcer Scale for Healing (PUSH) scores significantly improved. The strength of evidence for these findings, however, was very low because of very small sample sizes and different outcome measures for assessing wound healing. Neither the reduction in the wound volume nor the adverse events differed between the two groups. PRP has variable and site-specific preparation, including centrifuge use, making interpretation of study results problematic.

PRP is an autologous treatment, requiring a weekly sample of the patient’s blood for the weekly treatments. There are inherent issues related to platelet concentration, based on the patient’s platelet count. PRP requires a phlebotomist, centrifuge equipment with anticoagulants, and trained staff to prepare the patient’s PRP at each weekly application. Patients may seek such treatments given the somewhat promising outcomes. However, non-emergency transportation to clinic visits can be a significant barrier. There are large equity issues in that this treatment is not available at every facility, given the equipment and staff requirements.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(148) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low. The body of evidence had some limitations, including a small sample size, wide confidence intervals that included both benefit and harm due to treatment, and very short follow-up points. The benefits of PRP outweigh the harms/burden of treatment. Patient values and preferences had some variation because some patients may want this invasive, frequent treatment, while others will not want the added burden of frequent clinic visits. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

##### **_Recommendation_**

28. For the promotion of healing of pressure injuries, there is insufficient evidence to support the use of any particular biologics, specifically

 - Activated Macrophage Suspension (AMS)

 - Extracellular Matrix Graft (OASIS)

 - Fibroblast-Derived Dermal Placement

 - Fibroblast-Derived Growth Factor

 - Granulo Macrophage Colony Stimulating Growth Factor

 - Sequential Fibroblast Growth Factor and Granulo Macrophage Colony Stimulating Growth Factor

 - Nerve Growth Factor

 - Protease Modulating Matrix

 - Recombinant Platelet-Derived Growth Factor

 - Transforming Growth Factor

##### **(Neither for nor against | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

The Work Group came to the recommendation of insufficient evidence based on very low strength of evidence from the evidence review and clinical rationale. The evidence base came from two systematic reviews (149,150) and one RCT,(151) all of which compared biological agents to a conventional therapy control for the treatment of pressure injuries. Of note, the evidence was also very dated, with the included RCTs being at least 15 years old.

Activated macrophage suspension (AMS) is injected locally around the wound. In one RCT in the systematic review by Camesi et al. 2023,(149) AMS treatment increased the percentage of completely closed wounds but had no effect on the time to complete wound closure one year from treatment. There were some concerns surrounding randomization and the potential for bias in the

measurement of the outcomes. There were also some very serious limitations in precision, given the small sample sizes. It is not clear that AMS is available in the United States for human use.

Extracellular Matrix Graft (OASIS) is a topical treatment with a scaffold of extracellular matrix that is composed of porcine small intestinal submucosa.(152) In the RCT by Brown-Etris et al. 2019,(151) OASIS 100% healing rate did not differ between groups, but the 90% healing rate favored the OASIS group. There were very serious limitations with regard to study quality and risk of bias. There was also serious imprecision, given the small sample size.

Fibroblast-Derived dermal replacement is a topical treatment. In one RCT (150) the complete healing rate did not differ between fibroblast-derived dermal replacement with standard of care and standard of care alone. The study had serious limitations in terms of study quality and risk of bias. There was also very serious imprecision with a very small sample size and no measure of dispersion. It is not clear that this is available in the United States.

Fibroblast-Derived Growth Factor is a topical treatment. In one RCT within a systematic review (150), the Growth Factor versus placebo had a wound surface area reduction of 70% or more and favored fibroblast-derived growth factor. In another study of an RCT within a systematic review (150), where they looked at growth factor plus standard of care versus standard of care alone, there was no difference in complete healing rate between the groups. The studies had serious limitations in terms of study quality and risk of bias. There was also very serious imprecision with a very small sample size in one study, and the other had a wide confidence interval that included both harm and benefit due to treatment. It is not clear that this is available in the United States.

Granulo Macrophage Colony Stimulating Growth Factor is locally injected around the wound. In one RCT within a systematic review (150), the 100% healing rate did not differ between patients at six weeks and one-year follow-ups for those who had received the Granulo Macrophage Colony Stimulating Growth Factor with standard of care versus those who only received standard of care. The study had serious limitations in terms of study quality and risk of bias. There were no serious inconsistencies or indirectness. The study had very serious imprecision, including a small sample size and a wide confidence interval that included both harm and benefit due to treatment. It is not clear that this is available in the United States.

Sequential Fibroblast Growth Factor and Granulo Macrophage Colony Stimulating Growth Factor studies combine the two previous treatments above. In one RCT within a systematic review (150), the complete healing rate did not differ between the patients receiving the sequential treatment and the placebo treatment. The studies had serious limitations in terms of study quality and risk of bias. There were no serious inconsistencies or indirectness. There was very serious imprecision in that there was a single very small study and a wide confidence interval that included both benefit and harm due to treatment. As mentioned with regard to these products alone, it is not clear that this is available in the United States.

Nerve Growth Factor is a topical treatment. In one RCT within a systematic review (150), the 100% healing rate significantly improved among patients at six-week follow-up for those patients who had received Nerve Growth Factor with standard care versus standard care alone. There were serious limitations with regard to study quality and risk of bias; there were no serious

inconsistencies or indirectness in the study. There were very serious imprecision concerns due to very small sample sizes. This product may be available for off-label use.

Protease Modulating Matrix is a topical treatment. In one RCT, Reddy et al. 2008, within a systematic review by Tricco et al. 2015,(150) after two to eight weeks of treatment, complete wound healing did not differ between those receiving Protease Modulating Matrix and those receiving petrolatum-soaked gauze. There were serious limitations with regard to study quality and risk of bias; there were no serious inconsistencies or indirectness in the study. There were serious imprecision concerns due to small sample sizes. Protease modulating matrix is available in the United States under the label Protease Modulating Matrix.

Recombinant Platelet-Derived Growth Factor is an injectable or topical treatment. In two RCTs within a systematic review (150), at four-week follow-up, for those patients who received recombinant platelet-derived growth factor and those who received placebo did not differ in their complete wound healing. Doses were 100 µg/ml once or twice daily or 300 µg/ml once daily. There were serious limitations with regard to study quality and risk of bias. One study had serious inconsistencies, where a high percentage of the patients had unexplained heterogeneity. There was no serious indirectness in the studies. There was very serious imprecision in the studies, small sample sizes, and wide to very wide confidence intervals that included both benefit and harm due to the treatment.

Transforming Growth Factor is a topical treatment, and the studies did not specify whether it was TGF-alpha or TGF-beta. TGF-alpha impacts epithelial migration and proliferation while TGF-beta contributes to inflammation regulation, cell proliferation and migration, and extracellular matrix deposition. In one RCT within a systematic review (150), the complete healing rate between those patients receiving transforming growth factor versus placebo did not differ at 16-week follow-up. There were serious limitations with regard to study quality and risk of bias; there were no serious inconsistencies or indirectness in the study. There were very serious imprecision concerns in that there was a very small sample size and no effect size or measure of dispersion. This product may be available for off-label use in the United States.

When the Work Group chose to look at biologics and efficacy in pressure injury treatment, the Work Group had hoped to uncover some data that reflected the efficacy of skin substitutes, including regenerative human cell and tissue products, animal-derived grafts, and synthetic alternatives. The increase in expenditure in this space has been extraordinary, and it is imperative that the research supports the use of these costly materials. Although the research for use in other wounds, such as diabetic foot ulcers and venous leg ulcers, may support the use of these types of skin substitutes, there were no randomized controlled trial studies that looked at their use in pressure injury healing.

The systematic reviews tended to cover multiple types of treatment in different types of wounds, not just pressure injuries. One cannot simply extrapolate that what works in one type of wound will also work in pressure injuries, as each type of chronic wound has different barriers to healing.

The majority of these products, aside from the Extracellular Matrix Graft and the Proteasemodulating matrix, would only be available for off-label use. When considering this

recommendation, there was some variation between patient values and preferences. Although a few showed some wound healing, the vast majority did not. Some treatments would entail painful weekly injections. There are also serious equity concerns regarding access to this technology and having trained staff to extract or properly apply such treatments. Many of the treatments require weekly application and need adequate wound bed preparation, in the form of sharp debridement, prior to use. Transportation to appointments, as well as trained staff to do adequate wound bed preparation, may be barriers to care.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation of insufficient evidence to support the use of these particular biologics.(149-151) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low. The body of evidence had some limitations, including small sample sizes and wide variation in confidence intervals that included both benefit and treatment. The benefits of these biologic treatments were balanced with potential harm, which was small. However, using an ineffective intervention may delay the application of an effective intervention. Patient values and preferences varied somewhat because some patients may prefer to try a treatment regardless of efficacy, while others would find the frequent clinic visits and lab draws overly burdensome. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Neither for nor against_ recommendation.

##### **_Recommendation_**

29. There is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against the following interventions for risk identification, prevention, and management of pressure injuries:

 - mobile applications

 - home video conference telemonitoring

 - automated phone counseling

##### **(Neither for nor against | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

The Work Group reviewed 2 RCTS with very small sample sizes that examined the same mobile health application in populations with either spina bifida or spinal cord injuries. The study by Kryger and colleagues (2019) tested the Interactive Mobile Health and Rehabilitation (iMHere) mobile application with a small group of patients with spinal cord injuries (n=38) who were randomized to receive intervention or standard care without technology support.(153,154) The study by Dicianno et al. (2016) tested the use of the same application in patients with spina bifida (n=23). The other studies identified were provider-focused rather than patient-focused.(153,154) The reasoning for this recommendation was due to large variations in patient engagement with mobile applications. In addition, there was no comparison to control groups documented in the literature. The sample sizes were also very small with large standard deviations. Lastly, the lack of detail pertaining to the outcomes of the study, as well as the lack of emphasis on the incidence and prevalence of pressure injuries, led to the conclusion that the evidence was insufficient.

There were benefits noted in the review of the studies by the Work Group. Mainly, the use of technology tools improved communication between patients and providers. A referenced increase in patient satisfaction was also appreciated. Thirdly, the lack of travel and associated costs for the

patient was a significant benefit. The only harm that was noted was the potential for patients to become frustrated with the technology based on unfamiliarity, which ultimately could lead to a lack of use.

The Work Group noted other considerations that contributed to and supported this decision of the evidence review. Many patients in the studies found the application simple to use and appreciated the non-invasive assessment it supplied. Patients also received automated counseling calls that were deemed beneficial, and patient satisfaction with the application was measured in both studies. In addition, it was determined that at-home monitoring is more cost-effective for the health system in comparison to long-term hospitalization.

It is significant to note that there were some drawbacks discussed and realized by the Work Group. Initially, it was recognized that the use of this technology would require training staff and devoting time so that training could take place. Some patients were noted as having hesitancy with using new technology, as well as other patients having hearing and vision impairments. These respective impairments would create an impediment to the use of the application. Also, the location of the patient and lack of access to the internet, such as in rural settings, may hinder the use of such an application and the relay of vital information to the provider. Lastly, it was highlighted that there were no within-group or between-group differences in the measurement of pressure injury incidence.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(153,154) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low. The body of evidence had some limitations, including small sample sizes with large standard deviations, large variations in patient engagement with the application, and no comparison to a control group, and a lack of emphasis on pressure injury incidence and prevalence. The benefits of application use for pressure injury assessment, risk, and wound management slightly outweigh the potential harm of lack of use and frustration. Patient values and preferences were similar because patients did not want a pressure injury, and some preferred non-invasive assessments. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Neither for nor against_ recommendation.

## **X. Research Priorities**

The development of the VA/DOD Clinical Practice Guideline for the Prevention and Management of Pressure Injuries highlights advances in the understanding and prevention of pressure injuries while also identifying important gaps in the current evidence base. Limitations remain in areas such as risk assessment, early detection technologies, preventive interventions, and treatment strategies across diverse care settings and patient populations. Continued research in the following priority areas is essential to strengthen the quality and applicability of clinical recommendations, particularly for Veterans and active duty Service members.

### **A. Advancing Risk Assessment and Early Detection of Pressure Injuries**

Further research is needed to improve the accuracy, predictive ability, and clinical utility of tools used to identify individuals at risk for pressure injuries. Current risk assessment instruments may not adequately capture risk across diverse populations, including individuals with darker skin tones, older adults, or patients with conditions affecting skin integrity or mobility, such as spinal cord injury. In addition, the effectiveness of risk prediction in episodic high-risk settings, such as perioperative environments, remains unclear and requires additional investigation.

Future studies should evaluate emerging diagnostic technologies that aim to detect tissue damage earlier than visual inspection alone. These include modalities such as sub-epidermal moisture scanners, thermography, ultrasound, and advanced imaging technologies. Larger comparative effectiveness trials across different clinical settings, including inpatient, outpatient, home care, and long-term care, are needed to determine their diagnostic accuracy, predictive value, and clinical utility. Research exploring biomarkers, such as inflammatory markers collected through non-invasive methods, may also provide promising approaches to detecting early tissue damage and differentiating early-stage pressure injuries from normal skin.

### **B. Strengthening Prevention Strategies and Clinical Interventions**

Although many prevention strategies are commonly used in clinical practice, additional highquality research is needed to determine the most effective approaches for reducing pressure injury incidence. Studies evaluating support surfaces, repositioning strategies, turning systems, and offloading devices are limited by small sample sizes, inconsistent outcome measures, and variability in study design. Future trials should examine optimal repositioning frequencies, pressure redistribution strategies, and positioning techniques across different care settings and patient populations.

Research is also needed to evaluate the effectiveness of additional preventive interventions, including sacral protective dressings, moisture management strategies, barrier creams, and medical device-related pressure injury prevention approaches. Comparative studies across different technologies and products, including support surfaces and medical devices, would help clarify best practices. Future research should also incorporate cost-effectiveness analyses, standardized outcome measures, and patient-centered outcomes to better inform clinical decisionmaking and resource allocation.

### **C. Optimizing Mobility, Rehabilitation, and Functional Interventions**

Mobility and rehabilitation strategies play an important role in pressure injury prevention, yet there is limited high-quality evidence guiding their optimal implementation. Further research is needed to evaluate structured early mobilization protocols, including programs implemented by nursing teams as well as individualized rehabilitation plans developed by physical or occupational therapists. Studies should examine these interventions across multiple care environments, including acute care hospitals, outpatient settings, home care, skilled nursing facilities, and longterm care environments.

Future research should also focus on specific high-risk populations, such as geriatric patients, individuals with spinal cord injury, and critically ill patients. Evaluating the effectiveness of existing mobility initiatives, including structured mobility and rehabilitation programs used within large healthcare systems, may provide valuable insights into their role in preventing pressure injuries. Additionally, research should explore how pressure mapping technologies, positioning strategies, and varying frequency of repositioning may assist clinicians in identifying optimal seating and support surfaces for individual patients.

### **D. Improving Patient, Caregiver, and Healthcare Professional Education**

Education and training remain essential components of effective pressure injury prevention programs. However, current research evaluating educational interventions is limited. Additional studies are needed to assess the effectiveness of structured educational programs for healthcare professionals, including physicians, advanced practice providers, nurses, and other members of the multidisciplinary care team. Research should also explore the most effective training content and delivery methods for improving prevention practices across healthcare systems.

There is also a need for research focused on patient and caregiver education following hospital discharge. Longitudinal studies should examine how well patients and families retain prevention knowledge over time and identify the types of educational resources that best support pressure injury prevention in home and community settings. Understanding patient and family perspectives will be critical to designing education programs that are accessible, culturally appropriate, and tailored to high-risk populations.

### **E. Understanding the Role of Nutrition, Lifestyle Factors, and Comorbidities**

Nutrition and lifestyle factors play a significant role in pressure injury development and healing, yet substantial gaps remain in the evidence base. Future research should investigate the effects of nutritional interventions, including total protein intake, amino acid supplementation, micronutrient deficiencies, and food fortification strategies. Studies comparing plant-based and animal-based protein sources and evaluating the role of key nutrients such as zinc and vitamin C in wound healing would further strengthen the evidence base.

Research is also needed to examine the impact of broader lifestyle and health factors, including mobility, exercise, substance use, comorbidities, and medication use, on pressure injury risk and recovery. These studies should include diverse patient populations and consider social determinants of health, such as access to nutritious food and variations in community resources.

Well-designed prospective studies will help clarify how these factors interact with clinical interventions to influence pressure injury outcomes.

### **F. Advancing Treatment Approaches and Pressure Injury Healing Therapies**

Further research is required to evaluate emerging and existing treatments aimed at improving pressure injury healing. These include therapies such as negative pressure wound therapy, topical oxygen therapy, electrical stimulation, platelet-rich plasma, skin substitutes, and other advanced wound care technologies. Many current studies evaluating these treatments are limited by small sample sizes, inconsistent methodologies, and short follow-up periods, making it difficult to draw definitive conclusions about their effectiveness.

Future research should focus on well-designed randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes and standardized outcome measures. Studies should also evaluate patient-centered outcomes, including quality of life, pain reduction, healing time, recurrence rates, and costeffectiveness. Additionally, research examining surgical interventions for advanced-stage pressure injuries should focus on identifying optimal patient selection criteria, appropriate timing of surgical procedures, and long-term outcomes following treatment.

#### **_a. General Criteria for Inclusion in Systematic Evidence Review_**

- Randomized control trials (RCTs) or systematic reviews (SRs) of RCTs published on or after January 1, 2015, to March 31, 2025.

- Studies must have been published in English.

- Studies must focus on pressure injuries to be included.

- Publication must have been a full clinical study or SR; abstracts alone were not included. Similarly, letters, editorials, research protocols, and other publications that were not fulllength clinical studies were not accepted as evidence.

- Systematic reviews must have searched MEDLINE or EMBASE for eligible publications, performed a risk of bias assessment of included studies, and assessed the quality of evidence using a recognizable rating system, such as GRADE or something compatible (e.g., the one used by the AHRQ Evidence-based Practice Centers [EPCs]). If an existing review does not assess the overall quality of the evidence, evidence from the review must be reported in a manner that will allow us to judge the overall risk of bias, consistency, directness, and precision of evidence. We will not use an existing review as evidence if we are not able to assess the overall quality of the evidence in the review. If multiple SRs address a KQ, we will select the most recent and/or comprehensive review.

- RCTs must have an independent control group. Randomized crossover trials will only be included if data from the first period (prior to treatment crossover) were reported separately or an adequate washout period was used.

- For KQs 1 (behaviors that affect risk of developing pressure injuries), prospective cohort studies will be included. For KQ2 (early risk assessment tools), 7 (tools for diagnosis), and 8 (delayed healing), diagnostic accuracy studies will be included. Similarly, SRs with these study designs will be included.

- Study must have enrolled at least 20 patients (10 per study group for RCTs and 20 for observational studies).

- Study must have enrolled at least 85% of patients who meet the study population criteria. If the patient population falls below this threshold but the relevant population of patients is reported separately, then that study will be included.

- To ensure applicability to the VA/DOD healthcare systems and consistency across the CPG program, inclusion of individual studies was limited to very high Human Development Index (HDI) countries with an index ≥0. where standards of healthcare are comparable (e.g., U.S., Canada, United Kingdom, Western Europe, Israel, Japan, Hong Kong, Australia, and New Zealand). Inclusion of SRs was limited to those in which more than half of the included studies were from eligible regions.

 - These regions of interest are listed in Table 1 of the Statistical Annex of the 2023/24 Human Development Report produced by the United Nations Development Program.

- For the cost of care outcome, only data from the U.S. will be included.

- Study must have reported on at least one outcome of interest.

#### **_c. Rating the Quality of Individual Studies and the Body of Evidence_**

Sigma Health Consulting assessed the methodological risk of bias of individual diagnostic, observational, and interventional studies using the USPSTF method. Each study is assigned a rating of _Good_ , _Fair_ , or _Poor_ based on a set of criteria that vary depending on study design.

Detailed lists of criteria and definitions appear in Appendix VI of the USPSTF procedure manual.(156)

Next, Sigma Health Consulting assessed the overall quality of the body of evidence for each critical and important outcome using the GRADE approach. This approach considers the following factors: overall study quality (or overall risk of bias or study limitations), consistency of evidence, directness of evidence, and precision of evidence. The overall quality of the body of evidence is rated as _High_ , _Moderate_ , _Low_ , and _Very Low._

### **C. Developing Evidence-Based Recommendations**

In consultation with the VA Office of Quality and Patient Safety and the Clinical Quality Improvement Program, Defense Health Agency, and Sigma Health Consulting convened a 3.5day in-person recommendation development meeting from February 3rd -6th , 2026, to develop this CPG’s evidence-based recommendations. Two weeks before the meeting, Sigma Health Consulting finalized the systematic evidence review and distributed the report to the Work Group; findings were also presented during the recommendation development meeting (see Determining Recommendation Strength and Direction).

Led by the Champions, the Work Group interpreted the systematic evidence review’s findings and developed this CPG’s recommendations. The strength and direction of each recommendation were determined by assessing the quality of the overall evidence base, the associated benefits and harms, patient values and preferences, and other implications.

#### **_a. Determining Recommendation Strength and Direction_**

Per GRADE methodology, to assess the quality of the evidence base and assign a grade for the strength for each recommendation, the GRADE system uses the following four domains to assess the strength of each recommendation(60):

#### _1. Confidence in the Quality of the Evidence_

Confidence in the quality of the evidence reflects the quality of the evidence base and the certainty in that evidence. This second domain reflects the methodological quality of the studies for each outcome variable. In general, the strength of recommendation follows the level of evidence, but not always, as other domains may increase or decrease their strength. The evidence review used for the development of recommendations for lipid management, conducted by Sigma Health Consulting, assessed the confidence in the quality of the evidence base and assigned a rate of “High,” “Moderate,” “Low,” or “Very low.”

The elements that go into the confidence in the quality of the evidence include:

- Is there high or moderate quality evidence that answers this question?

- What is the overall certainty of this evidence?

#### _2. Balance of Desirable and Undesirable Outcomes_

Balance of desirable and undesirable outcomes refers to the size of anticipated benefits (e.g., increased longevity, reduction in morbid event, resolution of symptoms, improved quality of life, decreased resource use) and harms (e.g., decreased longevity, immediate serious complications, adverse event, impaired quality of life, increased resource use,

inconvenience/hassle) relative to each other. This domain is based on the understanding that most clinicians will offer patients therapeutic or preventive measures if the advantages of the intervention exceed the risks and adverse effects. The certainty or uncertainty of the clinician about the risk-benefit balance will greatly influence the strength of the recommendation.

Some of the discussion questions that fall under this domain include:

- Given the best estimate of typical values and preferences, are you confident that the benefits outweigh the harms and burden or vice versa?

- Are the desirable anticipated effects large?

- Are the undesirable anticipated effects small?

- Are the desirable effects large relative to undesirable effects?

#### _3. Patient Values and Preferences_

“Patient values and preferences” is an overarching term that includes patients’ perspectives, beliefs, expectations, and goals for health and life. More precisely, it refers to the processes that individuals use in considering the potential benefits, harms, costs, limitations, and inconvenience of the therapeutic or preventive measures in relation to one another. For some, the term “values” has the closest connotation to these processes. For others, the connotation of “preferences” best captures the notion of choice. In general, values and preferences increase the strength of the recommendation when there is high concordance and decrease it when there is great variability. In a situation in which the balance of benefits and risks are uncertain, eliciting the values and preferences of patients and empowering them and their surrogates to make decisions consistent with their goals of care becomes even more important. A recommendation can be described as having “similar values,” “some variation,” or “large variation” in typical values and preferences between patients and the larger populations of interest.

Some of the discussion questions that fall under the purview of values and preferences include:

- Are you confident about the typical values and preferences and are they similar across the target population?

- What are the patient’s values and preferences?

- Are the assumed or identified relative values similar across the target population?

#### _4. Other Implications_

Other implications consider the practicality of the recommendation, including resource use, equity, acceptability, feasibility and subgroup considerations. Resource use is related to the uncertainty around the cost-effectiveness of a therapeutic or preventive measure. For example, statin use in the frail elderly and others with multiple co-occurring conditions might not be effective and depending on the societal benchmark for willingness to pay, might not be a good use of resources. Equity, acceptability, feasibility, and subgroup considerations require similar judgments around the practicality of the recommendation.
