#### **QUALIFYING STATEMENTS**

The Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) and the Department of Defense (DOD) guidelines are based on the best information available at the time of publication. The guidelines are designed to provide information and assist decision making. They are not intended to define a standard of care and should not be construed as such, nor should the guidelines be interpreted as prescribing an exclusive course of management.

This clinical practice guideline (CPG) is based on a systematic review of both clinical and epidemiological evidence. Developed by a panel of multidisciplinary experts, it provides a clear explanation of the logical relationships between various care options and health outcomes while rating both the quality of the evidence and the strength of the recommendation.

Variations in practice will inevitably and appropriately occur when providers consider the needs of individual patients, available resources, and limitations unique to an institution or type of practice. Therefore, every health care professional using these guidelines is responsible for evaluating the appropriateness of applying them in each unique clinical situation using a patient-centered approach.

These guidelines are not intended to represent VA or DOD policies. Further, inclusion of recommendations for specific testing, therapeutic interventions, or both, within these guidelines does not guarantee coverage of civilian sector care.

# **I. Introduction**

The Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) and Department of Defense (DOD) Evidence-Based Practice Work Group (EBPWG) was established and first chartered in 2004, with a mission to advise the “…Health Executive Council (HEC) on the use of clinical and epidemiological evidence to improve the health of the population across the Veterans Health Administration (VHA) and Military Health System (MHS),” by facilitating the development of clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) for the VA and DOD populations.(1) The development and updating of VA/DOD CPGs is funded by VA Evidence Based Practice, Office of Quality and Patient Safety. The system wide goal of evidence-based guidelines, this CPG, is to guide providers in hypertension management in addition to offering management pathways supported by evidence. This CPG is intended to provide health care providers with a practical framework by which to evaluate, treat, and manage hypertension, address the individual needs and preferences of patients, and improve clinical outcomes.

In 2020, the VA and DOD published an updated CPG for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Hypertension (HTN) in Primary Care (2020 VA/DOD Hypertension CPG), which was based on evidence reviewed from December 15, 2013, through March 25, 2019. Since then, a growing body of research has expanded the general knowledge and understanding of the diagnosis and treatment of hypertension. Consequently, a recommendation to update the 2020 VA/DOD Hypertension CPG was initiated in July 2025 using published data from March 1, 2019, through November 12, 2025. This updated CPG’s use of the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach reflects a more rigorous application of the methodology than previous iterations.(2) Therefore, the strength of some recommendations might have been modified because of changes in the quality of the supporting evidence (see Evidence Quality and Recommendation Strength).

This CPG provides an evidence-based framework for evaluating and managing adult patients with hypertension who are eligible for care in the VA or DOD systems, including community care. Successful implementation of this CPG will:

- Emphasize the use of patient-centered care using risk factors and event history;

- Minimize preventable complications and morbidity;

- Optimize each patient’s health outcomes and improve quality of life; and

- Assess the patient’s condition and in collaboration with the patient, family, and caregivers, determine optimal treatment.

# **II. Background**

## **A. Epidemiology and Impact on the General Population**

Blood pressure (BP) is a continuous variable that can increase with advancing age. Elevated blood pressure along with elevated cholesterol, tobacco use, and diabetes, is an established risk factor for conditions like coronary artery disease (CAD), heart failure, stroke, peripheral vascular disease, chronic kidney disease (CKD) and cognitive decline. Also, conditions like CKD and/or

diabetes can elevate hypertensive risk. Increases in blood pressure have been shown to have a continuous, graded positive association with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (3), and are positively associated with fatal and non-fatal vascular and nonvascular events.(4) Hypertension, or pathologically elevated blood pressure, is a silent asymptomatic condition, which is considered a modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD). CVD has plagued the United States (US) as one of the most common causes of death since the 1900s.(5,6) In 2023, one in every three deaths was associated with CVD.(7) The prevalence of hypertension varies by age, sex, and ethnicity.(8) Blood pressure progressively increases with advancing age; almost half of the elderly population has hypertension, with rates higher in men (50.8%) than women (44.6%).(9) Hypertension develops at a younger age in individuals who are non-Hispanic Black, and may present with a higher prevalence of complications at the time of diagnosis, including stroke and end-stage kidney disease, when compared with other ethnic groups.(10) Jaeger et al. (2023) noted that between 2017 and 2020, a higher percentage of US adults with hypertension compared to those without were non-Hispanic Black (13.5% vs. 9.3%), while a lower percentage of those with hypertension compared to those without were Hispanic (12.3% vs.18.1%).(11) Martin et al. (2024)(8) found that more than half (50.4%) of US males and 43% of females ≥ 20 years of age have hypertension, mirroring the prevalence of CVD, which remains highest among nonHispanic Black females (59%) and non-Hispanic Black males (58.9%), findings supported by Jaeger et al. (2023).(11)

The definition of hypertension has evolved over the past five decades as clinical trials have progressively lowered the level of blood pressure at which the benefits of treatment outweigh the risks. In 2017, the American College of Cardiology (ACC)/American Heart Association (AHA) Guideline for the Prevention, Detection, Evaluation, and Management of High Blood Pressure in Adults redefined hypertension by lowering the threshold levels of 140/90 millimeters of mercury (mmHg) to 130/80 mmHg.(11) Based on data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), which studied 56,017 participants between 2017 and 2020, it is estimated that 115.3 million Americans have hypertension defined by the 2017 ACC/AHA BP guideline, systolic blood pressure (SBP) of 130 mmHg or higher or a diastolic blood pressure (DBP) of 80 mmHg or higher.(12,13) The asymptomatic nature of hypertension can make detection challenging; therefore, routine screening is paramount. Furthermore, the asymptomatic nature of hypertension can lead to challenges with adherence to treatment. In the US general population, hypertension control rates have decreased from 53.8% controlled (2013-2014) to 43.7% controlled (20172018).(14) It was lowest for Hispanic (36.8%), followed by non-Hispanic Black (38.5%) and nonHispanic Asian individuals (43.4%). Among US Veterans, hypertension control rates varied across sectors by race and ethnicity (range, 44.1%–97.5%); Black Veterans, followed by American Indian or Alaska Native Veterans, had the lowest mean control rates.(15) There was also significant geographical variance among poorly controlled hypertension control rates.(15) Advances in science and technology have helped drive a decline in mortality; however, clinicians still need to evolve their practice and remain engaged with their patients as they continue to help improve modifiable risk factors in their patients. In this CPG, the Work Group specifically investigated the point of proven benefit for hypertension treatment (see Recommendations 4-6); based on this evidence, the Work Group defines hypertension as SBP and/or DBP at or above 130/90 mmHg, assuming proper measurement technique.

## **B. Hypertension in the Department of Veterans Affairs and Department of Defense Populations**

Estimates of HTN prevalence among active-duty military are limited and undoubtedly skewed by policies that limit recruitment of individuals who already have cardiovascular risk factors such as HTN, obesity, and diabetes. Survey data report an overall prevalence of 9.1% in the active-duty population, with higher rates in men and more senior pay grades.(16) Compared to active-duty military who have not deployed, exposure to multiple stressful deployments was associated with a new diagnosis of HTN.(17) In the first study to directly compare cardiovascular health metrics of active-duty Army men and women with those of NHANES participants evaluated between 2011 and 2012, NHANES participants had a higher prevalence of ideal blood pressure defined as <120/80 mmHg (46% versus 27% for men, 63% versus 52% for women) compared to active-duty Army personnel, even though NHANES participants were considerably older than the Army personnel (only 50% of NHANES participants <40 years old).(18)

Applying the AHA and ACC definition of HTN as a systolic blood pressure of ≥130 mmHg or a diastolic blood pressure ≥80 mmHg (11), the prevalence of HTN among Veterans was 48.2% in fiscal years 2016-2019.(19) This prevalence closely aligns with the 48.1% prevalence reported for the US general population, based on NHANES data from 2017 to 2020.(20) Similar to the general population, the prevalence of HTN among Veterans increases with age, ranging from 10.7% among individuals 18-44 years to 62.7% among those aged 65 years and older. Male Veterans had a higher prevalence of HTN (50.1%) compared to female Veterans (27.3%). Among racial and ethnic groups, the prevalence was highest among Black Veterans (54.2%), followed by White (49.5%), Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islanders (47.4%), Hispanic (39.6%), and Asian (36.5%).(21) Blood pressure control rates, defined by a treatment goal of <140/90 mmHg among patients aged 45-64, were 76.6% for female and 75% for male Veterans in fiscal years 20162019. These control rates are higher than those reported in the general population (40.5%).(20) However, among all Veterans, younger Veterans and those with diabetes or residing in rural areas had lower rates of blood pressure control.(19) This CPG on the management of HTN in the primary care setting is intended to promote evidence-based management of HTN and thereby improve patients’ clinical outcomes. It can assist primary care providers in the screening and diagnosis of HTN, determination of appropriate treatment, and delivery of individualized interventions. Although it was developed for a broad range of clinical settings, it should be applied with enough flexibility to accommodate local practice and individual situations.

## **C. Hypertension Impact on Readiness in the Military**

Hypertension poses a direct threat to military readiness, impairing Service members’ health, performance, and overall operational readiness, as well as increasing risk for cardiovascular events. Hypertension is common in the DOD, with survey data from 2018 reporting that 9.1% of the active-duty population carried a hypertension diagnosis.(16) Evidence suggests that hypertension is underdiagnosed and undertreated in the military population, potentially increasing the risk of short-term impact on readiness and long-term adverse cardiovascular consequences.(18,22) Risk factors for developing hypertension, such as obstructive sleep apnea (23), tobacco use (16), and non-regulated supplement use (24) may be more common in the active-duty population compared to the general population in the United States. Furthermore,

common military exposures, such as combat, sleep deprivation, and traumatic experiences resulting in chronic post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) all increase the risk of hypertension.(17,25-27)

The proper diagnosis of hypertension is critical in the active-duty population. Consideration of white coat effect and white coat hypertension are important, as a new diagnosis could adversely impact duty status and unit readiness, while proper measurement of blood pressure may be challenging in operational clinical environments. Improper measurement can lead to both over and underdiagnosis.

The immediate impact on readiness for Service members with hypertension depends on many factors, such as the degree of blood pressure elevation, the Service member’s occupation or special duty, the timeline and location of the deployment, and the availability of medical resources while deployed. The diagnosis of hypertension is not immediately disqualifying for most duties, but certain special communities, such as aviation, have specific requirements for the assessment, management, and control of hypertension. In general, a Service member with significantly elevated blood pressure is unlikely to be fully medically ready or world-wide deployable, regardless of occupation. Mild elevations in blood pressure will restrict Service members in special populations from achieving full medical readiness. One study (28) found a six-fold increase in the prevalence of hypertension in Naval aviators with application of the more stringent hypertension diagnostic criteria from the 2017 ACC/AHA guidelines (29) compared to the Seventh Report of the Joint National Committee on Prevention, Detection, Evaluation, and Treatment of High Blood Pressure (JNC7) Guidelines, with up to 45% of Naval aviators potentially having the diagnosis of hypertension. Blood pressure control is seldom achieved rapidly as titration of therapy over months is often required, which may restrict a Service member from full readiness and deployability for an extended period. Furthermore, some medications may be restricted or limited in special populations (such as beta blockers in aviation). Other first-line medications may be challenging to initiate and titrate in austere environments (such as diuretics in heat environments), when routine laboratory monitoring is not available, or when side effects of therapy could jeopardize the Service member’s health and adversely affect the mission.

Most active-duty Service members can be fully medically ready and deployable world-wide with the diagnosis of hypertension if the diagnosis is made and management is initiated and managed in accordance with this CPG. In addition to the long-term health benefits, early and optimal treatment of hypertension can ensure blood pressure does not impact Service members’ readiness.

# **III. Scope of This Guideline**

This CPG is based on published clinical evidence and related information available between March 1, 2019, and November 12, 2025. It is intended to provide general guidance on best evidence-based practices (see Appendix A for additional information on the evidence review methodology). Although the CPG is intended to improve the quality of care and clinical outcomes (see Introduction), it is not intended to define a standard of care (i.e., mandated or strictly required care).

## **A. Guideline Audience**

This CPG is intended for use by providers who manage, co-manage, or otherwise care for adults with hypertension.

## **B. Guideline Population**

This CPG is intended for adults (18 years or older) with hypertension who are eligible for care in the VA or DOD systems, including community care.

# **VI. Summary of Guideline Development Methodology**

The methodology used in developing this CPG follows the Guideline for Guidelines, an internal document of the VA/DOD EBPWG updated in January 2019 that outlines procedures for developing and submitting VA/DOD CPGs.(30) The Guideline for Guidelines is available at http://www.healthquality.va.gov/policy/index.asp. This CPG also aligns with the National Academy of Medicine’s (NAM) principles of trustworthy CPGs (e.g., explanation of evidence quality and strength, management of potential conflicts of interest [COI], interdisciplinary stakeholder involvement, use of SR and external review).(31) Appendix A provides a detailed description of the CPG development methodology.

## **A. Evidence Quality and Recommendation Strength**

The Work Group used the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach to craft each recommendation and determine its strength. Per the GRADE approach, recommendations must be evidence-based and cannot be made based on expert opinion alone. The GRADE approach uses the following four domains to inform the strength of each recommendation (see Determining Recommendation Strength and Direction)(32):

1. Balance of desirable and undesirable outcomes

2. Confidence in the quality of the evidence

3. Patient or provider values and preferences

4. Other implications, as appropriate (e.g., resource use, equity, acceptability, feasibility, subgroup considerations)

Using these four domains, the Work Group determined the relative strength of each recommendation ( _Strong or Weak_ ). The strength of a recommendation is defined as the extent to

which one can be confident that the desirable effects of an intervention outweigh its undesirable effects and is based on the framework above, which incorporates the four domains.(33) A Strong recommendation generally indicates High or Moderate confidence in the quality of the available evidence, a clear difference in magnitude between the benefits and harms of an intervention, similar patient values and preferences, and understood influence of other implications (e.g., resource use, feasibility). A recommendation’s strength (i.e., Strong versus Weak) does not reference its clinical importance (e.g., a Weak recommendation is evidence-based and still important to clinical care).

In some instances, the systematic evidence review might have found little or no relevant evidence, inconclusive evidence, or conflicting evidence for a particular therapy or intervention. The way this finding is expressed in the CPG might vary. The Work Group might include a statement among its recommendations acknowledging insufficient evidence for or against a commonly practiced intervention, particularly if it lacks supporting clinical evidence and poses potential risks (e.g., high opportunity cost, misallocation of resources). In other cases, the Work Group might choose to remain silent in cases where evidence is lacking for a rarely used intervention or when an intervention, despite the absence of recent evidence, is considered the standard of care and has a favorable balance of benefits and harms.

Using these elements, the Work Group determines the strength and direction of each recommendation and formulates the recommendation with the general corresponding text as shown in Table 2.

**Table 2. Strength and Direction of Recommendations and General Corresponding Text**

|**Recommendation Strength and Direction**|**General Corresponding Text**|
|---|---|
|Strong for|We recommend . . .|
|Weak for|We suggest …|
|Neither for nor against|There is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against . . .|
|Weak against|We suggest against . . .|
|Strong against|We recommend against . . .|

This CPG’s use of GRADE reflects a more rigorous application of the methodology than previous iterations; the determination of the strength of the recommendation is more directly linked to the confidence in the quality of the evidence on outcomes that are critical to clinical decision-making. The confidence in the quality of the evidence is assessed using an objective, systematic approach independent of the clinical topic of interest. Therefore, recommendations on topics for which designing and conducting rigorous studies (e.g., randomized controlled trials [RCTs]), might be inherently more difficult, are typically considered lower quality evidence and, in turn, are usually Weak recommendations. Recommendations on topics for which rigorous studies can be designed and conducted (e.g., RCTs) may more often be Strong recommendations. Per GRADE, if the quality of evidence differs across the relevant critical outcomes, the lowest quality of evidence for any of the critical outcomes determines the overall quality of the evidence for a recommendation.(2,34) This stricter standard provides a consistent approach to determining

recommendation strengths. For additional information on GRADE or CPG methodology, see Appendix A.

## **B. Categorization of Clinical Practice Guideline Recommendations**

Evidence-based CPGs should be current. Except for an original version of a new CPG, staying current typically requires revision of a CPG’s previous versions based on new evidence or as scheduled subject to time-based expirations.(35) For example, the U.S. Preventative Services Task Force (USPSTF) has a process for monitoring the emergence of new evidence that could prompt an update of its recommendations, and it aims to review each topic at least every five years for either an update or reaffirmation.(36)

Recommendation categories are used to track how the previous CPG’s recommendations could be reconciled. These categories and their corresponding definitions are similar to those used by the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE, England).(37,38) Table 3 lists these categories, which are based on whether the evidence supporting a recommendation was systematically reviewed, the degree to which the previous CPG’s recommendation was modified, and whether a previous CPG’s recommendation is relevant in the updated CPG.

Additional information regarding these categories and their definitions can be found in Recommendation Categorization. The 2026 VA/DOD Hypertension CPG recommendation categories can be found in Recommendations. Appendix B outlines the 2026 VA/DOD Hypertension CPG’s recommendation categories.

# **VII. Approach to Care in the Department of Veterans Affairs and the Department of Defense**

## **A. Patient-Centered Care**

VA and DOD encourage providers to be sensitive to demographic, cultural, and other differences that affect patients’ values, needs, and preferences. Patient-centered care is aimed at treating the condition while also optimizing the individual’s overall health and well-being. Regardless of the care setting, all patients should have access to individualized evidence-based care. Patientcentered care can decrease patient anxiety, increase trust in providers, and improve treatment adherence.(40,41) A holistic health approach (https:/www.va.gov/wholehealth/) empowers and equips individuals to meet their personal health and well-being goals. Good communication is essential and should be supported by evidence-based information tailored to each patient’s needs. Guideline recommendations should be applied in a holistic approach to care that is patientcentered, culturally appropriate, and available to people with limited literacy skills and physical, sensory, or learning disabilities. The focus is using an individual’s risk factors and event history to guide the various treatment and management strategies for patients with hypertension.

## **B. Shared Decision-Making**

This CPG encourages providers to practice shared decision making (SDM), a process in which providers, patients, and patient care partners (e.g., family, friends, caregivers) consider clinical evidence of benefits and risks as well as patient values and preferences to make decisions regarding the patient’s treatment.(42) SDM is emphasized in “Crossing the Quality Chasm”, an Institute of Medicine, now NAM, report in 2001 (43) and is a core component of a patientcentered, whole health approach. Providers must be adept at presenting information to their patients regarding individual treatments, expected risks, possible outcomes, and levels and/or settings of care, especially where patient heterogeneity in weighing risks and benefits might exist. The VA and DOD have embraced SDM. Providers are encouraged to use SDM to individualize treatment goals and plans based on patient capabilities, needs, values, and preferences.

## **C. Patients with Co-occurring Conditions**

Co-occurring conditions can modify the degree of risk, impact diagnosis, influence patient and provider treatment priorities and clinical decisions, and affect the overall approach to hypertension treatment and management. Many Veterans, active-duty Service members, and their families have one or more co-occurring conditions. Because hypertension is often accompanied by cooccurring conditions, collaborative management with other care providers is often best. Some cooccurring conditions may require early specialist consultation to determine necessary changes in treatment or establish a common understanding of how care should be coordinated.

### **_a. Measurement Technique_**

**(Weak for | Reviewed, Not changed)**

### **_b. Monitoring_**

2. We suggest using home blood pressure monitoring (HBPM), ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM), or office blood pressure monitoring (OBPM) when necessary to further inform the diagnosis and/or management of hypertension. **(Weak for | Reviewed, Amended)**

#### **_Discussion_**

The diagnosis and management of HTN has been based primarily on measurement of blood pressure in the primary care office setting. Office measurements were used by most observational studies and all randomized controlled trials (RCTs) to define both the risk of various blood pressure levels and the goal blood pressures that lead to reduction in adverse cardiovascular outcomes or mortality. Historically, office blood pressure was measured manually using a mercury sphygmomanometer. However, manual office blood pressure (MOBP) measurements are no longer recommended due to human observer bias, calibration issues, and falsely elevated 'white coat' readings. Instead, recent studies have used fully automated oscillometric sphygmomanometers (automatic office blood pressure [AOBP]), programmed to have a fiveminute wait time followed by two to three BP measurements separated by 30-60 seconds and report averaged results. During the 2020 CPG recommendation development, two SRs by Pappaccogli et al. (2019)(57) and Roerecke et al. (2019)(58) indicated that in-office blood pressure readings mimicking the technique used in the clinical studies were very similar to daytime average ambulatory blood pressure monitors (ABPM). Routine office readings, commonly performed as a single attended measurement (OBPM), were significantly higher than measurements from fully automated and averaged readings. A study by Bacaicoa et al. (2025)(59) again confirmed AOBP (waiting room or clinic room) had better diagnostic accuracy than office blood pressure measurement (OBPM). Other commonly performed methods of BP measurement include home blood pressure measurement (HBPM), kiosk blood pressure measurement, and ABPM to include 12-hour and 24-hour monitors.

The 2025 evidence review assessed the most accurate method for measuring blood pressure, resulting in the inclusion of four SRs and 17 individual studies. Apart from two studies, all compared a measurement method (HBPM, AOBP, MOBP, attended or unattended OBPM, waiting room blood pressure, kiosk blood pressure, and one-hour clinic AOBP) against ABPM as the reference standard. The reference standard in each study varied, with different thresholds for the diagnosis of HTN and different methods and duration for obtaining ABPM. In addition, some studies evaluated methods to establish the diagnosis of HTN, while other studies evaluated methods for monitoring therapy. An SR/meta-analysis (MA) by Karnjanapiboonwong et al.

(2020)(60) found that both AOBP and HBPM demonstrated acceptable diagnostic performance compared to ABPM, with only marginally better diagnostic accuracy than OBPM. Multiple individual studies compared OBPM or HBPM with ABPM and suggested these methods can help confirm HTN and guide treatment decisions.(61-64) Mizuno et al. (2023)(61) specifically found similar diagnostic accuracy for OBPM and HBPM, although both methods had limited ability to identify all individuals with high BP using ABPM. Due to the wide variety of methods utilized, variation in thresholds and the reference standard, as well as different indications for performing the measurement, no single method for measurement was found to be optimal. We suggest using any of the methods reviewed based on patient preference and availability of devices, as well as clinician concern for potential white coat HTN effect or masked HTN. Efforts should be made at the system level to ensure access to AOBP across primary care clinical settings in the VA and DOD.

The benefits of using HBPM, AOBP, and/or ABPM slightly outweigh the risks of these methods. Incorporating HBPM, ABPM, and AOBP can improve the diagnostic accuracy for hypertension and more accurately monitor treatment response compared to single office measurement. Although the risk to patients from blood pressure monitoring devices is very low, some discomfort may occur, particularly with 24-hour ABPM. However, these minor discomforts are justified by the clinical advantages of an accurate diagnosis and treatment.

There is large variability in the availability of blood pressure measurement methods. While most primary care clinics in the VA and DOD utilize oscillometric sphygmomanometers for measurement of office blood pressure, not all these devices have the hardware or software available to perform AOBP. The additional time required for AOBP compared to standard office measurement is another barrier to proper technique in office. Unlike patients in the DOD, patients in the VA have access to HBPM devices provided by the VA. ABPM availability varies across VA and DOD facilities and is currently insufficient to support routine use for the diagnosis and management of HTN for all patients. From the patient’s perspective, all available methods are generally acceptable although there may be individual preferences, with patients preferring the most accurate measurement.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to Recommendation 1.(57-59) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, Not changed_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was very low. The body of evidence had limitations including that most studies compared a single measurement method to ABPM as a reference standard, leading to limited direct comparison between different measurement methods. The benefits of using AOBP include improved diagnostic accuracy to inform treatment decisions and alignment with the method used in the primary studies that demonstrate the benefits of treatment of hypertension. Although there is limited harm, such as patient discomfort, the benefits outweigh the negatives. Patient values and preferences vary; some patients prefer the convenience of HBPM, while others prefer in-clinic visits due to access to equipment. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to Recommendation 2.(59-61,64-66) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, Amended_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality

of the evidence was very low. This body of evidence also had similar limitations, including that most studies compared a single measurement method to ABPM as a reference standard, leading to limited direct comparison between different measurement methods. There was no defined standard for the reference, and results were inconsistent across studies. The benefit of using a combination of HBPM, ABPM, and AOBP ensures an appropriate method is available to all patients, improves diagnostic accuracy by potentially detecting white coat and masked HTN, and helps inform treatment decisions. This is an update from the prior guideline which focused on outof-office measurement methods. Similar to Recommendation 1, although there are some potential limited harms, such as patient discomfort, the benefits outweigh the potential burdens. Patient values and preferences vary; some patients prefer the convenience of HBPM, while others prefer in-clinic visits due to access to equipment. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

#### **_Recommendation_**

3. We suggest offering home blood pressure self-monitoring with co-interventions (e.g., nurse or pharmacist-led educational interventions; or the use of various mobile apps) to improve adherence to therapy.

 - **(Weak for | Reviewed, Amended)**

#### **_Discussion_**

Home blood pressure monitoring (HBPM) should utilize an automated blood-pressure cuff (either purchased by the patient or provided by the health-care system) to measure blood pressure at home so that trends of out-of-office blood pressure can be followed. Our evidence review included five SRs and 11 RCTs, which found greater adherence to therapy by patients who utilized HBPM both in isolation and in conjunction with a co-intervention. Co-interventions, such as nurse- or pharmacist-led education, or the use of various mobile apps, appear to more consistently improve adherence to therapy than HBPM alone. Both the 2014 and 2020 VA/DOD Hypertension CPGs recommend using HBPM for both diagnosis and treatment. Notably, Recommendation 5 within the 2020 update specifically notes that HBPM alone has a limited effect, but when paired with co-interventions, it significantly improves blood pressure control. The 2020 CPG also noted that HBPM, including tailored medication titration and education or lifestyle counseling, was associated with a lower blood pressure (MD [mean difference] in SBP - 6.1 mmHg). The current recommendation focuses only on the treatment of hypertension, specifically adherence to therapy, and achievement of blood pressure goals as the outcome.

The current recommendation is based on very low-quality evidence from an updated literature review.

Evidence for the use of HBPM without co-interventions was mixed. One RCT(67) demonstrated improved overall control of blood pressure in patients who were provided with a cuff to conduct four total BP measurements per week (82% vs. 54% achieving controlled hypertension), while another study demonstrated improved adherence to therapy (pill count) in patients with hypertension and diabetes when HBPM was paired with an education intervention.(68) Two other studies did not demonstrate significantly different adherence to therapy scores when patients were provided a home BP cuff with co-interventions.(69,70)

The impact was more notable when HBPM was paired with a co-intervention, such as nurse or pharmacist-led interventions or the use of HBPM with a mobile health app (mHealth). Medication adherence was measured using multiple objective methods across the studies, including pill counts, electronic medication monitoring, continuous multiple-interval gap metrics, prescription renewal ratio, and subjective questionnaire measurements.

Moderate-quality RCTs in the SR by Kim et al. (2023)(71) demonstrated that HBPM paired with nurse-led interventions produced a moderate impact on medication adherence and medication knowledge in patients with hypertension at risk for metabolic syndrome. Various types of interventions were utilized in these studies, but broadly they included provision of equipment/materials, education/discussion, and/or the use of a guideline/protocol/script, while patients met with providers individually or in group sessions. The interventions lasted a median of 12 weeks and most resulted in better adherence compared to usual care.

Similarly, moderate quality evidence from two RCTs demonstrated that HBPM paired with pharmacist-led face-to-face interventions resulted in better adherence to therapy than usual care, as measured by proportion of days covered or adherence to therapy questionnaires.(72,73) Both interventions utilized clinical pharmacists to educate patients, adjust medications, and encourage behavior changes, followed by frequent clinical evaluations. Conversely, pharmacy database reports given to providers (74) and enrollment of patients in therapeutic drug monitoring programs (75) did not produce greater adherence to therapy versus usual care.

The evidence for HBPM when paired with mHealth apps from two SRs is somewhat harder to assess, owing to significant heterogeneity in the type of applications utilized, as well as some inconsistent reporting of whether the applications required the input of HBPM results or if the applications were more focused on items such as medication reminders or behavior changes. The overall quality of evidence ranged from very low to moderate, and the results indicate the use of multiple kinds of mHealth apps improves adherence to anti-hypertensive therapy.(76,77) It was not possible to draw definite conclusions regarding the superiority of one type of application over another. App-based behavioral self-monitoring interventions had a small but significant effect in reducing SBP, on average, by 1.7 mmHg. A subgroup analysis revealed that behavioral self-monitoring interventions paired with tailored advice led to significantly greater improvements in both SBP and DBP than interventions lacking tailored advice. Additionally, there was evidence that an electronic reminder device alone did not lead to significant improvement in medication adherence to therapy, though this was a small study with few patients.(78)

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(71- 73,76,77) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, Amended_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was very low. The body of evidence had some limitations including heterogeneity in interventions, possibly unrelated clinical outcomes (e.g., improved medication adherence by a clinical adherence scale rather than improved blood pressure control in some studies), and lack of discussion on the cost of certain interventions. Co-interventions provide personalized patient feedback with actionable items versus static data points from HBPM without co-interventions. Benefits demonstrated in the studies include improved adherence by

pill count, proportion of days covered, and overall improved blood pressure. Overall, the benefits of using home blood pressure monitoring when paired with a co-intervention generally outweighed the potential harm from adverse events (AEs), which were small, though the magnitude of the positive impact was limited and varied across studies. Potential harms to the patient are minimal, though there is a cost associated with procurement of the HBPM cuff, and additional costs and challenges with implementation of some co-interventions (e.g., dedicating staff to teaching home blood pressure cuff use, interpreting data, etc.). Verifying the accuracy of a patient’s self-measurement technique can be logistically challenging, and incorrect selfmeasurement values may lead to misguided treatment. The Work Group noted that patient values and preferences are key to advocating for items such as lifestyle education within their hypertension management plan. A patient focus group expressed strong interest in using technology, a finding supported by the evidence report. The report highlighted several nurse-led interventions, including three studies that incorporated smartphone app-based medication reminders.(79) However, the Work Group also identified several limitations related to this recommendation, particularly concerning resource use, feasibility, and equity. Since the DOD does not provide free home blood pressure cuffs, this creates resource inequality and a significant financial barrier. This presents a challenge, as the patient focus group specifically expressed a preference for HBPM to be available. Finally, implementing supporting cointerventions would require resource allocation for training staff and associated time allotment for task completion. <mark>Implementing these co-interventions is feasible within current DOD structures by optimizing established Clinical PharmD and Disease Management nursing positions. This strategic alignment addresses the recommendation without requiring additional personnel authorizations, allowing for a concentrated focus on specialized training and execution time rather than hiring. T</mark> hus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

## **B. Treatment Goals and General Approaches to Hypertension Management**

### **_a. Blood Pressure Goals_**

#### **_Recommendation_**

4. For individuals with hypertension, we recommend treating to a target systolic blood pressure goal of <130 mmHg to reduce adverse cardiovascular outcomes, depending on patient characteristics and preferences.

#### **(Strong for | Reviewed, New-replaced)**

#### **_Discussion_**

Pharmacological treatment of HTN has been shown to decrease the risk of adverse cardiovascular events, including in those with co-occurring diabetes, CKD and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). Two SRs by Haze et al. (2025) and Rizwanullah et al. (2025) including 24 RCTs performed in a relatively broad population with HTN provided moderate strength of evidence that intensive blood pressure lowering to a SBP target of less than 130 mmHg, as compared to a higher target (SBP <140-150 mmHg), reduces composite cardiovascular outcome, which included stroke, non-fatal myocardial infarction and death from

cardiovascular causes.(80,81) An additional SR by Matsumoto et al. (2025) found no significant difference in composite cardiovascular outcome in individuals with HFpEF; however, the effect size was virtually identical to the above SRs.(82) The evidence for intensive BP lowering in individuals with HTN and concurrent diabetes was not reviewed separately; nevertheless, individuals with diabetes were included in several RCTs that were analyzed in the SRs.(83-85) Dedicated SR (86) focusing on specific populations with CKD did not report composite cardiovascular outcomes, but patients with CKD were included in several RCTs generating the evidence for other SRs.(80-82) There was also a moderate strength of evidence that intensive treatment to a SBP goal of <130 mmHg, as compared to a higher target (SBP ≥130 mmHg), reduces the risk of two individual outcomes, such as stroke(81), based on one SR of 12 RCTs, and cardiovascular death, based on two SRs of 24 RCTs.(80,81) Despite the similar effect size and direction of evidence, cardiovascular death risk reduction did not reach statistical significance in subpopulations with CKD or HFpEF.(82,86) There was also moderate evidence from one SR including 12 RCTs that intensive systolic blood pressure control (SBP goal <130 mmHg) reduces risk of heart failure in the general population with HTN (81), and is also associated with a reduction in heart failure hospitalizations among individuals with HFpEF in another SR.(82) Other vital outcomes, such as all-cause mortality and coronary events, were not reduced with more intensive lowering of BP. There were no studies comparing even more intensive BP lowering to a SBP goal <120 mmHg with an SBP goal of <130 mmHg.

It is important to note that targeting HTN treatment to a SBP goal of <130 mmHg as compared with SBP <140 mmHg was associated with increased risks of hypotension, syncope and acute kidney injury, but not with increased risks of falls associated with injury or in progression of CKD, including in subpopulations with CKD.(80-82,86) Although not reviewed as separate evidence for this CPG, an increasing number of contemporary RCTs, including those in systematically reviewed SRs, utilized the automated office BP (observed or unobserved) using a programmable device that takes automated BP measurements after five minutes rest and averages several BP values.(80,81) The previous SR by Roerecke et al. (2019) demonstrated that BP values obtained with automated office BP (as suggested in Recommendation 1), are significantly lower (as much as a 14 mmHg difference) than BP values obtained with routine office BP measurement technique.(58) Additionally, clinical trials targeted average SBP values across multiple follow-up visits rather than BP values from a single visit.

Generally, persons with hypertension and frailty, cognitive impairment, concomitant orthostatic hypotension, and octogenarians/nonagenarians were excluded from clinical trials targeting intensive BP control.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation. (80-82,86) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-replaced_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was moderate. The benefits of targeting a SBP goal of <130 mmHg in the general population with HTN outweigh the potential harms, which include hypotension, syncope and acute kidney injury. Individual values and preferences vary somewhat; however, most agree that avoiding cardiovascular disease, especially stroke, is critical. Shared decision-making between providers and individuals with HTN, with considerations that strike a balance between risk and benefits of intensive BP lowering is important. Targeting a lower SBP goal usually

requires a higher number of antihypertensive drugs, which some people would prefer to avoid, and some are unwilling to take. Additionally, some individuals may not achieve the desired SBP goals without experiencing adverse drug reactions. During the patient focus group, concerns about polypharmacy and potential side effects of more intensive BP lowering were confirmed; however, everyone agreed that avoidance of stroke and cardiovascular disease would shift the balance toward lower SBP goals. Other considerations when treating to a lower SBP goal are the need for more frequent visits for titration and monitoring of side effects of antihypertensive drugs, which may increase resource utilization and contribute to the treatment burden of the individual. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Strong for_ recommendation.

#### **_Recommendation_**

5. For individuals ≥30 years old with hypertension, we suggest treating to a target diastolic blood pressure goal of <90mmHg to reduce adverse cardiovascular outcomes, depending on patient characteristics and preferences. (See Recommendation 6) **(Weak for | Reviewed, Amended)**

#### **_Discussion_**

No new studies have addressed the benefits of lowering DBP to a goal of <90 mmHg to compare with higher targets since the publication of the 2020 VA/DOD HTN CPG. It is unlikely that such studies will be performed in the future because lowering SBP to a target of <130 mmHg was generally accompanied by a reduction in DBP to <90 mmHg. Therefore, the Work Group reviewed the evidence from the 2020 VA/DOD HTN CPG, which included 17 publications from 11 RCTs consisting of 59,304 participants.(87) Seven publications compared two different DBP targets, thus providing direct evidence for benefit or harm of different goals, while five publications compared treatment to a DBP target with an active drug compared to a placebo, and another five additional articles compared a DBP target to the usual care, thus providing indirect evidence for the question of treatment goals. Landmark studies, such as the Veterans Administration Cooperative Study Group on Antihypertensive Agents, the Hypertension Detection and Follow-up Program (HDFP), and the Medical Research Council (MRC) trial of treatment of mild HTN, as well as SR and meta-analysis by Saiz et al. (2018), all demonstrated substantial cardiovascular benefits by treating to a target DBP of <90 mmHg.(85,88-92) Eligibility for these studies differed somewhat, but all included patients 30 years of age or older. This recommendation may or may not apply to individuals aged 18 to 29, as clinical trials did not include patients younger than 30. However, it is unlikely that clinical trials will address HTN treatment and optimal DBP targets in younger adults, given the low prevalence of primary HTN in this group. The Work Group believes that younger adults with elevated DBP may also benefit from treatment.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation in the evidence review from the 2020 VA/DOD HTN CPG.(88) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, Amended_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was low due to serious study limitations, indirect comparisons and imprecision. The benefits of targeting a DBP goal of <90 mmHg in the general population aged 30 and older with HTN outweigh the potential harms. Other considerations regarding this recommendation included the lack of data from patients under 30 years, thus the specified age range. Therefore, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

#### **_Recommendation_**

6. For individuals with hypertension, there is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against treating to a target diastolic blood pressure goal of <80 mmHg, versus a target of <90 mmHg.

#### **(Neither for nor against | Reviewed, New-added)**

#### **_Discussion_**

Several recent guidelines on HTN, including the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association 2025 CPG (11), recommend treating to a target of DBP <80 mmHg. The current CPG reviewed evidence from three SRs that compared DBP targets; two SRs (93,94) focused on general population with HTN and one SR (95) focused on older adults. Evidence from three RCTs in a 2020 SR by Baffour-Awuah et al. (2020) found no difference between groups with DBP <80 mmHg vs. higher targets (<85 or <90 mmHg) for major cardiovascular events during 3.8 to 4.9 years of follow-up. Three individual RCTs from a 2022 SR by Reboldi et al. (2022) demonstrated a 19-26% risk reduction of myocardial infarction (MI) with DBP <80 mmHg, as compared with DBP 80-89 mmHg. However, SRs by Arguedas et al. (2020) and Baffour-Awuah et al. (2020) analyze an overlapping (but not identical) set of RCTs and found no significant between-group difference in risk of MI, although effect sizes were similar in all three SRs.(93-95) Evidence from five RCTs in three SRs (93-95) found no difference in the risk of stroke between groups with DBP <80 mmHg as compared with DBP 80-89 mmHg during 3.8 to 5.3 years of follow-up. The total adverse events were only reported from one RCT in one SR and the risk was not increased; however, that study included a limited number of participants (N=338) and had a relatively short follow-up period (19 months).(75)

Sub-analysis of systolic versus diastolic BP treatment targets in Baffour-Awuah et al. (2020) showed that achieving lower SBP was significantly more effective in reducing the relative risk of cardiovascular events as compared with DBP. Although not reviewed as separate evidence, the mean achieved DBP in RCTs evaluating SBP goals <120 vs. <140 mmHg was below 80 mmHg in all treatment groups, suggesting that lower DBP is achievable in most individuals treated to a target SBP <130 mmHg and it is tolerated without increased untoward effects.(84,96,97)

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(93-95) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was low due to concerns with methodological quality of the included RCTs and wide confidence intervals (CIs) in the reported outcomes. There is inconsistent benefit of lowering DBP <80 mmHg as compared with higher targets, but also no apparent increase in adverse events. Most individuals in whom SBP is targeted to <130 mmHg will also achieve DBP targets of <80 mmHg. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Neither for nor against_ recommendation.

### **_b. General Approaches to Hypertension Management_**

#### **_Recommendation_**

7. We suggest offering pharmacist-led medication management to achieve blood pressure goals.

**(Weak for | Reviewed, New-replaced)**

#### **_Discussion_**

This recommendation is intended for pharmacists in the clinic setting in both the VA and DOD, and not dispensing pharmacists. Pharmacist-led interventions have been effective in improving critical outcomes in hypertensive patients. Interventions evaluated during this evidence review include medication-focused interventions (medication initiation, adjustment, and recommendations to physicians) as well as sessions on adherence, lifestyle modification, blood pressure measurement, support for home blood pressure monitoring, and patient education. One SR by Mills et al. (2024) was identified for review in this CPG update.(51) Pharmacist-led interventions from 29 RCTs including 7,476 patients with hypertension demonstrated net changes in both SBP (-7.3 [95% CI, -9.1 to -5.6] mmHg) and DBP (-3.9 [95% CI, -5.2 to -2.5] mmHg) compared to usual care or minimal education.(51) Pairwise comparisons showed statistically greater reductions in BP with pharmacist-led interventions than with nurse-, physician-, or multiple-leader-led interventions.(51) Pharmacists' ability to titrate medications may partially explain the large net BP reduction observed in the reviewed trials. A smaller but still significant reduction in BP was seen after adjustment for implementation strategy (-6.4 [95% CI, -9.0 to -3.8] mmHg for SBP and -3.8 [95% CI, -6.2 to -1.4] mmHg for DBP), however, suggesting that BP lowering was not solely due to medication management.(51) Pharmacists’ expertise uniquely positions them to provide medication titration as well as effective counseling on other aspects of medication support, such as adherence and side effects, which are often barriers for patients.

Structure, staffing, and resources vary between the Medical Home Model (DOD) and Patient Aligned Care Team Model (VA), as well as by treatment environment. Dedicated pharmacy staff for the interventions reviewed may be limited or unavailable in some facilities or locations, such as deployment settings. Focus group data indicated that patients highly value personalized treatment plans and finding medication(s) that are effective and minimize side effects. Access to a pharmacist at VA and DOD medical facilities for discussion of pharmacotherapy options and/or medication management was uncommon but desired by participants.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(51) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-Replaced_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was low. The body of evidence had some limitations, such as difficulty masking those delivering the intervention or study participants. Some cluster trials did not enroll all study participants before randomization, and many older trials lacked prespecified statistical analysis plans. Additionally, the SR studies had modest sample sizes in some interventiondelivery categories. Significant heterogeneity was observed across all comparisons. The benefits of improved SBP and DBP outweigh the potential harm; it should be noted that Mills et al. (2024) did not report adverse events.(51) Patient values and preferences vary somewhat. Some may prefer interventions less focused on medications, whereas others highly value pharmacists’ expertise in individualizing medication regimens to maximize effectiveness and minimize side effects. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak For_ recommendation. The change from a _Strong for_ in 2020 to a _Weak for_ recommendation does not reflect evidence showing a smaller effect size, rather a stricter application of the GRADE methodology.

#### **_Recommendation_**

8. We suggest offering nurse-led interventions as an option for blood pressure management. **(Weak for | Reviewed, Not changed)**

#### **_Discussion_**

Nurse-led interventions for the management of HTN encompass a wide variety of treatment approaches, including sessions for medication adherence, lifestyle modification, BP measurement, protocol-based medication adjustment, support for HBPM, education materials, personalized therapeutic plans, and goal setting. Mills et al. (2024) included 19 studies (n=9,453) which evaluated nurse-led interventions against usual care or minimal education and found reductions in SBP (-3.0 [95% CI, -4.2 to -1.9] mmHg) and DBP (-1.8 [95% CI, -2.6 to -1.0] mmHg).(51) In general, nurse-led interventions are well received by patients and most providers. Patients often appreciate nurse-led interventions for increased or more timely access to care and shorter wait times related to seeing a primary care provider.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(51) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, Not changed_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was low. The body of evidence had some limitations, including difficulty masking those delivering the intervention or study participants; some cluster trials did not enroll all study participants before randomization, and many older trials lacked prespecified statistical analysis plans. Additionally, the SRs had modest sample sizes in some intervention-delivery categories. Significant heterogeneity was observed across all comparisons. The benefits of improved blood pressure outweigh the potential harms (none identified). Patient values and preferences vary somewhat. Interest and ability to modify lifestyle varies, as does receptiveness to medications; the nurse-led interventions reviewed for this update included components of both lifestyle and medication management. Additionally, some patients may prefer not to have additional healthcare appointments. The patient focus group did not explicitly discuss nurse-led interventions, but participants were enthusiastic about support options for lifestyle changes to improve blood pressure. The feasibility of implementing nurse-led interventions may vary based on local staffing resources. Other potential barriers include challenges with workload reimbursement and time limitations in clinic visits to provide health-coaching. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

#### **_Recommendation_**

9. We suggest offering registered dietitian-led nutrition interventions as an option for blood pressure management.

#### **(Weak for | Not reviewed, Not changed)**

#### **_Discussion_**

New evidence for dietitian-led interventions was not reviewed as part of this CPG update. Evidence from the 2020 VA/DOD HTN CPG update found that registered dietitian-led nutrition interventions, such as counseling on sodium restriction, weight loss, and increasing consumption of fruits and vegetables, resulted in modest but statistically significant lowering of SBP and DBP in treated and untreated hypertensive individuals when compared to usual

care.(50) A systematic review by Reigel et al. (2018), which included 2,050 patients across 13 RCTs, demonstrated that nutrition-based interventions resulted in lower SBP (MD -2.9 [95% CI, -4.0 to -1.7] mmHg) and lower DBP (-1.4 [95% CI, -2.1 to -0.6] mmHg) compared to usual care.(50) However, when studies were grouped based on whether the nutrition intervention was delivered by a registered dietitian versus other practitioners, the reduction in SBP was only significant for nutrition interventions with a registered dietitian and the effect size was larger (- 3.2 [95% CI, -4.1 to -2.3] mmHg). The greatest reductions in SBP were seen for dietitian-led nutrition interventions which included sodium restriction as one of the recommendations (-3.5 [95% CI, -4.5 to -2.5] mmHg). Significant lowering of DBP (-1.4 [95% CI, -2.1 to -0.6] mmHg) was seen for nutrition interventions compared to usual care, with a larger reduction (-1.7 [95% CI, -2.4 to -1.0] mmHg) when the intervention included both a registered dietitian and sodium restriction.

The Work Group did not systematically review new evidence related to this recommendation. Therefore, it is categorized as _Not Reviewed, Not Changed_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was low. The body of evidence had some limitations including a lack of uniformity across trials (i.e., nature and duration of interventions and assessment of outcomes); however, 13 out of 15 studies showed moderate heterogeneity.(50) Riegel et al. ( 2018) also noted a lack of blinding of investigators, a lack of allocation concealment, and concerns about subgroup analysis due to the small number of studies without a registered dietitian. The benefits of modest improvement in SBP and DBP outweighed the potential harm of additional clinical appointments, which may be perceived as burdensome, but no harms were reported from nutritional interventions. The Work Group expects there is variation in both interest and ability to modify diet. Patient preferences vary somewhat, as some patients may be resistant to changing eating patterns or face geographical barriers, such as in food deserts or deployment settings, as well as financial barriers to accessing certain types of healthy foods. Others may have high levels of motivation to improve diet quality to reduce pill burden. Focus group participants expressed a strong desire for individualized care, with many preferring treatment plans that include education on dietary modification and other lifestyle changes, rather than relying solely on medications. Access to a registered dietitian and wait times may also vary at different facilities based on staffing and appointment availability. Working with a registered dietitian can provide individualized guidance on improving nutrition within these constraints, as well as factoring in personal and cultural food preferences. There is also the potential to improve other comorbidities with dietary modification. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

#### **_Recommendation_**

10. We suggest offering one or more of the following digital health technologies, as an adjunct to usual care to improve blood pressure control:

 - App or text-supported remote home blood pressure monitoring system,

 - Patient messaging,

 - Smartphone applications,

 - Web-based platforms for management.

#### **(Weak for | Reviewed, New-replaced)**

#### **_Discussion_**

Evidence reviewed during this CPG update showed that digital telehealth interventions added to usual care protocols resulted in reductions of SBP variability. The strength of the evidence was low in favor of the additional digital health interventions as adjunct to usual care and aiding in the reduction of SBP. Digital health interventions, when integrated with standard care protocols, improved accessibility, engagement, and adherence. Continued monitoring, real-time feedback, and access to educational resources supported a more personalized care experience. Patients and healthcare providers may communicate through individual or group messaging and virtual audio-video platforms. Push notifications are also commonly used for reminders, including appointments and metric updates.

Siopis et al. (2023)(98) conducted an SR and meta-analysis that synthesized data from 29 RCTs consisting of 7,592 participants across 13 regions. The analysis evaluated effectiveness, reach, uptake, and feasibility of digital health interventions in adults with hypertension. Average reductions of -3.6 mmHg (SBP) and -2.5 mmHg (DBP) were observed. Individuals with chronic or complex hypertension saw larger reductions. Smartphone interventions showed the most statistically significant results, whereas short message service (SMS) and websites reached a larger audience and were more accessible.

Kelly et al. (2025)(99) conducted a meta-analysis that synthesized data from 75 RCTs consisting of 106,261 participants. The analysis evaluated effectiveness of digital health interventions in adults with hypertension (n=79,035) and Type 2 diabetes mellitus (n=27,226). Reductions in SBP (average -4.9 mmHg) and DBP (average -2.0 mmHg) were observed along with modest secondary benefits related to Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1C) reduction (average - 0.42%) in pre-diabetic/diabetic individuals.(98,99) Interventions assessed included virtual clinics/consultations, online platforms, telephone applications, and telephone text messages.

Kaihara et al. (2022)(52) conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis that synthesized data from 23 RCTs consisting of 5,743 participants across multiple regions related to home blood pressure telemonitoring (HBPT) systems with automatic data transmission. Average reductions of 6.0 mmHg for SBP and 2.4 mmHg for DBP were observed. Data transmission was achieved via BP data feedback programs, phone lines, Bluetooth, and smartphone applications. Some smartphone applications deployed a multi-disciplinary care model to include access to virtual nurses and pharmacists. Study duration ranged from 4 to 72 weeks, with an average duration of 27 weeks. The average completion rate for the included studies was 89.5%.(52)

Motta-Yanac et al. (2025)(53) conducted an SR and meta-analysis that synthesized data from 36 studies which included 26 RCTs consisting of 33,826 participants. The analysis evaluated effectiveness of digital health interventions (smartphone applications) in adults with hypertension. Average reductions of 1.6 mmHg in SBP and 0.6 mmHg in diastolic blood pressure were observed. Smartphone application interventions showed reductions in SBP (-1.9 [-3.4 to -0.6] mmHg) and were a more effective delivery method than SMS and phones calls, which showed no impact.(53,98)

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(52,53,98,99) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-replaced_ . The

Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was low with the suggestion of modest overall benefit for SBP reduction when digital interventions are used adjunct to usual care. The body of evidence had some limitations, including lack of evaluation standardization, reporting, and masking bias. Some of the studies included in the SR demonstrated reductions in SBP when digital health interventions were added as adjunct to usual care. Statistical impact varied largely due to the variety of digital health interventions evaluated. The benefits of digital health technologies for hypertensive patients outweighed the potential harms, such as device/technology dependency and data inaccuracy due to calibration/user error. Patient values and preferences vary largely due to willingness to learn new technologies and some patients may desire not to use technology. Additional factors supporting a _Weak for_ recommendation include acceptability, feasibility, equity, and subgroup considerations such as marketplace availability and end-user uptake. Availability of digital health interventions depends on what is offered by the treatment facility or nearby civilian institutions. The willingness of patients and providers to use digital health interventions and device-enabled features should be carefully assessed. Asynchronous and synchronous data collection, as well as device validation, should also be considered. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

## **C. Pharmacological Interventions**

#### **_Recommendation_**

11. For individuals with hypertension, we recommend a thiazide-type diuretic, dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker, or either an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor or an angiotensin II receptor blocker, as primary pharmacologic therapy to reduce adverse cardiovascular outcomes.

#### **(Strong for | Reviewed, Amended)**

#### **_Discussion_**

The effects of the various antihypertensive drug classes on reducing adverse cardiovascular outcomes, and not just differences in blood pressure lowering effect, were the focus for the recommendation in 2020 and reaffirmed for the 2026 update. The SR and meta-analysis of 50 RCTs by Thomopoulos et al. (2015) was the key body of evidence reviewed in 2020 to recommend these four main drug classes as initial therapy for HTN.(100) Head-to-head comparisons of different antihypertensive drug classes were made for the critical and important outcomes. The components of the critical outcome from the SR included the composite of fatal or nonfatal stroke and coronary heart disease (CHD) events (coronary death and nonfatal MI), both with and without hospitalizations for heart failure. In patients with HTN, the SR found that angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) were equivalent to angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs); ACEIs were equivalent to thiazide-type diuretics; and calcium channel blockers (CCBs) were equivalent to thiazide-type diuretics for composite cardiovascular outcomes. No significant differences in the critical outcome were found for the comparisons of ACEIs and CCBs or ARBs and CCBs; however, the conclusion about their equivalency could not be determined.(100) The evidence base for CCBs included several types of research: a mix of all CCBs; subclasses of CCBs (e.g., dihydropyridine [DHP] CCBs and non-dihydropyridine [non-DHP]); and long-acting formulations. Without literature reporting a head-to-head comparison, we did not have evidence for a recommendation specific to any particular sub-class of CCB.

Various formulations of CCBs, including long-acting CCBs (LA-CCBS), may have different effectiveness and safety profiles that prescribers should consider.

Updated evidence identified during this review was restricted to comparative effectiveness studies and included two network meta-analyses (NMA), three SRs, and three RCTs. The comparisons varied in classes of drugs vs. individual drugs and the outcomes evaluated. In total, there is insufficient data to inform on a preference of one class of agents vs. another for primary pharmacologic therapy (e.g., ACEIs, ARBs, CCBs, or thiazide-type diuretic).There were an extensive number of comparisons that addressed various cardiovascular outcomes with some mixed results. Several comparisons found no differences in cardiovascular outcomes. Notable findings where differences were observed included that ACEIs, ARBs, and diuretics were superior to CCBs for heart failure (low quality evidence from two SRs)(101,102) and CCBs and diuretics were superior to ACEIs for stroke reduction (moderate quality evidence from two SRs and one NMA).(101-103)

ACEIs and ARBs are not used concomitantly, due to well-recognized safety risks.

Although the evidence did not support a recommendation, providers can consider beta blockers as an alternative to the four main antihypertensive classes for patients with compelling indications, including those with underlying CAD, following MI, and in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction. Patients who are intolerant to the four main antihypertensive drug classes may also consider a beta blocker. The Thomopoulos et al. (2015) SR found beta blockers were equivalent to ARBs for the composite cardiovascular outcomes and reported no significant difference between beta blockers and CCBs or thiazide-type diuretics.(100) There were no critical outcomes reported for beta blockers compared to ACEIs. Some important outcomes showed beta blockers were inferior to the other drug classes, particularly for stroke reduction.(100) The 2026 updated evidence review supports beta-blockers as an alternative agent rather than primary therapy. One SR by Zhu et al. (2021) found that CCBs were superior to beta-blockers for major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), and stroke (both moderate quality evidence), as well as cardiovascular death (low quality evidence). ( 102) Two SRs (101,102) found that thiazides were superior to beta-blockers for cardiovascular events (moderate quality of evidence), and one NMA (55 RCTs)(103) showed that ACEIs were superior to beta-blockers for CV and all-cause mortality (low quality evidence). Beta blockers are not recommended as initial antihypertensive therapy, as the evidence is mixed as to whether there is a clear benefit in reducing cardiovascular events.

The Work Group agreed on a _Strong for_ recommendation due to the robust and extensive body of evidence from RCTs, meta-analyses, and SRs supporting use of four drug classes (e.g., thiazide-type diuretics, calcium channel blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, and angiotensin II receptor blockers).(100-103) Therefore, it is considered a _Reviewed, Amended_ recommendation, to clarify that the evidence base evaluated individual adverse cardiovascular outcomes as well as composite cardiovascular outcomes. The confidence in the quality of evidence was moderate, as the authors of the SR adjusted outcomes so that the blood pressure reductions in each drug class were similar.(100) This led to the conclusion that antihypertensive drugs from the same classes have the same blood pressure lowering effect, which may or may not be true. However, overall, the consensus of the Work Group was that the critical and

important outcome results supported that the four antihypertensive drug classes produced similar effects on reducing adverse cardiovascular events. Thomopoulos et al. (2015)(100) did not evaluate differences in adverse events, and the 2026 evidence review did not identify any relevant differences in adverse events. The Work Group concluded that the benefits of the reduction in adverse cardiovascular outcomes outweighed the harms, including the AEs associated with pharmacologic therapy (e.g., orthostatic hypotension, increases in serum creatinine, hyperkalemia, other metabolic concerns) along with other often-manageable side effects, and the risks associated with foregoing drug treatment. Although the four main drug classes for initial therapy were supported by the evidence, there is still some variation, as individual patients may show differences in personal choices, including tablet burden and frequency of medication administration. Additionally, patient-to-patient variation in tolerability of AEs can be a factor when choosing a particular antihypertensive drug class. This variability was also supported by the patient focus group, during which some of the patients indicated that providers focused on pharmacological therapies, rather than providing information and education on non-pharmacologic treatments. An important implication in terms of resource use is cost. Drug copayments for VA and TRICARE beneficiaries vary depending on factors such as dispensing venue (e.g., military treatment facilities versus mail order) and whether a generic versus branded formulation is dispensed. Cost may be less of an issue for active-duty personnel, who receive their medications at no cost. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Strong for_ recommendation.

#### **_Recommendation_**

12. For individuals with hypertension 65 years and older, we suggest a thiazide-type diuretic to reduce adverse cardiovascular outcomes. **(Weak for | Not reviewed, Amended)**

#### **_Discussion_**

In hypertensive patients over the age of 65, treatment with a thiazide-type diuretic compared to other treatment options has been shown to reduce composite cardiovascular outcomes which included heart failure and stroke.(104) In this same meta-analysis (Thomopoulos et al. [2018]), beta-blockers were less effective than all other therapies. For patients over 65, there were no significant differences in composite outcomes between CCBs, ACEIs, and ARBs. It is important to note that the age of 65 is an arbitrary cut-off that was used in this single meta-analysis, thus resulting in a _Weak for_ recommendation. Individual trials have used various age cut-offs for enrollment. Furthermore, this meta-analysis reported similar outcomes with an age cut-off of 60, compared to 65. Large RCTs are consistent with this recommendation, specifically ALLHAT, which is especially pertinent to VA/DOD providers due to the more than 7,000 Veterans included in the study.(105) Of note, ALLHAT enrolled patients age 55 and over and demonstrated improved CVD outcomes with chlorthalidone compared to amlodipine or lisinopril. Harms of thiazide-type diuretics versus other therapies were considered, but a meta-analysis of adverse events did not demonstrate any significant difference between thiazide-type diuretics and other therapies.(104) In general, the evidence supports the use of thiazide-type diuretics in older patients with HTN over other classes of medication.

While there is strong evidence that thiazide-type diuretics in general reduce composite cardiovascular outcomes, there is significant variability between agents in the thiazide-type diuretic class. The strongest trial evidence for improved CVD outcomes with thiazide-type diuretics involves chlorthalidone and indapamide, from ALLHAT(105) and the Hypertension in the Very Elderly Trial (HYVET)(106), respectively. In Avoiding Cardiovascular Events in Combination Therapy in Patients Living with Systolic Hypertension (ACCOMPLISH), the ACEI/CCB combination was favored over an ACEI/thiazide-type diuretic combination.(55) The dose and type of the thiazide-type diuretic in ACCOMPLISH (hydrochlorothiazide [HCTZ] 12.525 mg/day) was lower than equipotent doses of chlorthalidone or indapamide. For initial or addon treatment of HTN, chlorthalidone and indapamide result in more substantial decreases in blood pressure compared to low doses of HCTZ (12.5-25 mg/day). However, when chlorthalidone and HCTZ are used in equipotent doses (12.5-25 mg of chlorthalidone compared to 25-50mg of HCTZ), there were no differences in blood pressure control and cardiovascular outcomes.(107) Patient preferences may vary based on the requirement for laboratory monitoring with thiazide-type medications.

Although not included in the evidence review, the authors of a recent (Musini et al. [2025]) systematic review of composite cardiovascular outcomes in over 29,000 hypertensive individuals 60 years and older found that there was no new evidence since their previous review in 2019. composite cardiovascular outcomes more than other classes of antihypertensive agents.(108) An additional pragmatic clinical trial published in 2022 found no differences in cardiovascular outcomes among over 13,500 VA participants who were 65 years or older taking hydrochlorothiazide in a dose of at least 25 mg. They were then randomized to continue hydrochlorothiazide vs. a comparable number of participants were randomized to a pharmacologically equivalent dose (12.5 or 25 mg daily) of chlorthalidone.(107) 95% were on the lower doses of HCTZ (25 mg daily) or chlorthalidone (12.5 mg daily).

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation from the 2014 VA/DOD Hypertension CPG.(104,105) Therefore, it is categorized as _Not reviewed, Amended_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was moderate. The benefits outweigh the potential harm due to cardiovascular risk and blood pressure reduction, although there could be side effects from the medications. Patient values and preferences vary somewhat, because some patients may find the potential side effects to be intolerable, such as edema. Other implications include feasibility, acceptability, and subgroup considerations due to VA/DOD formulary considerations. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

#### **_Recommendation_**

13. For individuals on maximally tolerated triple therapy (i.e., a thiazide-type diuretic; calcium channel blockers; and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor or angiotensin II receptor blocker), who have not achieved blood pressure control, we suggest adding spironolactone or amiloride.

**(Weak for | Reviewed, Amended)**

#### **_Discussion_**

The mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (MRA) spironolactone more effectively reduces SBP and DBP compared to placebo and alternative antihypertensive agents (see below) when added as the fourth agent to the treatment regimen of patients with resistant HTN (RHTN). More specifically, in the PATHWAY 2 randomized controlled trial (n=335), spironolactone was significantly more effective than bisoprolol, doxazosin, and placebo in RHTN patients, reducing home SBP by 8.8 mmHg more than placebo, and 4.3 mmHg more than the other two active drugs. Nearly 60% in the spironolactone arm achieved their target BP compared to 40% in the other two active agent arms.(109) These findings are supported by a meta-analysis that included 24 RCTs consisting of 3,458 patients with RHTN, which compared 12 active pharmacologic or interventional treatments for RHTN to one another or placebo/sham.(110) Fourteen of the RCTs reported on pharmacologic interventions. In all pharmacologic comparisons, spironolactone was the only agent that showed a statistically significant reduction in blood pressure over other agents (e.g., guanfacine).

In the recent SPARE randomized controlled trial from Korea (n=118), amiloride (an epithelial sodium channel blocker) was noninferior to spironolactone for lowering both SBP and DBP.(109,111) As such, amiloride may be considered as an effective alternative to spironolactone with the benefit of avoiding adverse effects of gynecomastia and mastodynia. Eplerenone (a more selective MRA) is another alternative with lower risk of side effects, although this was not part of our evidence review since there are no randomized trials in patients with RHTN comparing eplerenone to spironolactone. From a safety perspective, spironolactone, eplerenone, and amiloride all consistently resulted in a modest increase in serum potassium, which requires monitoring, particularly at the start of therapy or after dose titration.

Compared with spironolactone, the addition of other antihypertensive drugs to the three-drug regimen of patients with RHTN were less effective in reducing blood pressure (e.g., betablockers and alpha blockers).(109) There is very limited, older data supporting the use of central sympatholytic drugs (clonidine, guanfacine) and vasodilators (hydralazine, minoxidil); therefore, these should only be used if all prior medication classes cannot be used or tolerated, or provide an inadequate blood pressure response.(112)

In patients with a history of hyperkalemia and diminished renal function, spironolactone, amiloride, and eplerenone should be used cautiously and with frequent monitoring of potassium and renal function (within one week of initiation). It should be used with extreme caution in patients with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) less than 45 ml/min/1.73m2 or a serum potassium of 5 millimole (mmol)/liter or greater. Since spironolactone has both antiandrogenic and endocrine effects, it should also be used cautiously in patients with menstrual irregularities, mastodynia, gynecomastia, or sexual dysfunction. Regardless of baseline risk, patients should be informed of and monitored for potential anti-androgenic side effects (e.g., gynecomastia in 5-10% of cases). Should intolerable side effects arise, alternative non-inferior agents should be offered with lower risks of gynecomastia or sexual dysfunction.

Before adding a fourth drug to the treatment regimen, medication adherence and the feasibility of required laboratory monitoring should be assessed. Single-pill combination medication

(SPCM) may improve adherence. Cost and access concerns should also be considered when prescribing new agents versus generic options. Particularly if medication adherence is a concern, the risks/benefits of non-pharmacologic therapies (e.g., low sodium, diet, exercise, renal denervation) should be discussed with each patient through a shared decision-making process. Spironolactone interferes with tests that assess for hyperaldosteronism, a secondary form of HTN, so initiation should be postponed at least four weeks if such tests are anticipated but not yet completed.

If spironolactone or amiloride or other alternatives are not tolerated or cannot provide an adequate response in blood pressure, there are recent randomized trials supporting the use of newer agents for RHTN. Aprocitentan, an endothelin receptor antagonist, was evaluated in the PRECISION trial (n=730), which demonstrated modest efficacy compared to placebo (placeboadjusted drop [25 mg dose] of 3.7 mmHg office SBP and 5.9 mmHg 24h ABPM at four weeks, with sustained effect at 48 weeks); it has not yet been compared in head-to-head trials with other alternative agents, and hence its overall place in therapy is unclear.(113) Novel aldosterone synthase inhibitors (ASI) have also demonstrated efficacy compared to placebo and may offer an alternative to other fourth-line agents. To date, baxdrostat is the only ASI that has received Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval. (114) Patients should be cautioned about side effects for both agents.(115)

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(110,111,114) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, Amended_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was low. The body of evidence had some limitations including limited sample sizes, mostly indirect comparisons, and a limited number of randomized trials. The benefits of once-daily spironolactone outweigh the potential harm of adverse events when the patient with RHTN is chosen carefully and monitored closely as therapy is initiated. Amiloride is a potassium-sparing diuretic that can be considered as an alternative to spironolactone, although it must also be used cautiously in those at risk for hyperkalemia, and potassium should be monitored. Patient values and preferences vary somewhat, because some patients prefer non-invasive treatments, while others may find the burden of a fourth antihypertensive agent and its potential side effects to be intolerable. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

#### **_Recommendation_**

14. For initiation of pharmacological hypertension treatment, there is insufficient evidence to recommend starting combination therapy over monotherapy with sequential addition of another medication to reduce adverse cardiovascular outcomes.

#### **(Neither for nor against | Reviewed, Amended)**

#### **_Discussion_**

For the treatment of hypertension, there is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against initiating SPCM over initiating monotherapy with the sequential addition of another medication and titrating to effect, particularly in the VA/DOD population using medications that are first-line and FDA-approved. This recommendation differs from other recommendations (i.e., ACC/AHA). The systematic evidence review conducted for this CPG update did not identify any studies that met inclusion criteria; for example, the QUARTET study (116) utilized beta blockers as part of the multi-drug combination, a drug not considered first-line therapy. Neither the MacDonald et al. (2017)(117) nor the Wang et al. (2023)(118) studies compared SPCM directly to monotherapy with sequential addition of medications in the comparator arm. Therefore, these studies could not be included in the evidence review for this VA/DOD guideline, despite SPCM showing benefits for more rapid achievement of blood pressure goals. Providers and patients, after shared discussion, including careful consideration of both potential advantages of lowerdose combination pills (i.e., reduction in the number of pills taken, a simplified regimen, reduction in follow-up visits, increase in adherence and persistence, lower side effect profile, reduction in clinical inertia, reduction in co-payments and possible increase in positive outcomes) and potential disadvantages (i.e., not using medications in the doses found to reduce adverse cardiovascular events in randomized trials) and shared decision making, may decide to consider single pill combination therapy as initial HTN therapy.

After systematically reviewing evidence related to the recommendation, the Work Group’s recommendation was characterized as _Reviewed, Amended_ .(116-118) The body of evidence had limitations including the lack of studies with proper comparators, study design (i.e., no comparisons of initiating combination therapy versus initiating monotherapy with sequential addition of additional drugs), use of non-VA/DOD populations and use of non-FDA approved medications. The confidence in the quality of evidence was very low. Potential benefits (improved adherence, possible morbidity and mortality benefit) were largely balanced with potential harm (general applicability, mildly hypertensive patients requiring therapy, potential for longer duration of non-optimally treated hypertension with stepped care). With respect to patient values and preferences, while studies show that in general, patients will adhere more to single pill therapy than multiple pill therapy, this selection should be tailored to specific patient characteristics such as severity of HTN, feasibility of follow-up frequency, and fear of possible side effects. Some patients may prefer reducing the number of pills, but multi-drug fixed dose single pill combinations may not be available locally. Because this is a _Reviewed, Amended_ recommendation, the Work Group systematically searched for evidence related to the recommendation in the evidence review conducted as part of this guideline update; however, no evidence met inclusion criteria. Therefore, this recommendation is based on evidence cited in the 2014 VA/DOD HTN CPG and is _Neither for nor against_ .

## **D. Lifestyle Interventions**

### **_a. Weight Reduction_**

#### **_Recommendation_**

15. For individuals with hypertension and co-occurring overweight/obesity, we suggest weight loss to lower blood pressure. (See VA/DOD CPG for Management of Overweight/Obesity) **(Weak for | Reviewed, Amended)**

#### **_Discussion_**

Yang et al. (2023) in a SR and MA assessed the effect of weight loss on SBP and DBP in patients who have overweight/obesity.(49) The SR and MA included studies in which patients who had overweight/obesity were treated with surgery, medications, or lifestyle interventions (behavior change, nutrition, and physical activity to promote weight loss). They found that SBP and DBP significantly decreased by 5.9 mmHg (95% CI, 3.5-8.0) and 3.4 mmHg (95% CI, 1.9-4.8), respectively, when patients experienced a mean reduction in body mass index (BMI) of 2.3 kg/m2 . When BMI was decreased by 4.1 kg/m2 , SBP and DBP further decreased by 6.8 mmHg (95% CI, 5.2-8.1) and 3.7 mmHg (95% CI, 2.0-5.2), respectively. Overall, they found that reductions in BP in patients who had overweight/obesity were more pronounced when their BMI decreased by three or more kg/m2 . Two other SRs by Basile et al. (2025)(119) and Sibenhofer et al. (2021)(120) also demonstrated that when patients who have overweight/obesity lose weight with medications, SBP and DBP decrease significantly.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(49,119,120) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, Amended_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was very low. The body of evidence had significant limitations including high heterogeneity, selection bias, and failure to separate medical interventions (weight loss medication vs. surgery).(49) The benefits of weight loss outweighed the potential harms, as weight loss results in reduction of other morbidities and mortality. Patient values and preferences varied largely because some patients may prefer less invasive methods to lose weight, while others are unable to successfully lose weight through behavior change, nutrition, and physical activity. Some patients also may not qualify for certain medications or surgeries. The Work Group considered other factors such as equity, feasibility, acceptability, and subgroup considerations when forming this recommendation. While behavior change, nutrition, and physical activity are not limited by insurance coverage, some patients may live in areas where access to healthy foods and outdoor space is limited. Medical deserts also pose barriers for some patients to access specialists such as surgeons and therefore may necessitate utilizing other options such as medications for weight loss. Furthermore, for military readiness, Service members may not be eligible for certain medications or procedures. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

#### **_Recommendation_**

16. In individuals with hypertension and co-occurring overweight/obesity, we recommend Glucagon-like Peptide 1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist-based medications for weight loss and lowering blood pressure. (See VA/DOD CPG for Management of Overweight/Obesity) **(Strong for | Reviewed, New-replaced)**

#### **_Discussion_**

Basile et al. (2025) in an SR and meta-analysis reported that patients who had overweight or obesity experienced higher weight loss and associated reduction in SBP and DBP when given Glucagon-like Peptide 1 receptor agonist (GLP-1 RA)-related medications compared to patients given placebo treatment.(119) The study included 82 RCTs (n=80,940) that assessed changes in SBP in addition to weight loss and 75 RCTs (n=72,781) that assessed changes in DBP. Patients who received GLP-1 RA-related medications experienced reduction in SBP by 3.4 mmHg and DBP by 0.9 mmHg. A greater reduction in weight was significantly associated with a greater reduction in BP. Subgroup analysis was also performed to compare single agonist GLP-1 RArelated medications to dual agonists and triple agonists. The authors found that the most significant reduction in BP was associated with dual agonists (SBP -5.1 mmHg; DBP -1.8 mmHg) and triple agonists (SBP -6.6 mmHg; DBP -2.1 mmHg). The strength of the evidence was rated as moderate.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(119) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New-replaced_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was moderate. The body of evidence had some limitations including very high heterogeneity due to studies with smaller sample sizes being included in the analyses. The included studies also lacked long-term follow-up (median follow-up was eight months). The lowest body mass index for inclusion into GLP-1 RA studies was 27. These drugs were investigated primarily for diabetes control and/or weight loss, and blood pressure reduction was an added benefit in secondary analyses. The benefits of utilizing GLP-1 RA-related medications for weight loss and BP reduction outweigh the potential harms. The benefits seen include improved sleep quality in those with sleep apnea, control of diabetes, and overall positive cardiovascular effects. The harms include possible extreme weight loss and disordered eating in some patients resulting in muscle wasting, particularly when protein intake is low or decreases, and when patients are unable to maintain adequate resistance and strength training. There are also known gastrointestinal (GI) side effects such as nausea, diarrhea, constipation, and poor appetite. Patient values and preferences varied somewhat because some patients may prefer less invasive methods to achieve weight loss, while others have attempted lifestyle modifications with no success. In recommending GLP-1 RA-related medications as a means to lose weight with resultant blood pressure decrease, the Work Group considered patient acceptability, equity, subgroup considerations, and feasibility. GLP-1 RA medications are very popular among patients who have overweight or obesity and wish to lose weight efficiently. The effectiveness of these medications in helping patients lose weight has been widely publicized. However, insurance coverage for these medications, which are not available in generic versions, require preauthorization in the VA and DOD. Pre-authorization for insurance coverage of GLP-1 RAs often requires a documented trial of lifestyle modifications and trial of other weight-loss medications. Some patients end up paying out of pocket for access to these medications as a result. GLP-1 RA medications are also not covered under plans like TRICARE for Life, which are utilized by military retirees and their spouses. As for subgroup considerations, these medications are not recommended for pregnant persons and may impact military readiness if not appropriately titrated. In older patients and those with physical limitations, loss of muscle mass may increase morbidity and mortality by leading to osteoporosis, falls, and subsequent bone fractures. Serious

consideration should be given to consulting nutrition and physical therapy and to encourage physical activity when prescribing GLP-1 RAs in elderly patients. Furthermore, injectable GLP-1 RA medications require refrigeration, which may not be available to Service members who frequently train in the field or who are on deployment. For Service members who need to deploy, a waiver is also required. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Strong for_ recommendation.

#### **_Recommendation_**

17. In select individuals with hypertension and co-occurring obesity, we suggest referral for evaluation for bariatric surgery for weight loss and lowering blood pressure, depending on patient characteristics and preferences. (See VA/DOD CPG for Management of Overweight/Obesity)

**(Weak for | Reviewed, Amended)**

#### **_Discussion_**

Wang et al. (2021) conducted a systematic review of 19 RCTs and one SR on the effects of weight loss through surgery on blood pressure reduction in patients who had hypertension and cooccurring obesity.(121) The SR demonstrated that patients with mean BMI ranging from 29-56 who underwent bariatric surgery experienced a reduction in SBP by 3.9 mmHg (weighted mean difference[WMD]), DBP by 2.8 mmHg (WMD), and antihypertensive medication use when compared to patients with obesity who received non-surgical interventions (lifestyle modification and/or medications) to achieve weight loss. Surgical interventions assessed included laparoscopic adjustable gastric banding, Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, sleeve gastrectomy, biliopancreatic diversion with duodenal switch, vertical banded gastroplasty, and duodenal-jejunal bypass liner. Patients with obesity who underwent Roux-en-Y gastric bypass experienced the most significant reduction in BP in addition to successful weight loss. Non-surgical treatments for obesity, which were used as comparators, included dieting, weight reducing medications, and behavioral therapy. The evidence base included GLP-1 RAs available within the inclusion dates March 1, 2019, to November 12, 2025, such as exenatide, were compared against surgical intervention in two studies, metformin in one study, and orlistat or sibutramine in three studies. The strength of the evidence from this SR was moderate in favor of surgical intervention in achieving weight loss to lower BP.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(121) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, Amended_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was moderate. The body of evidence had some limitations including reporting and attrition bias. The trials were designed as a means for weight loss and reduction in blood pressure was an added benefit in secondary analyses. Some of the studies included in the systematic review did not demonstrate a change in blood pressure despite weight loss, and others had high rates of attrition and loss to follow-up. The benefits of bariatric surgery for weight loss to lower blood pressure slightly outweighed the potential harms, such as post-operative complications and long-term effects of surgery to include malnutrition. Patient values and preferences varied largely because some patients prefer non-invasive treatments while others prefer not to take medications long-term. Other implications that support a _Weak for_ recommendation include equity, acceptability, feasibility, and subgroup considerations. Surgery may not be available at all VA/DOD sites with availability dependent on whether bariatric surgery is offered by the treatment

facility or at nearby civilian institutions. Some military hospitals are in rural areas where surgeon capacity is limited. Additionally, patients who do not meet criteria for obesity are not eligible for bariatric surgery as a treatment for weight loss, and Service members are not eligible for bariatric surgery with regard to medical retention standards. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

#### **_Recommendation_**

18. In individuals with hypertension and co-occurring overweight/obesity, there is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against orlistat or phentermine/topiramate for weight loss and lowering blood pressure. (See VA/DOD CPG for Management of Overweight/Obesity) **(Neither for nor against | Reviewed, New-replaced)**

#### **_Discussion_**

The evidence for this recommendation came from a Cochrane Review by Siebenhofer et al. (2021)(120), which assessed for weight loss and BP reduction in patients using orlistat and phentermine/topiramate. The authors found that patients taking orlistat or phentermine/topiramate for weight loss also experienced reductions in SBP and DBP compared to those treated with placebo. One SR included in the study assessed findings from four RCTs on orlistat. The median reduction in SBP and DBP were statistically significant, but very modest at 2.7 mmHg and 2.0 mmHg, respectively. However, based on the 2025 VA/DOD Clinical Practice Guideline for The Management of Adult Overweight and Obesity, orlistat treatment did not lead to a clinically significant difference in weight loss compared to lifestyle interventions, leading to a _Neither for nor against_ recommendation.(122) As for phentermine/topiramate, one RCT from one SR demonstrated median reductions in SBP and DBP of 2.0 mmHg and 1.3 mmHg, respectively, for low-dose treatment, and 4.2 mmHg and 2.0 mmHg, respectively, for high-dose treatment. While the 2025 Clinical Practice Guideline for the Management of Adult Overweight and Obesity made a treatment for weight loss, the evidence was rated as low.

The Work Group also reviewed evidence regarding treatment of overweight/obesity with naltrexone/bupropion. While patients in the study experienced a reduction in weight, they did not experience any reduction in BP.(120) Thus, the Work Group did not develop any recommendations for use of this medication combination in this CPG.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(120) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, Amended_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was low. The body of evidence had some limitations including bias, imprecision, smaller sample sizes, inconsistent blinding across studies, and lack of long-term outcomes due to attrition bias. The benefits of utilizing these two medications for weight loss and lowering BP are balanced with the potential harms. Another benefit seen includes overall positive cardiovascular effects. The harms include GI side effects that may result in frequent loose stools and loss of electrolytes for orlistat. For the DOD, such side effects are undesirable for Service members training in the field or on deployment and may interfere with maintaining physical fitness. Phentermine/topiramate is associated with emergent cardiovascular events, in a dose-response manner with higher doses increasing risk compared to placebo.(120,123) Phentermine/topiramate also carries twice the risk of suicide compared to placebo.(124) Patient values and preferences

varied somewhat, because some patients may prefer less invasive methods to achieve weight loss while others have attempted lifestyle modifications with no success. Patients may be willing to trial phentermine/topiramate as a steppingstone to obtaining insurance authorization and coverage for new medications (GLP-1 RAs). Orlistat and phentermine/topiramate are used to treat overweight/obesity and the evidence was deemed low from the VA/DOD Obesity/Overweight Clinical Practice Guideline. Reductions in blood pressure would be an indirect outcome of effective weight loss. The Work Group considered the following implications: patient acceptability, equity, and subgroup considerations. Medications such as orlistat and phentermine/topiramate have not been as widely accepted and preferred by patients due to their more extensive and undesirable side effect profile compared to effectiveness in promoting weight loss when equated to newer weight loss medications such as GLP-1 RAs. They are also not recommended for patients with certain kidney, heart, and liver conditions. Additionally, the combination of phentermine and topiramate is not recommended for patients at risk of suicide or for pregnant persons. There is also a requirement for women of childbearing age who take phentermine/topiramate to undergo frequent pregnancy testing. These medications are more accessible, as they are typically cheaper and covered by insurance policies for both the VA and DOD, and do not require insurance pre-authorization. However, pharmacies that want to dispense phentermine/topiramate (Qsymia®) must be enrolled in Qsymia Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy (REMS) program to be authorized to dispense the medication. Not all military treatment facilities are enrolled in this program, which means patients will have a copay if they obtain the medication through civilian pharmacies or the Tricare Mail-order Pharmacy. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Neither for nor against_ recommendation.

### **_b. Dietary Modifications_**

#### **_Recommendation_**

19. We recommend a combination of a healthy low-sodium diet with less than 2,300 mg/day of sodium and physical activity greater than 120 minutes/week to improve blood pressure control.

#### **(Strong for | Reviewed, New-added)**

#### **_Discussion_**

The evidence in support of this recommendation explored combinations of lifestyle interventions to improve blood pressure. The strongest evidence supported a combination of a low sodium diet with sodium less than 2,300 mg/day and physical activity ranging from low intensity (such as walking and yoga) to moderate/high intensity aerobic exercise (such as biking and group exercise programs) to improve blood pressure. Evidence also supported the addition of resistance training in combination with diet and aerobic exercise as an all-inclusive approach to a healthy lifestyle to improve blood pressure.

The current evidence aligned with the previous 2020 VA/DOD HTN CPG, noting that aerobic and resistance exercise interventions, both individually and in combination, have been shown to reduce blood pressure. The evidence reviewed included one SR and nine RCTs. All study participants had HTN. The authors used a variety of lifestyle interventions (LSI) including diet, physical activity (PA), Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), and mindfulness. The use of the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet and physical activity was the most

common combination. All but one of the research studies including the combination of a low sodium diet and physical activity showed reductions in blood pressure. The one study that did not show a reduction in blood pressure also had participants with the least amount of physical activity. Combinations of a low sodium diet and CBT did show reductions in blood pressure. The combination of a low sodium diet and mindfulness did not show significant changes in blood pressure, the strength of evidence for this was moderate.

Krishnamoorthy et al. (2023)(47) conducted a SR of 14 RCTs on the effects of healthy diet (HD) and physical activity (PA) vs. usual care that showed a reduction in both SBP and DBP. RCTs by Unda Villafuerte et al. (2024)(125), Blumenthal et al. (2021)(126), Hinderliter et al. (2021)(127), and Sayon-Orea et al. (2019)(128) all noted an improvement in SBP compared to the control group. Unda Villafuerte et al. (2024)(125), Blumenthal et al. (2021)(126), and Hinderliter et al. 2021(126) were all able to demonstrate a significant improvement in DBP for subjects receiving multicomponent lifestyle interventions vs. usual care.

PA in the current research reviewed varied in amount, type, and intensity. The 2020 VA/DOD HTN CPG concluded that “aerobic and resistance exercise interventions, both individually and in combination, have been shown to reduce blood pressure.”(129) We recommend including resistance training into PA recommendations along with aerobic exercise to improve blood pressure control. An umbrella review by Pescatello et al. (2021)(48) demonstrated that participating in PA reduced blood pressure among adults with HTN as well as those with normal BP, and those that were diagnosed with pre-hypertension. The umbrella review comprised 15 meta-analyses that assessed PA in adults with HTN, six of which found the greatest reduction in BP occurred in adults with diagnosed hypertension. PA included low- to vigorous-intensity activities. Five MAs assessed aerobic exercise, three MAs assessed resistance training, and one MA assessed combined aerobic and resistance training and its effects on BP. PA was shown to reduce systolic BP by 5 to 17 mmHg (in 13 studies) and diastolic BP by 2 to 10 mmHg (in 14 studies) compared to no PA. By category: aerobic exercise reduced SBP by 8.3 mmHg and 5.2 mmHg on average (5 MAs); resistance training reduced SBP by 5.3 mmHg and 5.2 mmHg on average (3 MAs); aerobic and resistance training together reduced SBP by 5.3 mmHg and DBP by 5.6 mmHg (1 MA). The strength of the evidence was rated as very low for PA alone. HTN is the leading modifiable risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) morbidity in the U.S., and PA and HD provide improved mood, mental health, sleep and overall well-being. PA >120 min/week at some intensity level, either low impact, moderate, high or a combination of intensities with resistance training, was achievable regardless of conditioning level. PA would also increase military readiness in our Service members.

DASH diet with group CBT (126) led to significant improvement in SBP compared to usual care control, mean change for intervention at four months: 16.1 (95% CI, -13.0 to 19.2), mean change for control: -3.4; (95% CI, -0.4 to -6.4); p<0.001. Throughout the evidence, the DASH diet was most effective for reducing sodium intake. It is recommended that the provider and patients work through shared decision making to include diet and PA recommendations, and to include the optional referral to a dietitian to support diet education. DASH or DASH + CBT led to greater reduction in 10-year risk of CVD events and lifetime risk of CV death compared to usual care but did not differ from one another. One SR (47) reported significant SBP improvement compared to usual care control in an MA with 6 RCTs: MD: -9.9 (95% CI, -13.3 to -6.4); p<0.05. RCTs by Unda

Villafuerte et al. (2024)(p<0.001)(125), Blumenthal et al. (2021)(p=0.005)(126), and Sayon-Orea et al. (2019)(p<0.001)(128) all reported significant decreases in SBP for diet + exercise compared to control.

Several organizations recommend or suggest targets for daily sodium intake. The AHA recommends no more than 2,300 mg of sodium a day. Both the AHA and the ACC recommend the ideal limit is no more than 1,500 mg/day for most adults, especially for those with high blood pressure. However, sodium intake lower than 2,300 mg/day may be harmful for patients with heart failure.(130) Notably, sodium restriction can result in poor dietary quality with inadequate macronutrient and micronutrient intake which has been associated with clinical instability. The ACC does not endorse a specific sodium threshold for heart failure patients and instead uses the language of “avoiding excessive sodium intake” rather than prescribing a strict numerical target. The average daily sodium intake in the U.S. is over 3,400 mg/day excluding table salt, well above this guideline’s recommendation of 2,300 mg/day.(131) Disagreement regarding the exact cutpoint at which risks outweigh benefits should not distract from a unified public health message that a reduction in sodium intake from current levels is desirable. Benefits of sodium reduction and PA included improvements in CVD biomarkers, mood, insulin sensitivity, and other comorbidities. A combination of a healthy lifestyle and group physical activities also reduced loneliness in patients who participated in group physical activities and is a key proponent in supporting activity duty readiness.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(47,125) Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, New added_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was moderate. The body of evidence had some limitations including randomization inconsistencies, allocation concealment and blinding of patients, standardization of study personnel and assessors, lack of intention-to-treat analysis and high attrition. There were also limitations with broad sampling that included patients with normal BP, pre-hypertension, and hypertension.(48) The studies included in this umbrella review also did not uniformly stratify data by age, sex, socioeconomic status, intensity/duration/frequency of exercise nor body mass index, all of which could be effect modifiers. The previous related recommendations in 2014 and 2020 noted the benefits of improved SBP, DBP and comorbidities from a low-sodium diet with PA, as well as benefits to readiness of active-duty personnel, which outweigh the potential harm. A risk of participating in PA is musculoskeletal injury if patients are deconditioned and begin higher intensity activities too quickly. Additionally, risk of sodium reduction is potentially harmful to cardiovascular outcomes if intake is reduced to below recommended levels. However, some patients may dislike a low-sodium diet and/or dislike or have limited ability to exercise, so the Work Group determined that there is some variation in patient values and preferences with regards to participating in PA. Participating in PA requires time commitment, which may not be feasible for patients with multiple jobs or family obligations. Patients with disabilities and certain medical conditions may also be limited in their ability to participate in PA. Findings from the VA/DOD patient focus group concluded that participants desire alternatives to medications. They value an individualized approach to the treatment of their hypertension and would prefer to focus on LSIs including dietary changes. Other implications that were assessed included equity, feasibility, and subgroup considerations. There are a wide variety of resources that can assist patients in getting started with increasing PA and diet changes such as books, internet classes,

free community classes, mobile applications, and handouts from medical professionals can also be accessed with little to no cost. Furthermore, PA is a core requirement for fitness for duty and military readiness for Service members. While there may be some barriers to exercising outside (e.g., safety concerns or lack of parks nearby), there are many opportunities to engage in indoor exercise with little to no equipment depending on the patient’s resources and preferences. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Strong for_ recommendation.

## **E. Procedural Interventions**

#### **_Recommendation_**

20. In individuals with hypertension and co-occurring renal artery stenosis, we recommend pharmacotherapy over renal artery angioplasty or stenting. **(Strong for | Reviewed, New-added)**

#### **_Discussion_**

A meta-analysis that included eight randomized (or quasi-randomized) controlled trials consisting of 2,444 participants was reviewed. This study included individuals greater than 18 years old, with hypertension (diastolic BP >95 mmHg and significant atherosclerotic renal artery stenosis [unilateral or bilateral reduction in luminal diameter of >50%]). Patients with fibromuscular dysplasia, total occlusion of the renal artery, an affected kidney size of less than eight centimeters, malignant hypertension, or a serum creatinine >500 mol/L (>5.66 mg/dL) were excluded. The trials compared either balloon angioplasty (with or without stent insertion) to medical therapy. Overall results demonstrated that intervention with angioplasty/stent was not associated with an improvement in SBP (-1.1, 95% CI, 3.4 – 1.2 mmHg); cardiovascular outcomes (OR [odds ratio] 0.9 [95% CI, 0.7 – 1.1]), renal adverse outcomes (OR 0.9 [95% CI, 0.7 – 1.3]), or serum creatinine at 2 years (MD -6.5 µmol/L [95% CI, -19.5 to 6.5 µmol/L)(MD -0.07 mg/dL [95% CI, -0.2 to 0.07 mg/dL]). There was a reduction in DBP at 2 years or end of follow-up (n=869) of -1.8 mmHg, (95% CI, -3.5 to -0.2) and a small reduction in antihypertensive medications (n=1,777) -0.2 (95% CI, - 0.3 to -0.04).(132) Medical therapy should be similar to that used in the two pivotal randomized controlled trials. For the CORAL study, medical therapy included an angiotensin II receptor blocker with or without hydrochlorothiazide and the combination of amlodipine-atorvastatin with a target BP of less than 140/90 mmHg in patients without coexisting conditions and less than 130/80 mmHg in those with diabetes or CKD.(133) In the ASTRAL trial, therapy consisted of statins, antiplatelet agents and optimal blood-pressure control.(134)

Adverse events were inconsistently defined and reported across studies. Periprocedural deaths occurred in 4 of 839 individuals (0.5%) across three trials.(133-135) Other uncommon adverse events included pseudoaneurysms, distal embolization, groin hematoma, bleeding requiring transfusion, acute kidney injury and renal artery occlusion with a combined event rate of less than 3.2% across four RCTs.(133-136)

The CORAL and ASTRAL trials found no difference in outcomes by severity of CKD at baseline or severity of renal artery stenosis.(133,134) The CORAL trial found no interaction between treatment group and the following characteristics: sex, Black race, or diabetes.(133)

Based on the relevant studies reviewed in the current CPG evidence base, in patients with hypertension and renal artery stenosis, we recommend medical therapy over angioplasty or

stenting. The Work Group reviewed related evidence; therefore, this recommendation is _Reviewed, New-added_ . The body of evidence had some limitations including small sample size, lack of power for clinically significant outcomes, and limited inclusion criteria.(132) The evidence review did not include individuals with fibromuscular dysplasia, flash pulmonary edema or acute decompensation (rapidly worsening kidney disease), single functioning kidney, or severe/difficultto-control hypertension. The strength of evidence was rated as low or very low depending on the outcome. The benefits of medical therapy over angioplasty/stenting for cardiovascular, renal outcomes and SBP were similar, and were balanced with the potential harm of periprocedural death and other complications which were small. Patient values and preferences were similar because while most patients would prefer medical therapy, some may prefer the other intervention. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Strong for_ medical therapy recommendation. Patients with renal artery stenosis should undergo a trial of medical therapy over intervention/stenting given the similar clinical outcomes and small but known risks of the intervention.

#### **_Recommendation_**

21. In select individuals with resistant or uncontrolled hypertension, who have an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) above 40 mL/min/1.73m2 , we suggest renal artery denervation to lower blood pressure, depending on patient characteristics and preferences.

#### **(Weak for | Reviewed, New-added)**

#### **_Discussion_**

A meta-analysis(132) consisting of 13 randomized controlled trials involving 2,478 participants was identified as part of the evidence review. This meta-analysis compared the effect of renal nerve denervation (RDN) versus sham therapy on blood pressure. The meta-analysis identified three methods for nerve ablation (radiofrequency, ultrasound, and alcohol-mediated), all of which were included. The median duration to the primary endpoint assessment was three months (range 2-6 months). Overall RDN compared to sham procedure reduced both 24-hour ambulatory SBP and DBP by 4.4 mmHg (95% CI, 2.7- 6.1) and 2.6 mmHg (95% CI, 1.5 - 3.6), respectively. Office SBP and DBP was also reduced with intervention compared to sham (SBP -6.6; 95% CI, -9.7 – 3.7); (DBP -3.5; 95% CI, -5.4 – -1.6).

The meta-analysis attempted to evaluate the long-term BP associated with RDN compared to sham. In the primary studies evaluating long term outcomes (i.e. greater than six months), the patients were unblinded, medication changes were permitted, and crossover from the sham control to the intervention group was permitted. Duration of the follow-up ranged from 6 months to 36 months. RDN reduced both 24 hour and office SBP, -5.4 mmHg (95% CI, -7.8 to -3.1) and -6.9 mmHg (95% CI, -11.2 to -2.7), respectively. DBP was not reported.

There was no difference between trials that included those with or without concomitant antihypertensive medications (–4.8 mmHg; 95% CI, –7.7 to –2.0; P=0.001; I2 =79% versus –4.0 mmHg; 95% CI, –5.7 to –2.3; P<0.00001; I2 =32%, respectively; P for interaction, 0.62).

There was also no interaction between the method (radiofrequency, ultrasound or alcoholmediated) of RDN used and any measure of blood pressure, but this was likely an underpowered comparison.

There were no safety differences between RDN and sham. Adverse events assessed included allcause death, embolic event, stroke, vascular complication, renal artery stenosis, hypertensive crisis and hospitalization for heart failure.

There are several limitations, including little information on BP control after six months and no information on clinical outcomes (e.g., myocardial infarction, stroke, etc.). The studies were conducted in large referral centers with a high volume of procedures. It is unclear if this experience will be generalizable to smaller facilities or those with limited procedure experience. Most trials excluded those with an eGFR <40 – 45 mL/min/1.73m2 and those age >75-80.

Another meta-analysis evaluated RDN(137) for the treatment of resistant hypertension. This study included 24 randomized controlled trials that compared RDN with either sham procedure or standard-of-care treatment. They only included studies that enrolled patients with resistant hypertension (the paper doesn’t define how they operationally defined resistant hypertension). Treatment duration varied from eight weeks to 48 months.

The only outcome assessed was blood pressure and serum creatinine. Overall SBP was reduced (measured in various ways) by 3.5 mmHg (95% CI, -1.1 to -5.9) and DBP was reduced (measured in various ways) by 3.3 mmHg (95% CI, -1.3 to -5.3). Only three studies reported on changes in serum creatinine. There was no difference in serum creatinine at the end of these studies (MD - 2.5 µmol/L (95% CI, -7.9 to -2.9 µmol/L).

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation (132,137) identified through the systematic evidence review. Therefore, it is categorized as _Reviewed, Newadded_ . The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was low. The body of evidence had some limitations including small sample size, underpowered to identify differences in RDN modality, lack of clinical outcomes, limited duration of follow-up, and a lack of subgroup analyses. The benefits of RDN included improved SBP and DBP both in all comers, and in those with resistant hypertension, and slightly outweighed sham therapy given the limited reported adverse outcomes associated with RDN. Patient values and preferences are likely somewhat varied, because some patients may prefer a procedure over medical therapy for adverse effects, or because of the number of BP medications they are prescribed (i.e. resistant hypertension). Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

# **X. Research Priorities**

During the development of the 2026 VA/DOD Hypertension CPG, the Work Group identified topics needing additional research, including areas requiring stronger evidence to support current recommendations and research exploring new areas to guide future CPG updates.

## **A. Monitoring**

Further research evaluating clinical outcomes, specifically cardiovascular outcomes, based on treatment to home blood pressure goals, is needed to support HBPM. Evaluation of the costbenefit for providing HBPM to Veterans and active-duty Service members, with a specific focus on the potential long-term cost vs. benefit of better blood pressure control throughout the entire health-care enterprise, should be explored. Studies should also be done to assess barriers on the implementation of AOBP, as adoption has been limited.

## **B. Blood Pressure Goals**

Future research should prioritize identifying the most promising population(s) for more intensive SBP lowering and whether even lower SBP targets (<120 mmHg) are more beneficial as compared with a SBP goal of <130 mmHg. Additionally, research should consider age specifics for goals given the large age span across the active-duty DOD to VA patient population, considering those who are elderly, frail, and possibly unable to tolerate a more intensive target. Further clinical trials should also explore exclusively enrolling individuals with an elevated DBP > 90mmHg.

## **C. Pharmacotherapy**

Future studies should explore outcome data in those with resistant hypertension, as they are at increased risk for cardiovascular events, to inform clinicians on which fourth-line drug will have the greatest impact on cardiovascular events. Research should also be conducted on the impact of age, sex, and comorbidities like diabetes, CKD, coronary artery disease (CAD), or prior stroke on the response to specific medications in patients with resistant hypertension.

Head-to-head comparisons of alternative agents are needed, including aprocitentan and baxdrostat, as well as RCTs that evaluate the safety and efficacy of stepped therapy versus multidrug combination single-pill medication using FDA-approved medications in VA/DOD or similar populations. Additionally, robust head-to-head comparisons evaluating within-medicationclass differences, including differences in age, hypertension stage, comorbidities, morbidity, and mortality, and pharmacogenetics to aid in identifying responders, would be beneficial.

## **D. Lifestyle Interventions**

Future research in this space should stratify the effects of physical activity on BP by frequency, intensity, and duration of exercise, including the use of cardiac rehabilitation or other group exercise classes provided by trained personnel. Socioeconomic status of the sample population should be explored to provide more insight into equity, feasibility, and subgroup considerations. Additionally, research on smoking and alcohol cessation and how it impacts blood pressure should be done using bio-verification vs. self-reporting.

## **E. Other Treatment Modalities**

Future research should explore healthcare professional-led interventions to manage blood pressure. Studies should evaluate the cost-effectiveness of pharmacist-led interventions for hypertension, given their resource-use implications. Dietitian-led interventions should also be evaluated, focusing on the intensity and duration of the intervention types that are most effective for improving blood pressure control. Additionally, more research is needed to evaluate the effectiveness of nurse-led interventions to manage hypertension across specific subgroups (e.g., age, ethnicity, comorbidities) and to explore the effectiveness of nurse-led interventions that include initiation and titration of antihypertensive medications using established protocols.

Further research is needed to explore long-term comparisons between incretin-based therapies and bariatric surgery, as well as adherence differences with various weight loss medications, to include GLP-1 receptor agonists, and assess the long-term effects of these medications and the sustainability of desired outcomes in patients.

Additionally, some populations remain unstudied regarding renal artery stenosis (RAS), including those with fibromuscular dysplasia or those with recurrent hospitalizations for volume issues; these should be objectives for future research. In these patients, it may be reasonable to attempt intervention for RAS or involve them in future clinical trials. Future research should also evaluate the long-term effects of renal artery denervation on blood pressure, as well as its impact on clinical outcomes.

Lastly, future research should include continued research with new and emerging digital health technologies to include artificial intelligence integration/assistance, synchronous versus asynchronous modalities, and explore demographic preferences in relation to digital health technology uptake.

## **B. Conducting the Systematic Review**

Literature searches identified 15,138 citations potentially addressing the KQs of interest for this evidence review. Of these, 13,031 were excluded upon title/abstract review for lack of relevance to the CPG topic, not being published in English, or publication prior to the inclusion date. ### **_a. General Criteria for Inclusion in Systematic Evidence Review_**

- Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) or SRs of RCTs published on or after March 1, 2019, to November 12, 2025.

- Studies must have been published in English.

- Publication must have been a full clinical study or SR; abstracts alone were not included. Similarly, letters, editorials, research protocols, and other publications that were not fulllength clinical studies were not accepted as evidence.

- Systematic reviews must have searched MEDLINE or EMBASE for eligible publications, performed a risk of bias assessment of included studies, and assessed the quality of evidence using a recognizable rating system, such as GRADE or something compatible (e.g., the one used by the AHRQ Evidence-based Practice Centers [EPCs]). If an existing review did not assess the overall quality of the evidence, evidence from the review was reported in a manner that allowed us to judge the overall risk of bias, consistency, directness, and precision of evidence. We did not use an existing review as evidence if we were not able to assess the overall quality of the evidence in the review. If multiple SRs addressed a KQ, we selected the most recent and/or comprehensive review. In many cases, we used multiple SRs to address a single KQ, as each SR dealt with a different intervention or intervention-comparator combination.

- RCTs must have had an independent control group. Randomized crossover trials were only included if data from the first period (prior to treatment crossover) was reported separately or an adequate washout period was used.

- For KQ 1, diagnostic accuracy studies and SRs of diagnostic accuracy were included. For KQ 3 (weight loss), RCTs with pre-planned subgroup analyses, observational studies and SRs of observational studies that report on groups who did and did not lose weight were used to address the KQ. For all other KQs, only SRs of RCTs and individual RCTs were considered.

- Study must have enrolled at least 20 patients (10 per study group for RCTs and 20 for observational studies).

- Study must have enrolled at least 85% of patients who meet the study population criteria. If the patient population fell below this threshold but the relevant population of patients was reported separately, then that study was included.

- To ensure applicability to the VA/DOD healthcare systems, and ensure consistency across the CPG program, inclusion of individual studies was limited to very high Human Development Index (HDI), countries with an index ≥0.8 where standards of healthcare are comparable (e.g., United States, Canada, United Kingdom, Western Europe, Israel, Japan, Hong Kong, Australia, and New Zealand). Inclusion of the systematic reviews was limited to those including more than half of the studies from eligible regions.

 - These regions of interest are listed in Table 1 of the Statistical Annex of the 2023/24 Human Development Report produced by the United Nations Development Program.

- Study must have reported on at least one outcome of interest.

### **_c. Rating the Quality of Individual Studies and the Body of Evidence_**

Sigma Health Consulting assessed the methodological risk of bias of individual diagnostic, observational, and interventional studies using the USPSTF method. Each study is assigned a rating of _Good_ , _Fair_ , or _Poor_ based on a set of criteria that vary depending on study design. Detailed lists of criteria and definitions appear in Appendix VI of the USPSTF procedure manual.(36)

Next, Sigma Health Consulting assessed the overall quality of the body of evidence for each critical and important outcome using the GRADE approach. This approach considers the following factors: overall study quality (or overall risk of bias or study limitations), consistency of evidence, directness of evidence, and precision of evidence. The overall quality of the body of evidence is rated as _High_ , _Moderate_ , _Low_ , and _Very Low._

## **C. Developing Evidence-Based Recommendations**

In consultation with the VA Office of Quality and Patient Safety and the Clinical Quality Improvement Program, Defense Health Agency, and Sigma Health Consulting convened a 3.5 day in-person recommendation development meeting from February 23-27, 2026, to develop this

CPG’s evidence-based recommendations. Two weeks before the meeting, Sigma Health Consulting finalized the systematic evidence review and distributed the report to the Work Group; findings were also presented during the recommendation development meeting.

Led by the Champions, the Work Group interpreted the systematic evidence review’s findings and developed this CPG’s recommendations. The strength and direction of each recommendation were determined by assessing the quality of the overall evidence base, the associated benefits and harms, patient values and preferences, and other implications (see Determining Recommendation Strength and Direction).

### **_a. Determining Recommendation Strength and Direction_**

Per GRADE methodology, to assess the quality of the evidence base and assign a grade for the strength for each recommendation, the GRADE system uses the following four domains to assess the strength of each recommendation (32):

### _1. Confidence in the Quality of the Evidence_

Confidence in the quality of the evidence reflects the quality of the evidence base and the certainty in that evidence. This second domain reflects the methodological quality of the studies for each outcome variable. In general, the strength of recommendation follows the level of evidence, but not always, as other domains may increase or decrease their strength. The evidence review used for the development of recommendations for hypertension diagnosis and management, conducted by Sigma Health Consulting, assessed the confidence in the quality of the evidence base and assigned a rate of “High,” “Moderate,” “Low,” or “Very low.”

The elements that go into the confidence in the quality of the evidence include:

- Is there high or moderate quality evidence that answers this question?

- What is the overall certainty of this evidence?

### _2. Balance of Desirable and Undesirable Outcomes_

Balance of desirable and undesirable outcomes refers to the size of anticipated benefits (e.g., increased longevity, reduction in morbid event, resolution of symptoms, improved quality of life, decreased resource use) and harms (e.g., decreased longevity, immediate serious complications, adverse event, impaired quality of life, increased resource use, inconvenience/hassle) relative to each other. This domain is based on the understanding that most providers will offer patients therapeutic or preventive measures if the advantages of the intervention exceed the risks and adverse effects. The certainty or uncertainty of the provider about the risk-benefit balance will greatly influence the strength of the recommendation.

Some of the discussion questions that fall under this domain include:

- Given the best estimate of typical values and preferences, are you confident that the benefits outweigh the harms and burden or vice versa?

- Are the desirable anticipated effects large?

- Are the undesirable anticipated effects small?

- Are the desirable effects large relative to undesirable effects?

### _3. Patient Values and Preferences_

“Patient values and preferences” is an overarching term that includes patients’ perspectives, beliefs, expectations, and goals for health and life. More precisely, it refers to the processes that individuals use in considering the potential benefits, harms, costs, limitations, and inconvenience of the therapeutic or preventive measures in relation to one another. For some, the term “values” has the closest connotation to these processes. For others, the connotation of “preferences” best captures the notion of choice. In general, values and preferences increase the strength of the recommendation when there is high concordance and decrease it when there is great variability. In a situation in which the balance of benefits and risks are uncertain, eliciting the values and preferences of patients and empowering them and their surrogates to make decisions consistent with their goals of care becomes even more important. A recommendation can be described as having “similar values,” “some variation,” or “large variation” in typical values and preferences between patients and the larger populations of interest.

Some of the discussion questions that fall under the purview of values and preferences include:

- Are you confident about the typical values and preferences and are they similar across the target population?

- What are the patient’s values and preferences?

- Are the assumed or identified relative values similar across the target population?

### _4. Other Implications_

Other implications consider the practicality of the recommendation, including resources use, equity, acceptability, feasibility and subgroup considerations. Resource use is related to the uncertainty around the cost-effectiveness of a therapeutic or preventive measure. For example, statin use in the frail elderly and others with multiple co-occurring conditions might not be effective and depending on the societal benchmark for willingness to pay, might not be a good use of resources. Equity, acceptability, feasibility, and subgroup considerations require similar judgments around the practicality of the recommendation.

## **D. Recommendation Categorization**

#### _1. Recommendation Categories and Definitions_

For use in the 2026 Hypertension CPG, a set of recommendation categories was adapted from those used by the U ’s NICE.(37,38) These categories, along with their corresponding definitions, were used to account for the various ways in which recommendations could have been updated from the 2020 VA/DOD Hypertension CPG. A summary of the recommendation categories and definitions is available in Table 4.

_2. Categorizing Recommendations with an Updated Review of the Evidence_

Recommendations were first categorized by whether they were based on an updated review of the evidence. If evidence had been reviewed, recommendations were categorized as “New added,” “New-replaced,” “Not changed,” “Amended,” or “Deleted.”

“Reviewed, New-added” recommendations were original, new recommendations that were not in the 2020 Hypertension CPG. “Reviewed, New-replaced” recommendations were in the previous version VA/DOD Clinical Practice Guideline on Diagnosis and Management of Hypertension guideline but were modified to align with the updated review of the evidence. These recommendations could have also included clinically significant changes to the previous version.

Recommendations categorized as “Reviewed, Not changed” were carried forward from the previous version of the CPG unchanged.

Recommendations could have also been designated “Reviewed, Deleted.” These were recommendations from the previous version of the CPG that were not brought forward to the updated guideline after review of the evidence. This occurred if the evidence supporting the recommendations was out of date, to the extent that there was no longer any basis to recommend a particular course of care and/or new evidence suggests a shift in care, rendering recommendations in the previous version of the guideline obsolete.

_3. Categorizing Recommendations without an Updated Review of the Evidence_

There were also cases in which it was necessary to carry forward recommendations from the previous version of the CPG without a review of the evidence. Due to time and budget constraints, the update of the Hypertension CPG could not review all available evidence on hypertension diagnosis and management but instead focused its KQs on new or updated scientific research or areas that were not previously covered in the CPG.

For areas of research that have not changed, and for which recommendations made in the previous version of the guideline were still relevant, recommendations could have been carried forward to the updated guideline without an updated review of the evidence. The support for these recommendations in the updated CPG was thus also carried forward from the previous version of the CPG. These recommendations were categorized as “Not reviewed.” If evidence had not been reviewed, recommendations could have been categorized as “Not changed,” Amended,” or “Deleted.”

“Not reviewed, Not changed” recommendations refer to recommendations from the previous version of the Hypertension CPG that were carried forward unchanged to the updated version. The category of “Not reviewed, Amended” was used to designate recommendations which were modified from the 2020 VA/DOD Hypertension CPG with the updated GRADE language, as explained above.

Recommendations could also have been categorized as “Not reviewed, Deleted” if they were determined to be out of scope. A recommendation was out of scope if it pertained to a topic (e.g., population, care setting, treatment, condition) outside of the scope for the updated CPG as defined by the Work Group.

The categories for the recommendations included in the 2026 version of the guideline are noted under the Recommendations. The categories for the recommendations from the 2020 VA/DOD Hypertension CPG are noted in Appendix C.

## **E. Drafting and Finalizing the Guideline**

Following the face-to-face meeting, the Champions and Work Group members were given writing assignments to craft discussion sections to support each of the new recommendations. The Work Group also created tables, appendices, and other sections for inclusion in the 2026 VA/DOD

Hypertension CPG for inclusion. During this time, the Champions and Work Group also updated the algorithm.

After developing the initial draft of the CPG, an iterative review process was used to solicit feedback on and revise the CPG. Once they were developed, the first two drafts of the CPG were posted on the Hypertension Wiki Website for a period of 14-20 business days for internal review and comment by the Work Group. All feedback submitted during each review period was reviewed and discussed by the Work Group, and appropriate revisions were made to the CPG. Following the Draft 3 review and comment period, the Work Group reviewed external feedback and created a final draft of the CPG.

# **Appendix E: Patient Focus Group Methods and Findings**

## **A. Methods**

VA and DOD Leadership recruited seven participants for the focus group with support from the Champions and other Work Group members as needed. The goal of recruitment for this Patient Focus Group was to have a group of engaging, diverse patients receiving VA or DOD healthcare services, who could cogently explain their experience with hypertension. Participants were mixed in terms of receiving care from the VA and DOD, with one receiving care from some civilian providers. Participants’ length of hypertension treatment ranged widely from as recent as two years to as long as 30 years.

The Work Group, with support from Sigma Health Consulting, identified topics on which participants’ input was important to consider in developing the CPG. Sigma Health Consulting developed, and the Work Group approved, an interview guide covering these topics. The focus group facilitator who led the discussion used the guide to elicit the participants’ perspectives about their treatment and overall care. Not all questions included in the Moderator’s Guide for the Hypertension Patient Focus Group were addressed by participants, but because the moderator encouraged conversation between the Patient Focus Group members, most topics were covered.

 - **B. Patient Focus Group Findings**

- **_a. Participants stressed the importance of whole-person approaches using individualized treatment plans_**

 - Most participants emphasized that treatments should be individualized because a ‘onesize-fits-all' approach often fails to address high blood pressure.

 - Many participants mentioned that they wanted to resist ‘lifelong pills’ and instead focus on lifestyle changes like quitting smoking, losing weight, and adjusting their diet.

- **_b. Participants emphasized that continuity of providers, integrated records, care coordination, and communication between VA and DOD systems are essential for effective treatment and engagement._**

 - Some participants reported that they received care from providers at different facilities, and the communication between providers was lacking, causing discrepancies and the participants feeling as though they had to relay information.

 - Overall, participants emphasized the need for stronger communication and coordination amongst all providers (primary care, specialists, pharmacists, etc.)

- **_c. Participants emphasized the importance of accurate screening and early diagnosis of high blood pressure, hyperaldosteronism, and co-occurring conditions._**

 - Participants noted that early diagnosis and treatment of hypertension and co-occurring conditions would help patients reduce their cardiovascular risk and get their hypertension and the side effects of medications under control sooner.

 - Participants also mentioned that accurate screening is valuable and would have helped them receive an effective treatment plan earlier in their hypertension management process.

- **_d. Participants highlighted the value of finding medication(s) that are effective and minimize side effect._**

 - Participants valued having a medication (or combination) that minimized disruptions to their daily life because of pharmacotherapy side effects or hypertension symptoms.

 - The participants also reported having felt relief when they finally found medication(s) that controlled their hypertension.

- **_e. Participants found monitoring hypertension at home to be both beneficial and uncomplicated, utilizing devices and mobile applications to track blood pressure measurements._**

 - Most participants noted they did not experience difficulty when measuring their blood pressure at home, and sometimes prefer it compared to in a clinic.

 - Participants also mentioned that they take advantage of mobile applications to share blood pressure readings taken at home with their healthcare provider

## **A. Module A: Screening and Diagnosis**

1. Module A begins with Box 1, in the shape of a rounded rectangle: “Individuals in the health care system with a properly measured blood pressure (Sidebar 1)”.

2. Box 1 connects to Box 2, in the shape of a hexagon, asks the question: “Is the patient currently being treated for hypertension?”

 - a. If the answer is “Yes” to Box 2, then Box 3.

 - b. If the answer is “No” to Box 2, then Box 4.

3. Box 3, in the shape of an oval: “Go to Module B, Box 10”.

4. Box 4, in the shape of a hexagon, asks the question: “Is repeated measurement SBP ≥130mmHg or DBP ≥ 0mmHg? (Sidebars 2 & 4)”

 - a. If the answer is “Yes” to Box 4, then Box 5.

 - b. If the answer is “No” to Box 4, then Box .

5. Box 5, in the shape of a rectangle: “Confirm hypertension diagnosis. (Sidebar 2) Obtain history, physical and assess for target organ damage and comorbid conditions (Sidebar 3); consider baseline testing as appropriate”.

6. Box 5 connects to Box , in the shape of a hexagon, asks the question: “Is a secondary cause of hypertension suspected?”

 - a. If the answer is “Yes” to Box , then Box 8.

 - b. If the answer is “No” to Box , then Box .

7. Box , in the shape of a rectangle: “Follow up:

 - Check blood pressure at routine clinic visits;

 - Address other CV risk factors and offer lifestyle modifications as appropriate”.

8. Box 8, in the shape of a rectangle: “Evaluate as indicated; consider referral to specialist”.

9. Box , in the shape of an oval: “Go to Module B, Box 10”.

## **B. Module B: Treatment**

1. Module B begins with Box 10, in the shape of a rounded rectangle: “Patient needs hypertension management and treatment”.

2. Box 10 connects to Box 11, in the shape of a rectangle: “Assess/Reassess patient values and preferences; discuss and implement lifestyle modification utilizing shareddecision making and motivational interviewing (Sidebar 5)”.

3. Box 11 connects to Box 12, in the shape of a hexagon, asks the question: “Patient needs and agrees to pharmacotherapy?”

 - a. If the answer is “Yes” to Box 12, then Box 13.

 - b. If the answer is “No” to Box 12, then Box 14.

4. Box 13, in the shape of a rounded rectangle: “Initiate, continue, or optimize pharmacotherapy (Sidebars 7, 8, 9)”.

5. Box 13 connects to Box 14, in the shape of a rectangle: “Implement one or more of the following (as appropriate) to improve adherence and/or blood pressure control:

 - HBPM (home blood pressure monitoring)

 - Pharmacist-led, nurse-led, or dietitian-led co-interventions (Sidebar 5)

 - Digital Health Interventions (Sidebar 6)”.

6. Box 15, in the shape of a rectangle: “Follow-up regularly (e.g., monthly) until:

 - Blood pressure is at goal (Sidebar 4)

 - Treatment regimen is optimized (Sidebar 8) Note: Co-occurring conditions and other patient characteristics and preferences may change frequency of follow-up visits.”

7. Box 15 connects to Box 16, in the shape of a hexagon, asks the question: “Is the patient’s blood pressure at goal?”

 - a. If the answer is “Yes” to Box 18, then Box 17.

b. If the answer is “No” to Box 18, then return to Box 13.

8. Box 17, in the shape of a rectangle:

 - Encourage patient engagement in blood pressure monitoring and healthy lifestyle in between visits.”
