## **I. Introduction**

The Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) and Department of Defense (DOD) Evidence-Based Practice Work Group (EBPWG) was established and first chartered in 2004. Its mission is to advise the “…Health Executive Council on the use of clinical and epidemiological evidence to improve the health of the population across the Veterans Health Administration and Military Health System,” by facilitating the development of clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) for the VA and DOD populations.(1) The development and update of VA/DOD CPGs is funded by the VA Evidence-Based Practice, Office of Quality and Patient Safety. The system-wide goal of evidence-based CPGs is to improve patient health and well-being.

In 2025, the VA and DOD initiated the creation of the Menopause CPG, based on evidence reviewed from January 1, 2015, to June 30, 2025. This CPG uses the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach, reflecting a rigorous application of the methodology.(2) Therefore, the strength of some recommendations may have been modified based on the confidence in the quality of the supporting evidence (see Evidence Quality and Recommendation Strength).

The VA/DOD EBPWG initiated the creation of the VA/DOD Menopause CPG in 2025. This CPG provides an evidence-based framework for evaluating and managing individuals with naturally occurring or surgically/iatrogenically induced menopause or perimenopause. Successful implementation of this CPG will result in:

- Accurate assessment of the patient’s menopausal status and, in collaboration with the patient, determine the most appropriate treatment plan;

- Minimization of preventable complications and morbidity using early screening and risk assessments;

- Emphasis on the use of informed, patient-centered care;

- Optimization of daily functioning, functional independence, health outcomes, and quality of life

## **II. Background**

### **A. Menopause and Perimenopause Description and Epidemiology**

##### _Perimenopause_

Perimenopause is the transitional stage of reproductive aging that precedes menopause. Perimenopause typically begins in the mid- to late 40s, though the onset varies by individual and symptoms may begin in the mid- to late 30s. The duration of perimenopause averages about four years but can extend up to a decade before the final transition to menopause.(3) Perimenopause is a universal physiological process, yet its manifestations and impact on health and functioning vary widely.

Perimenopause begins with changes in menstrual cycle length or regularity due to declining ovarian function and ends with menopause.(4) Hormonal signals between the brain and ovaries

become less synchronized, resulting in Luteal Out-Of-Phase (LOOP) cycles with recruitment of a new follicle during the luteal phase of the current cycle. Simultaneously, ovulation becomes increasingly unpredictable. As a result, progesterone is oftentimes the first hormone to show consistent decline while estrogen widely fluctuates, leading to elevations followed by sharp declines. Although less pronounced, testosterone levels gradually decrease during the menopausal transition.

These hormonal changes ultimately produce a wide range of clinical manifestations. Common symptoms include vasomotor instability (hot flashes, night sweats), irregular or heavy menstrual bleeding, and urogenital symptoms.(5) Nearly 80% of individuals experience vasomotor symptoms during the transition, and up to one-third report vasomotor symptoms that are moderate to severe.(6) Sleep disturbances, depressive symptoms, anxiety, mood changes, and cognitive complaints (such as perceived memory difficulties) are also common and may exacerbate existing mental health conditions or impair occupational functioning.(7) Patients may present with a single or multiple symptoms, and when multiple systems are involved, symptoms can interact with and intensify one another. The unpredictability of menstrual cycles and associated symptoms can further adversely affect quality of life, relationships, and daily activities.(8)

Perimenopause is also considered a critical window of vulnerability for longer-term health risks, including bone loss, cardiovascular changes, and metabolic alterations.(9) Recognition and timely management of perimenopausal symptoms can therefore have both immediate and long-lasting benefits.

In the general population, the demographic effect is substantial. More than 50 million women in the United States (U.S.) are currently aged 45 years and older;(10) thus, a large proportion are experiencing or approaching perimenopause. Stigma and under-recognition of perimenopausal concerns may discourage patients from seeking care, leading to a lack of symptom management and the possibility of increased health care costs.(11)

##### _Menopause_

Natural menopause is defined as the cessation of ovarian function. Natural menopause is diagnosed retrospectively after one year of amenorrhea. Surgically induced menopause is caused by bilateral oophorectomies before the final menstrual period. (See Sections C and D for more information about menopause types and factors that impact menopause onset.)

In the U.S., the average age of menopause onset is 51 years old.(12) During peri- and postmenopause, patients may experience a variety of symptoms, including vasomotor symptoms, cognitive impairment, changes in hair and skin, musculoskeletal symptoms, vaginal changes, decreased libido, changes in bladder symptoms, sleep disturbances, metabolic changes, and weight gain. While mood changes may be experienced in menopause, there is no statistically significant increase compared to perimenopause. These symptoms can be mild, moderate, or severe, and may last on average between 7 and 12 years. For some patients, vasomotor symptoms may persist beyond 12 years.(7) Because menopause is defined retrospectively, patients may not know when they have transitioned to menopause. This determination can be further complicated in individuals who have undergone hysterectomy, uterine ablations, or are receiving hormonal treatments, making it difficult to pinpoint the onset of menopause. Clinicians

should reassure patients that symptom-based management is appropriate for most treatment decisions.

### **B. Menopause in the Department of Defense and the Department of Veterans Affairs Populations and Its Impact**

##### _DOD_

Women comprise a growing proportion of the U.S. Armed Forces. In 2023, women represented 17.7% of active duty personnel (225,119 of 1,273,382) and 21.9% of the Reserve (167,762 of 767,238). The average age in the female population is 28.6 for active duty and 32 for Reservists, with a sizable number of officers older than 41 years (22.6%), after which age there is no further breakdown by the DOD.(13) As the force ages and retention of all Service members remains a priority, perimenopause and menopause are a consideration which may affect readiness, health, safety, and retention for thousands of female Service members.

The effect of perimenopausal and menopausal symptoms, such as impaired heat tolerance, sleep disturbance, impaired concentration, mood disturbances, and effects on physical performance, may be disruptive to a Service member’s duties as well as having operational relevance, particularly in austere environments where the military serves. Unmanaged symptoms may contribute to decreased readiness, safety risks, profile limitations, and attrition. Menopausal hormone therapy (MHT) may be underutilized in the active duty population. A 2025 retrospective study of female active duty Service members aged 45 to 64 years in the U.S. Army, Air Force, Navy, and Marine Corps from 2018 to 2022 showed that the prevalence of MHT use among this population is lower than MHT use among the U.S. Veteran population of the same age, though it is higher than the U.S. general population age 45 and older.(14)

Unfortunately, more direct, peer-reviewed data on menopause among currently serving U.S. military women is sparse. There are no studies specifically on menopause in Service members, as most evidence in the U.S. comes from Veterans in the VA. There are limited quantitative data linking menopausal symptoms to duty performance, mishaps, or attrition. Evidence is needed on racial and ethnic differences in symptom duration and severity (well-documented in civilian cohorts) as they pertain to the military setting.

National surveys and mixed-methods studies of United Kingdom (U.K.) female active duty Service members reveal that menopausal symptoms are common, affect work, and are inconsistently managed. Themes from these studies included symptom effects on performance, fear of career consequences, and barriers to accessing knowledgeable clinicians or appropriate therapy.(15) In the absence of robust data in the U.S. DOD, the literature from the U.K. suggests that there is work to be done in supporting servicewomen during this transition.

##### _VA_

The U.S. Department of Veteran Affairs, Veterans Health Administration (VHA), provided healthcare to more than 700,000 women Veterans in FY 2024, of whom nearly half (41%) were between the ages of 45 and 64.(16) 45% of the women in this age cohort who used VA health services in Fiscal Year (FY) 2024 belonged to a racial or ethnic minority group (Table 1).(17) VHA provides comprehensive perimenopause and menopause care through an interdisciplinary

approach, starting with the Veterans' designated women’s health primary care providers (WH PCPs) who are trained and experienced in the provision of women’s health care. Care is tailored to the needs of the specific Veteran using a shared decision-making process in which providers, patients, and patient care partners (e.g., family, friends, caregivers) consider the benefits and risks as well as patient values and preferences to make decisions regarding the patient’s treatment.

VHA offers a range of evidence-based treatment modalities to manage symptoms related to perimenopause and menopause, including hormone therapy, non-hormonal medications, mental health support and treatment, pelvic floor physical therapy, stress management, weight management, and whole health approaches. In addition, gynecologists and other trained providers, such as clinical pharmacists, are available to support complex medical management of symptoms related to perimenopause and menopause. Mental and behavioral health providers can provide evidence-based treatments for symptoms associated with perimenopause and menopause, including sleep disturbance, mood disorders, cognitive changes, sexual dysfunction, and vasomotor symptoms. Complementary and integrative health (CIH) offerings such as mindfulness, yoga, and clinical hypnosis are also offered for menopause-related symptoms.

These efforts include attention to the unique and complex care needs of the women Veteran population, including prevalent trauma history, chronic pain, other chronic health conditions, and possible military exposures, all of which may influence perimenopause and menopause symptom burden.(18)

### **C. Factors Affecting Menopause Onset**

Natural menopause is a natural biological transition occurring in the second half of life, but the experience varies significantly amongst individuals, due to a variety of factors, including heredity, race and ethnicity, family history, gynecological, medical, and mental health history, lifestyle behaviors, toxin exposure, and military service. Early menopause occurs between the ages of 40 and 45, and premature menopause occurs before the age of 40.

##### _Genetics and Heredity_

A key component of menopause onset is driven by ovarian aging,(19) a complex process influenced by multiple factors, including genetics and family history. Genetics is a key driver of the age variation around menopause onset, accounting for up to 50% of this variation (20), and a patient’s family history of female family members’ age at menopause may provide clues to the potential timing of the menopause transition.(21)

##### _Race, Ethnicity, and Socioeconomic Status_

Women Veterans aged 45-64 years are a diverse group with 130,518 (48%) women identifying with a minority group. In FY 24, the composition is as follows: 140,456 (49%) White, 98,192 (34%) Black or African American, 19,187 (7%) Hispanic or Latina, 4,295 (1.5%) Asian, and American Indian or Alaska native, Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, and multi-race each at 1%. Research is continuing to examine differences in menopause onset and experience for individuals from different racial and ethnic identities, including intersections with socioeconomic status. Initial analysis of the Study of Women's Health Across the Nation (SWAN) study, a multisite, multiracial/ethnic, prospective, longitudinal cohort study of 3,302 women aged 42 to 52 years with an intact uterus, at least one ovary, not on hormonal therapy, and not pregnant or lactating, did not find any difference in menopausal onset between Black and White women.(22) However, re-analysis in 2023 with adjustment for selection bias revealed that Black women were 13% more likely to experience early natural menopause and 3.2-fold more likely to have surgical menopause than White women.(23) Black women in the SWAN cohort also experienced a longer duration of menopause transition.(22) In a very large multiethnic cohort study, both U.S.-born and non-U.S.-born Latina experienced natural menopause earlier and Japanese Americans later than non-Latina women.(24) The influence of race and ethnicity on the timing of menopause onset for Asian Americans is less clear, as these may be bidirectionally impacted by additional factors, including socioeconomic and lifestyle factors.(22,25)

In the SWAN study, low socioeconomic status metrics, including limited education and unemployment, were independently associated with earlier natural menopause.(22,26) The negative impact of low socioeconomic status was corroborated in a study comparing women living in very high and very low vulnerability neighborhoods as defined by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)’s Social Vulnerability Index; the former experienced natural menopause approximately two years earlier than the latter.(27) A recent cross-sectional survey study of over 67,000 women found that after adjustment for socioeconomic status as determined by household income and structure, educational level, public assistance with

income and/or food stamps, and employment status, Black and Hispanic women experienced more severe menopausal symptoms than their White and Asian peers.(25)

##### _Obstetric and Gynecologic History_

The interplay between reproductive health history, life events, menopausal symptoms, and timing is complex and multifactorial, and the subject of diverse ongoing research.(28,29) Reproductive health life events that temporarily reduce ovulation, such as pregnancy and breastfeeding, have been associated with a delay in menopause onset.(28,29) Similarly, oral contraceptive use has been associated with later menopause onset.(22,29) A recent metaanalysis (MA) also identified an association between menarche after age 13 and later menopause onset.(29) In contrast, hysterectomy, even with ovarian conservation, may be associated with earlier ovarian failure and earlier menopause onset.(30) Infertility history may also influence both menopause symptom severity(31) and timing.(32) Elucidating the possible links between infertility history and menopause is complicated by the multifactorial nature of both infertility and its treatment, as well as menopause itself.

##### _Autoimmune Disease_

Some autoimmune diseases have been linked to early menopause. The prevalence of early menopause is increased in women with thyroiditis(33-36), Addison’s disease (37), Type 1 diabetes(38-40), polyglandular autoimmune diseases, vasculitis, systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, sarcoidosis, and inflammatory bowel disease.(41)

##### _Pharmacotherapy and Radiation Therapy_

Chemotherapeutic agents can increase the risk of premature menopause in a cumulative dosedependent manner. The most common chemotherapeutic agents associated with premature menopause are alkylating agents, with cyclophosphamide being the most well-studied. Other alkylating agents associated with ovarian toxicity include busulfan, carmustine, chlorambucil, ifosfamide, lomustine, mechlorethamine, melphalan, procarbazine, and thiotepa, as well as the nonclassical alkylators dacarbazine and temozolomide. Platinum-based agents (cisplatin and carboplatin) also heighten the risk of premature ovarian insufficiency and reduced ovarian reserve. Additionally, anthracyclines (e.g., doxorubicin) cause ovarian damage through mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress, and taxanes (e.g., paclitaxel) can interfere with oocyte meiotic spindle function. The dose, duration, and age at the time of use determine its impact on ovarian function. Pelvic and ovarian radiation therapy are another major cause of premature ovarian insufficiency (POI). Beyond chemotherapy, adjuvant endocrine treatments used in cancer care and management of uterine fibroids and endometriosis can also trigger menopausal symptoms, including aromatase inhibitors (AIs) (anastrozole, letrozole, and exemestane), selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs) (tamoxifen and toremifene), the selective estrogen receptor degrader fulvestrant, and gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonists (goserelin, leuprolide, and triptorelin). (42-44)

##### _Stress_

Chronic stress may play a role in early menopause onset. In a cohort of 3,176 Korean women participating in the 5th Korean National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), chronic stress was linked to an earlier onset of menopause by approximately five months.(45)

_Lifestyle Behaviors_

Although the relation between diet and menopause onset has been studied, current data do not definitively clarify the influence of diet types on menopausal timing.(46-48) Similarly, there are no conclusive temporal effects between weight and exercise intensity on menopause onset.(22,46,47,49-51)

Tobacco smoking is strongly linked with earlier menopause by 1.5 to 2 years.(22,24,26,46,49,52,53) This adverse impact is dependent on the magnitude of tobacco use.(54)(54)(54)(54) At the 14- and 21-year follow-up time points in one study, women who quit smoking experience a comparable onset of menopause to the nonsmoker group.(52) Alcohol consumption 5-7 days/week or ≥11 drinks/week is associated with delayed menopause by 1.3 to 5.5 years.(22,54) Caffeine intake does not affect menopause onset.(54)(54)(54)(54)

##### _Toxic Chemical Exposures_

Environmental chemicals and pollutants exposure is associated with 1.9-3.8 years of earlier onset of menopause compared to those without exposure. In the U.S. NHANES of 31,575 females, polychlorinated biphenyls (electric transformer equipment, industrial fluids, and building and construction materials), dioxins (combustion byproducts), phthalates (plasticizers), organophosphate pesticides, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (combustion products), and organophosphate esters (flame retardants and plasticizers) are not only linked to earlier onset but also exhibit a dose-dependent effect.(55,56) Information on military service in this cohort of women is not available. For female Veterans, there is currently no conclusive evidence that hazardous materials exposure is directly linked to early menopause; however, an increased prevalence of infertility is noted in personnel with exposure to petrochemicals and polychlorinated biphenyls during military service compared to those without exposure, which suggests that toxic chemical exposure may play a role in ovarian dysfunction. This topic warrants further investigation.(57)

_Military Service_

Military sexual trauma is disproportionately high among female Veterans and is strongly linked to menopausal symptoms. There is a 2.4-fold higher risk of developing vasomotor symptoms and a 2.2-fold higher risk of experiencing vaginal symptoms (vaginal irritation and dryness and/or dyspareunia) among women Veterans with a history of military sexual trauma compared to those with no such history.(18) Another risk factor for menopausal symptoms is post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), which is more prevalent in Veteran women compared to non-Veteran women and can result from prolonged stress from recurrent exposure to life-threatening conditions and combat during military service.(58) Finally, in a study of women aged ≤ 45 years during the 19901991 Gulf War, similar proportions of deployed and non-deployed women reported experiencing

early menopause (14.8% vs. 15.9%, respectively, p=not significant [NS]). The determination of possible early menopause is limited by recall bias of self-reporting data, absence of menopause onset age, and lack of objective data on menopause status (unable to exclude menstrual suppression while using birth control, secondary amenorrhea, or bilateral oophorectomy with or without hysterectomy). Despite similar prevalence, within this Gulf War Era Cohort of 668 deployed women, the adjusted odds of reporting early menopause are higher in women who experienced Gulf War Illness (Odds Ratio (OR): 1.83, 95% Confidence Interval (CI): 1.14-2.95, p=0.01) and probable PTSD (OR 2.45, 95% CI 1.54-3.90, p<0.001).(59)

### **D. Primary Ovarian Insufficiency**

Primary Ovarian Insufficiency (POI) resembles natural menopause in its clinical presentation, including hypoestrogenism, vasomotor symptoms, urogenital atrophy, increased risk of osteoporosis, and increased cardiovascular risk. The distinction is the age of onset: while menopause occurs around age 51, POI occurs before age 40. Unlike menopause, POI has an unpredictable course, with intermittent ovarian activity and spontaneous ovulation possible, resulting in conception in 5-10% of affected women.(60-62)

According to the 2024 American Society for Reproductive Medicine/European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology (ASRM/ESHRE) Guideline, the diagnosis of POI requires menstrual disturbance (amenorrhea or oligomenorrhea) for at least 4 months, accompanied by elevated follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH >25 international units per liter [IU/L]) with simultaneous low estradiol (often <50 picograms per milliliter [pg/mL] is utilized) on at least one occasion.(60) The prevalence of POI is approximately 1-3.5% of women under 40 years.(60,63)

POI can be categorized as spontaneous, iatrogenic, or surgical. Spontaneous POI may be idiopathic or associated with genetic, autoimmune, or metabolic etiologies.(63) Iatrogenic POI commonly occurs after gonadotoxic therapies such as chemotherapy or pelvic radiation, while surgical POI results from bilateral oophorectomy.(60,63)

POI has major fertility implications. In spontaneous POI, although overall fertility is reduced, intermittent ovarian activity may result in spontaneous ovulation and pregnancy in a minority of cases.(62,64) In contrast, following bilateral oophorectomy, ovulation is not possible due to the absence of ovarian tissue, and natural conception cannot occur. In other forms of iatrogenic POI, particularly after highly gonadotoxic therapies, ovarian function is often absent, and natural conception is unlikely. Because ovarian activity may be intermittent in spontaneous POI and some forms of iatrogenic POI, patients should be counseled on effective contraceptive options if pregnancy is not desired. Fertility preservation, including oocyte or embryo cryopreservation, should be offered to those at risk before gonadotoxic therapy or planned surgical removal of ovaries. For individuals with established POI, donor oocyte in vitro fertilization provides the most effective path to pregnancy.(60,64)

If a patient is under the age of 40 and suspected to have spontaneous POI, referral to a reproductive endocrinologist or other specialist is preferred. If no reproductive endocrinologist is available, the next appropriate referral would be to a gynecologist for diagnostic evaluation and counseling.(60)

Pelvic radiation is a common cause of iatrogenic POI and may also result in significant vaginal, cervical, and uterine tissue changes. Management of patients with radiation-induced POI generally follows the same principles as management of other forms of POI, including hormone therapy when not otherwise contraindicated. Patients with an intact uterus receiving systemic estrogen therapy should also receive adequate progestogen for endometrial protection. Management should be individualized in collaboration with oncology specialists when there is a history of hormone-sensitive malignancy.(65-67)

MHT is strongly recommended, in the absence of contraindications, at least until the average age of natural menopause (approximately age 50-51 years). In contrast to treatment initiated after natural menopause, hormone therapy in POI is intended to replace ovarian hormone production and reduce the long-term risks associated with prolonged hypoestrogenism, including bone loss, cardiovascular disease, and genitourinary symptoms.(65,66)

Because intermittent ovarian function and spontaneous ovulation may still occur in some individuals with POI, contraception should be discussed when pregnancy is not desired despite the use of hormone therapy.(60) Estrogen therapy should be combined with a progestogen in women with an intact uterus. Replacement-dose estrogen therapy is generally recommended, such as oral estradiol 1–2 milligrams (mg) daily or transdermal estradiol 100 micrograms (mcg) daily, with appropriate endometrial protection in patients with an intact uterus. Because these replacement regimens do not provide contraception and intermittent ovarian activity may occur, patients who do not desire pregnancy should be counseled regarding effective contraceptive options. Combined hormonal contraceptives may be considered for those seeking both symptom management and contraception, although physiologic hormone replacement regimens may be preferable for optimizing bone health. If the patient is at risk for unintended pregnancy, contraceptive options should be considered. Management should also include bone density monitoring, cardiovascular risk reduction, and psychological support.(60,63,65,66)

### **E. Force Readiness**

_Effect on Military Readiness_

Symptom management during perimenopause and menopause is not just a health and wellness issue for Service members; it is a critical component of military readiness. Untreated symptoms can substantially undermine physical, cognitive, and emotional capabilities in some women, which are fundamental for operational effectiveness. A failure to provide adequate care for these treatable symptoms carries significant consequences, not only for the individual Service member but for the force as a whole.(68)

Symptoms can directly threaten a Service member’s ability to perform duties in multiple ways, including:

- Cognitive Impairment: “Brain fog”, memory issues, and difficulty concentrating reduce focus, decision-making ability, and situational awareness, which are critical in high-stakes environments.

- Sleep Disruption: Night sweats, insomnia, and other symptoms can impact sleep quality, leading to delayed reaction time, impaired judgement, and degradation of overall physical and cognitive performance.

- Musculoskeletal Injuries: Decreasing bone density and muscle mass increase the risk of injury, directly compromising the ability to train, deploy, and perform core tasks.

- Genitourinary Symptoms: Hypoestrogenism is a risk factor for recurrent urinary tract infections. In addition, urinary symptoms can lead women to delay hydration and increase sick call visits, disrupting daily duties and field exercises.

- Mood Disturbances: Increased anxiety, irritability, and higher risk of depression can impact team cohesion, leadership effectiveness, and overall performance, while simultaneously risking an increase in medical profiles that limit a Service member’s duties and deploy ability.

- Menstrual Irregularities: Unpredictable and heavy bleeding can impede execution in the field environment where hygiene supplies and sanitation may be limited.

The consequences can create a ripple effect that impacts career longevity, retention, and operational stamina. Furthermore, untreated menopausal symptoms also increase the risk of chronic diseases such as diabetes, cardiovascular disease (CVD), obesity, metabolic syndrome, and osteoarthritis, further influencing long-term fitness and increasing the healthcare burden within DOD and VA systems. (69-71)

### **C. Demographic and Other Terminology in This Guideline**

The demographic terms in this guideline are based on the published literature sources included in the systematic review (SR) and the evidence base. The Work Group used terms such as Black rather than African American and White rather than Caucasian to avoid presumptions about ancestry and improve clarity and consistency. To accurately present the research evidence on which this CPG is based, the Work Group made every effort to use the same terminology as reported in the published literature base of SRs, clinical trials, and other studies. Consequently, the usage of demographic terms in this CPG may vary and appear inconsistent.

Precise and intentional language has been utilized in this document to describe the hormones involved in menopause. “Estrogen” is used to describe the endogenous 18-carbon chemical compounds that have varying affinities for estrogen receptors, of which 17β-estradiol is the most relevant and active. “Progesterone” is used to describe the endogenous 21-carbon chemical compounds that bind and activate progesterone receptors.

When referencing systemic MHT, estrogen, or “E” as shorthand, is used to describe the estrogenic component of a MHT regimen. Conjugated estrogen (CE) denotes compounds formulated with multiple active estrogens from either equine or synthetic sources. Estradiol references either endogenous 17β-estradiol produced by the ovary or the bio-identical synthetic 17β-estradiol. Systemic estradiol is available in various forms, including oral pills, transdermal gels, sprays, creams, patches, and systemic vaginal rings. Non-oral forms of estradiol bypass the liver and are thought to carry less risk of venous thromboembolism. Local vaginal estradiol, used to treat GSM, is not considered systemic and does not carry the same risks. Local preparations include vaginal creams, tablets, and a local vaginal ring (distinct from the systemic vaginal ring). Phytoestrogens are naturally occurring compounds with structural similarity to estrogens obtained and consumed in dietary sources.

When referencing systemic MHT, progestogen, or “P” as shorthand, is used as shorthand for the progestogenic component of an MHT regimen. “Progesterone” references either the endogenous hormone produced by the ovary or the bio-identical micronized progesterone (MP) available as a pill. “Progestin” denotes synthetic compounds with progesterone-like activity and includes medroxyprogesterone acetates, norethindrone, norethindrone acetate, and levonorgestrel. The class “progestogen” includes both progesterone and progestins. Progestogen is an important component of MHT if the woman has a uterus, as it protects the lining of the uterus. The progestogen can be taken daily or 12 days per month if the patient prefers having a monthly cycle.

## **VI. Summary of Guideline Development Methodology**

The methodology used in developing this CPG follows the Guideline for Guidelines, an internal document of the VA/DOD EBPWG updated in January 2019 that outlines procedures for developing and submitting VA/DOD CPGs.(72) The Guideline for Guidelines is available at http://www.healthquality.va.gov/policy/index.asp. This CPG also aligns with the National Academy of Medicine’s (NAM) principles of trustworthy CPGs (e.g., explanation of evidence quality and strength, management of potential conflicts of interest [COI], interdisciplinary stakeholder involvement, use of SR and external review).(73) Appendix A provides a detailed description of the CPG development methodology.

### **A. Evidence Quality and Recommendation Strength**

The Work Group used the GRADE approach to craft each recommendation and determine its strength. Per the GRADE approach, recommendations must be evidence-based and cannot be made based on expert opinion alone. The GRADE approach uses the following four domains to inform the strength of each recommendation (see Determining Recommendation Strength and Direction).(74)

1. Balance of desirable and undesirable outcomes

2. Confidence in the quality of the evidence

3. Patient or provider values and preferences

4. Other implications, as appropriate (e.g., resource use, equity, acceptability, feasibility, subgroup considerations)

Using these four domains, the Work Group determined the relative strength of each recommendation ( _Strong or Weak_ ). The strength of a recommendation is defined as the extent to which one can be confident that the desirable effects of an intervention outweigh its undesirable effects and is based on the framework above, which incorporates the four domains.(75) A Strong recommendation generally indicates High or Moderate confidence in the quality of the available evidence, a clear difference in magnitude between the benefits and harms of an intervention, little variability in patient values and preferences, and limited concerns about the influence of other implications (e.g., resource use, feasibility).

In some instances, insufficient evidence exists on which to base a recommendation for or against a particular therapy, preventive measure, or other intervention. For example, the systematic evidence review might have found little or no relevant evidence, inconclusive evidence, or conflicting evidence for the intervention. The way this finding is expressed in the CPG might vary. In such instances, the Work Group might include among its set of recommendations a statement of insufficient evidence for an intervention that might be in common practice, although it is unsupported by clinical evidence, and particularly if other risks of continuing its use might exist (e.g., high opportunity cost, misallocation of resources). In other cases, the Work Group might decide to exclude this type of statement about an intervention. For example, the Work Group might remain silent where an absence of evidence occurs for a rarely used intervention. In other cases, an intervention might have a favorable balance of benefits and harms but might be a standard of care for which no recent evidence has been generated.

Using these elements, the Work Group determines the strength and direction of each recommendation and formulates the recommendation with the general corresponding text as shown in Table 3.

**Table 3. Strength and Direction of Recommendations and General Corresponding Text**

|**Recommendation Strength and Direction**|**General Corresponding Text**|
|---|---|
|Strongfor|We recommend…|
|Weak for|We suggest…|
|Neither for nor against|There is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against…|
|Weak against|We suggest against…|
|Strongagainst|We recommend against...|

It is important to note that a recommendation’s strength (i.e., Strong versus Weak) is distinct from its clinical importance (e.g., a Weak recommendation is evidence based and still important to clinical care). The strength of each recommendation is shown in Recommendations.

This CPG’s use of GRADE reflects a rigorous application of the methodology; the determination of the strength of the recommendation is directly linked to the confidence in the quality of the evidence on outcomes that are critical to clinical decision making. The confidence in the quality of

the evidence is assessed using an objective, systematic approach independent of the clinical topic of interest. Therefore, recommendations on topics for which designing and conducting rigorous studies might be inherently more difficult (e.g., randomized controlled trials [RCT]) are typically supported by lower quality evidence and, in turn, Weak recommendations. Recommendations on topics for which rigorous studies can be designed and conducted might more often be Strong recommendations. Per GRADE, if the quality of evidence differs across the relevant critical outcomes, the lowest quality of evidence for any of the critical outcomes determines the overall quality of the evidence for a recommendation.(2,76) This strict standard provides a consistent approach to determining recommendation strengths. For additional information on GRADE or CPG methodology, see Appendix A.

## **VII. Approach to Care in the Department of Veterans Affairs and the Department of Defense**

### **A. Patient-Centered Care**

VA and DOD encourage providers to be sensitive to demographic, cultural, and other differences that affect patients’ values, needs, and preferences. Patient-centered care is aimed at treating the condition while also optimizing the individual’s overall health and well-being. Regardless of the care setting, all patients should have access to individualized evidence-based care. Patientcentered care can decrease patient anxiety, increase trust in providers, and improve treatment adherence.(82,83) A holistic whole-person health approach (https:/www.va.gov/wholehealth/) empowers and equips individuals to meet their personal health and well-being goals. Good communication is essential and should be supported by evidence-based information tailored to each patient’s needs. Guideline recommendations should be applied in a holistic approach to care that is patient-centered, culturally appropriate, and available to people with limited literacy skills and physical, sensory, or learning disabilities.

### **B. Shared Decision Making**

This CPG encourages providers to practice shared decision-making, a process in which providers, patients, and family/friend/caregiver consider clinical evidence of benefits and risks as well as patient values and preferences to make decisions regarding the patient’s treatment.(84) Shared decision making is emphasized in _Crossing the Quality Chasm_ , an Institute of Medicine report from 2001 (85), and is a core component of a holistic health approach. Providers must be adept at presenting information to their patients regarding individual treatments, expected risks, expected outcomes, and levels or settings of care, or both, especially where patient heterogeneity in weighing risks and benefits might exist. The Veterans Health Administration and the Military Health System have embraced shared decision-making. Providers are encouraged to use shared decision-making to individualize treatment goals and plans based on patient capabilities, needs, and preferences.

### **C. Patients with Co-occurring Conditions**

Co-occurring health conditions are important to recognize because they can contribute to the risk of early menopause, impact the management of menopause, influence patient or provider treatment priorities and clinical decisions, and affect the overall provider approach to the management of menopause. Providers should expect that many Veterans, Service members, and their families will have one or more co-occurring health condition(s). Because menopause is often accompanied by co-occurring conditions, collaborative management with other care providers is often best. Some co-occurring conditions may require early specialist consultation to determine necessary changes in treatment or establish a common understanding of how care should be coordinated.

### **A. Diagnosis of Perimenopause and Menopause**

##### **_Recommendation_**

1. We suggest against using laboratory testing (e.g., anti-müllerian hormone and/or folliclestimulating hormone) for the diagnosis of menopause and perimenopause. **(Weak against | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

Menopause is a natural biological process marking the end of a woman's reproductive years, defined as 12 consecutive months of amenorrhea in the absence of other pathological or physiological causes. Perimenopause refers to the transitional period preceding menopause, which can last several years and extends until 12 months after the final menstrual period.

Menopause remains a clinical diagnosis. The Work Group evaluated the available literature to assess the utility of laboratory markers in the diagnosis of menopause and perimenopause and found that laboratory testing adds limited clinical value and may, in fact, be misleading.

The SWAN(87) and the Stages of Reproductive Aging Workshop (STRAW)(12) have demonstrated significant day-to-day variability in hormone levels, particularly estradiol, progesterone, and FSH, during perimenopause. This variability makes it unreliable to use a single laboratory value for diagnosis or treatment decisions. Furthermore, hormone levels do not consistently correlate with the severity or presence of menopausal symptoms, and results that appear "normal" may provide false reassurance to patients experiencing significant symptoms.

_Estradiol and Progesterone_

During perimenopause, estradiol and progesterone levels fluctuate widely, often varying significantly from day to day. These fluctuations result in overlapping hormone levels between perimenopausal and reproductive-aged women, further complicating the interpretation of laboratory results.

##### _FSH_

FSH levels also vary considerably during the perimenopausal transition. While persistently elevated FSH levels over a four-month period have been proposed as a diagnostic tool for menopause (88), this approach is impractical in clinical settings and does not account for the influence of exogenous factors such as hormonal contraceptives or hormone therapy, which can significantly alter FSH levels.

##### _Serum anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH)_

AMH is produced by the granulosa cells of growing ovarian follicles and reflects the remaining ovarian follicular reserve. As ovarian function declines, AMH levels decrease, with a steep decline observed prior to the final menstrual period, making it a marker of interest in the diagnosis of menopause.

The Work Group evaluated the literature and identified one SR exploring the use of AMH as a diagnostic tool for menopause. The review did not support its clinical utility, and the overall

strength of evidence was rated as Very low. Notably, age was found to be a better predictor of menopause than any laboratory value. One included study examined AMH levels in women already clinically diagnosed with menopause and found AMH to be low, an unsurprising finding that does not support its diagnostic utility. Additional limitations of the available studies included the inclusion of non-randomized trials, reliance on non-standardized questionnaires, and variability in the assays used to measure AMH.

While the predictive value of AMH for menopause increases with age, particularly in late reproductive-aged women as the time to menopause shortens, its utility as a standalone diagnostic test remains unproven.

In recent years, marketing campaigns, cash-based practices, and social media have increasingly encouraged women to request hormone testing as part of their evaluation for menopausal symptoms. As a result, many patients present to their providers requesting hormone laboratory testing. However, the evidence clearly demonstrates that routine hormone testing has limited clinical value in the diagnosis and management of menopause and can be counterproductive. When laboratory results appear "normal" in the setting of significant menopausal symptoms, this disconnect can lead to confusion, delayed treatment, and false reassurance for both patients and providers.

The Work Group finds that laboratory testing, including FSH, AMH, estradiol, and progesterone, carries potential harm when used routinely for the diagnosis of menopause or perimenopause. Such testing is costly, subject to significant variability, and does not provide clinically relevant or actionable information in most cases.

Based on the available evidence, including the SWAN and STRAW data, the Work Group recommends against the routine use of laboratory testing for the diagnosis of menopause and perimenopause. Providers should prioritize a symptom-based, patient-centered approach to diagnosis and management, relying on clinical judgment rather than laboratory values.

Laboratory testing may have utility in the following select clinical scenarios: women who have undergone hysterectomy, desire fertility preservation, suspected POI, or have undergone chemotherapy or other gonadotoxic treatments. Outside of these specific circumstances, laboratory testing should not replace clinical judgment or patient-centered care.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(89) It is categorized as Reviewed, New-added. The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low. The body of evidence had limitations, primarily due to the inclusion of non-RCTs, non-standardized questionnaires, and the use of different assays to measure AMH. The benefits of checking labs in attempts to diagnose menopause or perimenopause were outweighed by the inaccuracies and cost. Patient values and preferences varied largely because some patients would likely want to know the approximate time until menopause and may not care about cost. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak against_ recommendation.

### **B. Management of Menopausal Symptoms**

#### **_a. Vasomotor Symptoms_**

##### **Introduction to 2026 Menopause Recommendations for the Management of Vasomotor Symptoms**

MHT is widely recognized as the most effective treatment for vasomotor symptoms (VMS). Professional guidelines from the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG), The Menopause Society, and the Endocrine Society consistently recommend hormone therapy as the first-line treatment for menopausal VMS. However, not all patients are candidates for MHT due to medical contraindications, coexisting conditions, comorbidities, or personal preference.

The Work Group acknowledges the importance of providing individualized care and offering a range of treatment options. In developing these guidelines, we reviewed the evidence to balance the benefits and risks of MHT while also addressing the need for effective non-hormonal alternatives. Additionally, the use of MHT is nuanced and must be tailored to the patient’s specific circumstances, including the presence of a uterus, underlying medical conditions, and concurrent medications. “Bioidentical hormones” are products with the same chemical structure as endogenous hormones. In the U.S., several Food and Drug Administration (FDA) ‑ approved bioidentical hormone products are available; these undergo rigorous evaluation for safety, efficacy, and quality. In contrast, compounded bioidentical hormones are custom-made formulations that are not FDA ‑ tested or approved. These products are unregulated, and there is no reliable evidence supporting their safety or effectiveness. The literature search for this CPG did not identify any studies evaluating compounded bioidentical hormone therapy.

Consistent with recommendations from ACOG, the Menopause Society, and the Endocrine Society, the Work Group supports using FDA ‑ approved products whenever they are available, rather than compounded alternatives.(90-92)

Additional considerations include access to knowledgeable and affordable care. Within the VA and DOD healthcare systems, variability in provider training, experience, and comfort with menopause management may affect patients’ ability to receive timely, evidence-based counseling and treatment. Ensuring equitable access to appropriately trained clinicians is therefore an important implementation consideration that may influence patient preferences, treatment uptake, and overall outcomes.

This section outlines evidence-based recommendations for the management of vasomotor symptoms, including both hormonal and non-hormonal approaches, to ensure comprehensive and patient-centered care.

##### **_Recommendation_**

2. In women with a uterus, we recommend systemic estradiol and progesterone for the treatment of vasomotor symptoms. **(Strong for | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

MHT is an effective treatment for VMS, with efficacy demonstrated across multiple RCTs. However, the safety profile of MHT varies by formulation, underscoring the importance of selecting regimens that provide symptom relief while maintaining uterine safety in women with an intact uterus. The 2026 VA/DOD Menopause Work Group’s strong recommendation for estradiol and progesterone for the effective treatment of VMS in women with an intact uterus is supported by high-quality RCTs. The strength of evidence was Moderate based on Lobo et al. 2018 (93) and Black et al. 2021 (94). In the REPLENISH Trial(94), estradiol/progesterone significantly reduced both the frequency and severity of moderate to severe VMS compared to placebo. The 1 mg estradiol/100 mg progesterone and 0.5 mg estradiol/100 mg progesterone doses met all efficacy endpoints, with improvements observed as early as week 4 and sustained through week 12. These findings were further detailed by Lobo et al. 2018 (93), which demonstrated statistically significant reductions in VMS frequency and severity with the same dose combinations compared to placebo, confirming both consistency and dose responsiveness of effect. The REPLENISH Trial demonstrated that the incidence of endometrial hyperplasia was 0% at 12 months. Systemic estradiol options now available include an oral pill, transdermal gel, spray, or patch, and a systemic vaginal ring.

The benefits of therapy outweigh the potential harms/burden. The benefits of this treatment include a reduction in the frequency, severity, and unpredictability of VMS, including hot flashes and night sweats, which are among the most disruptive manifestations of menopause. Treatment is also associated with improvements in sleep quality, likely mediated through more effective control of nocturnal VMS. In addition to improving VMS, many patients report reductions in musculoskeletal discomfort, including arthralgias, which may contribute to overall functional improvement and quality of life. Additional benefits may include improvements in skin integrity, such as decreased dryness and pruritus, and preservation of bone mineral density (BMD), reflecting the known physiologic effects of estrogen on skeletal health. While short-term safety has been demonstrated in RCTs, long-term risks and uncommon adverse outcomes remain incompletely characterized, necessitating individualized risk-benefit assessment and ongoing surveillance. Importantly, in women with an intact uterus, the inclusion of progesterone (either daily or taken 12 days per month) provides adequate endometrial protection, mitigating the risk of estrogen-induced endometrial hyperplasia and supporting the safety of combined therapy in this population. When selecting systemic estrogen therapy, clinicians should consider the route of administration as part of individualized shared decision-making. Although the recommendation applies to systemic estradiol regardless of route, nonoral formulations (e.g., transdermal patches, gels, and sprays) may be associated with a lower risk of venous thromboembolism than oral estrogen. In addition, palpitations are a commonly reported symptom during menopause transition and may improve with treatment of vasomotor symptoms in some women; however, the current literature does not demonstrate a direct causal relationship between systemic estradiol therapy and palpitations. Patients presenting with new-onset, persistent, or concerning palpitations should undergo an appropriate cardiovascular evaluation to exclude underlying cardiac or other medical conditions.

The Work Group also reviewed evidence related to systemic conjugated equine estrogen (CEE), both alone and in combination with MPA. While CEE-based regimens have demonstrated benefit

for VMS, the evidence identified important safety concerns. Compared with placebo, CEE+MPA was associated with an increased risk of stroke and invasive breast cancer, while CEE alone was associated with an increased risk of stroke, ovarian cancer, and ovarian cancer mortality. These safety signals were observed in large randomized controlled trials and long-term follow-up analyses and were considered clinically meaningful despite relatively low absolute event rates.(95-97) Consequently, when systemic menopausal hormone therapy is indicated for the treatment of VMS, the Work Group favored estradiol-based therapy combined with micronized progesterone because the evidence demonstrated effective symptom relief, adequate endometrial protection in women with a uterus, and a more favorable overall benefit-risk profile than CEEbased regiments.(95-97)

Patient values and preferences regarding the use of combined estradiol and progesterone for the treatment of VMS in women with a uterus largely varied and play an important role in shared decision-making. Many patients experiencing moderate to severe symptoms prioritize timely access to MHT to achieve symptom relief and improve quality of life. Conversely, some patients prefer to avoid pharmacologic therapy altogether, reflecting concerns about medication use, perceived risks, or a desire for nonpharmacologic approaches. Preferences regarding (e.g., oral versus transdermal) differ among patients and are often influenced by convenience, prior experiences, and perceptions of safety. Risk tolerance varies substantially, particularly with respect to concerns about cancer, cardiovascular events, or other serious adverse outcomes. A subset of patients expresses a preference for “bioidentical” hormone therapies, defined as plantderived hormones that are structurally identical to endogenous human hormones. However, there is considerable misinformation surrounding the term “bioidentical,” and clinicians should provide patient education to clarify that FDA-approved bioidentical hormone therapies are distinct from compounded bioidentical hormone preparations. There are FDA-approved formulations of MHT that are bioidentical. FDA-approved products should be preferred over compounded formulations when available.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to the use of combined estradiol and progesterone for the treatment of VMS, including data from RCTs such as the REPLENISH trial and associated analyses reported by Lobo et al. 2018 (93) and Black et al. 2021 (94) identified through the systematic evidence review. Therefore, it is categorized as Reviewed, New-added. Several studies included in the evidence review were conducted outside the United States and evaluated formulations that are not FDA-approved or commercially available in the U.S. The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of evidence was Moderate. The body of evidence had some limitations, including limited long-term follow-up and low event rates for rare but serious adverse outcomes, which reduced precision for estimating uncommon harms despite adequate sample size for efficacy outcomes.(93,94) The benefits of recommending estradiol and progesterone for effective treatment of VMS in women with a uterus outweigh the potential harm, which were generally infrequent and mild to moderate in severity in the reviewed trials. Patient values and preferences varied greatly; many patients prioritize effective symptom relief and quality of life improvement, while others express concern regarding hormone therapy risks or prefer nonhormonal options. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Strong for_ recommendation.

##### **_Recommendation_**

3. In women with a uterus, we suggest the use of conjugated estrogen with bazedoxifene for the treatment of vasomotor symptoms. **(Weak for | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

The VA/DOD Menopause Work Group endorses a weak suggestion for the use of systemic estrogen with bazedoxifene (BZA) for the treatment of VMS in women with a uterus. The strength of evidence is rated very low due to significant methodological limitations in the evidence base rather than an absence of treatment effect. In pooled analyses, BZA 20 mg/CE 0.45 mg reduced the daily number of hot flashes compared with placebo (standard mean difference [SMD] -5.89; 95% CI: -8.67 to -3.11), while BZA 20 mg/CE 0.625 mg demonstrated a similar or greater reduction (SMD -6.36; 95% CI: -9.36 to -3.36). Both doses also reduced moderate to severe hot flashes compared with placebo, although estimates demonstrated very high statistical heterogeneity (high I2 ~98-98%). I2 is a statistical measure used in meta-analyses to quantify how much the study results differ from each other. A high I2 indicates that a large proportion of the variability in the study results is due to real differences between studies rather than chance.(98,99) The pooled studies (98,99) had considerable differences such as populations, estrogen doses, outcome definitions, and follow-up durations, which limited confidence in the precision and generalizability of effect estimates. In a SR of seven RCTs, Bi et al. 2022 (99) found that CE/BZA significantly reduced the daily number of hot flashes, including moderate to severe hot flashes, compared to placebo. Both approved doses (BZA 20 mg with CE 0.45 mg or 0.625 mg) demonstrated benefit, with greater symptom reduction observed at the higher estrogen dose. These findings are supported by individual Selective Estrogens, Menopause, and Response to Therapy (SMART) trials summarized by Archer et al. 2016 (98), which demonstrated significant reductions in VMS frequency and severity with CE/BZA compared to placebo, while showing no increase in endometrial hyperplasia. The benefits of therapy slightly outweigh harms/burden. While the therapeutic benefits observed were comparable to those described in the narrative supporting Recommendation 2, adverse events were infrequent across reviewed trials. Serious cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events were rare, though the low number of events limited statistical precision and precluded robust estimation of long-term risk. Consequently, despite an overall acceptable short-term safety profile, uncertainty remains regarding uncommon and longerterm adverse outcomes.

Patient values and preferences regarding the use of systemic estrogen with BZA for the treatment of VMS in women with a uterus are generally similar to those described for Recommendation 2, particularly the desire for effective symptom relief, avoidance of progestins, and consideration of individual risk tolerance. An important distinguishing factor for this therapy is the limited modes of delivery, as estrogen-bazedoxifene is currently available only in specific oral formulations, which may not align with the preferences of patients who favor transdermal or alternative routes of administration. Other implementation considerations are also largely similar to Recommendation 2 but include additional system-level constraints. In particular, availability within the VA and DOD pharmacy formularies may be limited, which can affect access, continuity of care, and patient willingness to initiate or continue therapy. As with other menopausal hormone therapies, variability

in provider familiarity with this formulation may further influence uptake and shared decisionmaking. From a subgroup perspective, estrogen with BZA may be particularly attractive to women with a uterus who have concerns about breast cancer risk or who prefer to avoid progestogen exposure, given BZA’s role as an estrogen antagonist in breast and endometrial tissue. Additionally, this regimen may be valued by patients with osteopenia or concerns about bone health, as systemic estrogen and selective estrogen receptor modulators confer known skeletal benefits. These considerations highlight the importance of shared decision-making that incorporates symptom burden, comorbidities, access considerations, and patient preferences when selecting therapy.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to the suggested use of systemic CE/BZA for the treatment of VMS in women with a uterus, including an SR of seven RCTs (99) and the SMART trial **_(_** 98 **_)_** evidence summarized in the Menopause CPG Evidence Synthesis Report. Therefore, it is categorized as Reviewed, New-added. The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low, because the body of evidence had very serious study limitations and serious inconsistency with very high heterogeneity (I2 ~98-98%) in pooled VMS analyses. The benefits of CE/BZA slightly outweigh the potential harm because CE/BZA reduced the daily number of hot flushes and moderate-to-severe hot flashes compared with placebo and was associated with improvements in sleep-related outcomes and menopause-related quality of life (MENQOL).(99) Potential harms were infrequently reported in the VMS evidence synthesis, and key serious outcomes were not consistently reported, limiting precision and long-term risk estimation. Patient values and preferences varied largely as patients prioritize meaningful VMS relief and a progestin-free option, while others may be risk-averse given uncertainty in uncommon or longer-term adverse outcomes. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

##### **_Recommendation_**

4. In women without a uterus, we suggest the use of systemic estradiol monotherapy for the treatment of vasomotor symptoms. **(Weak for | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

The VA/DOD Menopause Work Group endorses a _Weak for_ recommendation for the use of systemic estradiol as an effective treatment for VMS in women without a uterus. For women without a uterus, the suggested use of systemic estradiol for moderate to severe VMS is primarily driven by the consistent evidence of symptom benefits versus placebo across multiple RCTs. Additionally, the ability to use estrogen without needing endometrial protection removes the uterus-specific risk that otherwise necessitates adding a progestogen. Gaudard et al. 2016,(100) provides a synthesized RCT evidence that showed estradiol (including transdermal patch and other systemic routes) reduces VMS frequency and intensity compared with placebo. For transdermal patch studies, pooled estimates showed a moderate to large reduction in hot flash frequency (e.g., SMD -0.68 [95% CI -0.83 to -0.53]) and a clinically meaningful reduction in hot flash intensity (e.g., MD -19.94 points on a 0-100 Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) [95% CI -24.86 to -15.02]). However, confidence is limited because pooled analyses demonstrated

inconsistency/heterogeneity (e.g., I2 67% for patch frequency; I2 54% for patch intensity) and the underlying trials were downgraded for study limitations (risk of bias/poor reporting) and imprecision, resulting in a low strength of evidence conclusion in the SR. The benefits of therapy outweigh harms/burden. While the therapeutic benefits observed were comparable to those described in the narrative supporting Recommendation 2, Gaudard et al. 2016 (100) notes that evidence regarding uncommon and long-term adverse outcomes remains limited, resulting in ongoing uncertainty about these risks.

Patient values and preferences related to the suggested use of systemic estradiol as an effective treatment for VMS in women without a uterus are generally similar to those described for Recommendation 2, particularly the desire for meaningful symptom relief, improvement in quality of life, and individualized risk-benefit assessment. As estrogen-only therapy does not require endometrial protection in this population, some patients may value the simplicity of treatment and avoidance of additional medications. Preferences regarding route of administration vary and may influence acceptability and adherence. For example, transdermal estrogen patches may be affected by perspiration and can detach prematurely or cause local skin irritation, while systemic estrogen gels may be perceived as inconvenient or cumbersome to apply. Intranasal estrogen preparations, although an alternative systemic option, may be non-formulary within the VA/DOD healthcare system, potentially limiting access. Additional considerations include access to appropriately trained providers and variability in clinician knowledge and comfort with menopause management, which may affect patient counseling, treatment selection, and continuity of care. These system-level factors, combined with individual preferences for route, convenience, and risk tolerance, underscore the importance of shared decision-making when considering systemic estradiol for VMS in women without a uterus.

One consideration regarding estrogen monotherapy in women post-hysterectomy should be the co-occurring diagnosis of endometriosis. Although outside the scope of the evidence review, some evidence suggests that an estrogen + progestogen (E+P) regimen is preferable to an E-only regimen in these patients for the suppression of endometriosis.(101)

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to the suggested use of systemic estradiol for the treatment of VMS in women without a uterus, including the SR by Gaudard et al. 2016,(100) 23 RCTs evaluating multiple systemic estradiol routes compared with placebo. Therefore, it is categorized as Reviewed, New-added. The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Low, because the body of evidence had limitations including risk-ofbias/study reporting concerns, statistical heterogeneity in pooled estimates (e.g., patch trials with I2 in the moderate-to-high range), and limited evidence for uncommon/long-term harms (e.g., no available data in this review for outcomes such as myocardial infarction [MI], stroke, or breast cancer). The benefits of systemic estradiol outweighed the potential harms for many appropriately selected patients because estradiol was associated with clinically meaningful reductions in hotflush frequency and intensity versus placebo. In women without a uterus, estradiol can be used without a progestogen, avoiding the endometrial hyperplasia concern that applies to unopposed estrogen in women with a uterus.(100) Potential harms included a higher likelihood of treatmentemergent adverse events compared with placebo and ongoing uncertainty regarding rare or longterm adverse outcomes due to limited data. Patient values and preferences varied largely as many patients prioritize effective VMS relief and improved function, while others may prefer

nonhormonal options or place higher weight on uncertainty regarding uncommon or long-term harms. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

##### **_Recommendation_**

5. For the treatment of vasomotor symptoms of menopause, we suggest against the use of progestogen monotherapy. **(Weak against | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

The VA/DOD evidence synthesis identified two RCTs evaluating micronized progesterone monotherapy versus placebo for VMS treatment, and both found no statistically significant difference in key VMS outcomes (frequency, score, and/or intensity). In Beinert et al. 2024 (102) reductions in moderate to severe VMS frequency were large in both progesterone and placebo groups, and differences between groups were not statistically significant, with interpretation limited by premature trial discontinuation, small sample size, and inadequate statistical power. In Prior et al. 2023,(103) the primary endpoint (3rd month VMS score) did not differ by therapy, and the trial also reported that variability and uncertainty limited confidence in the estimate. Due to the small body of evidence (only two RCTs), underpowered studies, and imprecise estimates, the VA/DOD Menopause Work Group rated the strength of evidence as low. The benefits and harms/burden are balanced. Potential benefits include mood stabilization with reductions in anxiety, decreased endometriosis-related pain, and uterine protection. Potential harms include ongoing vasomotor symptoms due to uncertain efficacy, irregular or breakthrough bleeding, breast pain, and additional adverse effects that remain insufficiently characterized.

Patient values and preferences relevant to the determination of a weak against recommendation of progestogen monotherapy for the treatment of VMS are generally consistent with those described for Recommendation 2, with important additional considerations. In particular, patients with contraindications to estrogen therapy may express interest in progestogen monotherapy as a potential alternative, despite the current uncertainty regarding its effectiveness for VMS. Other considerations include the relative ease of access and affordability of progestogen formulations, which may influence patient interest and utilization, especially in resource-constrained settings. However, access to appropriately trained providers remains a key factor, as counseling on uncertain benefits, potential harms, and alternative treatment options requires clinical expertise. Variability in provider knowledge and experience with menopause management may further affect patient understanding, shared decision-making, and consistency of care.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to micronized progesterone monotherapy for the treatment of VMS, including two RCTs: Prior et al. 2023 (103) and Beinert et al. 2024 (102), as summarized in the Menopause CPG evidence synthesis. Therefore, it is categorized as Reviewed, New-added. The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Low, because the body of evidence has important limitations, including a small number of trials, limited sample sizes, large placebo response, and imprecision/insufficient power to detect clinically meaningful differences (including premature trial discontinuation and underpowering in Effects of Progesterone on Vasomotor Symptoms in Postmenopausal Women [PROGEST]).(102) Benefits and harms are balanced. Potential harms were generally infrequent

but not absent; reported adverse effects included vaginal bleeding and breast pain in PROGEST. Patient values and preferences varied largely as some patients may prioritize potential sleeprelated or perceived symptom benefits, while others prefer therapies with clearer efficacy and may be unwilling to accept uncertain benefit alongside possible bleeding or other adverse effects. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak against_ recommendation for progestogen monotherapy for VMS.

##### **_Recommendation_**

6. We recommend cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) to manage vasomotor symptoms related to menopause.

**(Strong for | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

CBT is a brief, structured psychotherapy that helps people strengthen adaptive thought patterns and behaviors. Some versions of CBT specifically target menopausal symptoms, including depression, anxiety, vasomotor symptoms, sleep disturbances, sexual dysfunction, and cognitive changes.

Women going through the menopausal transition often experience adverse physical changes such as hot flashes (vasomotor symptoms) that can significantly impact sleep, functioning, and overall quality of life. CBT has been proposed as a low-risk treatment for menopausal symptoms to be used as a stand-alone or an adjunct to hormonal therapy. Unlike hormone therapy, it works by changing the perception and response to symptoms rather than eliminating the physical occurrence itself.

This recommendation is based on one SR (104), and one RCT.(105) The RCT studied Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Menopause (CBT-Meno), a manualized treatment specifically adapted to address menopausal symptoms. The SR covered a variety of CBT protocols, including but not limited to CBT-Meno. As there were no direct comparison studies of CBT-Meno and other forms of CBT, the recommendation encompasses all forms of CBT. There is evidence of improvement in coping with vasomotor symptoms related to menopause with CBT over waitlist control group. CBT reduces the bother, stress, and negative effects of hot flashes.

The overall strength of evidence is Moderate. The SR, which included 8 RCTs (104), was determined to be of fair quality based on risk of bias. Approximately 90% of the RCTs were judged to be at high risk of performance bias. The RCTs (105) overall quality of the included study is fair, as the study was not powered for beliefs outcomes.

The benefits of CBT outweigh the potential harm. Treatment burden, or the time and effort the patient would need to invest in CBT, would be the only potential harm.

Patient values and preferences regarding CBT for menopausal symptoms showed a large variation. While some patients value nonpharmacologic interventions, others prefer pharmacologic options. Some participants cited challenges with access to CBT for menopausal symptoms. Focus group participants disliked care fragmentation, and CBT integrates treatment of multiple menopausal symptoms in one intervention.

Even though CBT is considered an acceptable treatment by providers, the limited availability of CBT therapy for menopause within the VA/DOD may be a limiting factor in offering this treatment modality to patients.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation in Ye et al. 2022 (104) and Donegan et al. 2022 (105). Therefore, it is categorized as Reviewed, New-Added. The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Moderate. The benefits of CBT to treat or manage vasomotor symptoms related to menopause outweigh the potential harm, which only includes the treatment burden. Patient values and preferences varied largely, because overall, participants valued nonpharmacological interventions for the management of their menopausal symptoms but cited challenges with access and effectiveness. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Strong for_ recommendation.

##### **_Recommendation_**

7. We suggest elinzanetant or fezolinetant for vasomotor symptoms in menopausal women. **(Weak for | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

Elinzanetant and fezolinetant are newer non-hormonal agents that act as antagonists at neurokinin receptors in the hypothalamus (fezolinetant at NK3 receptors and elinzanetant at both NK1 and NK3 receptors). Evidence supporting fezolinetant was from an SR by Ismail et al. 2025 (106) which included 8 articles and 7 RCTs that compared this NK3 antagonist to placebo and found a beneficial effect across all critical outcomes. In particular, patients from 2 RCTs (n=570) had a mean reduction of 2.5 hot flashes per day and a modest improvement in severity of VMS compared to placebo at 12 weeks. Evidence in the study by Ismail et al. 2025 (106) was considered poor due to 4 of the included studies being found to be at high risk of bias due to issues with deviations from intended interventions and randomization; a majority of the studies included in the meta-analysis were at high risk of bias.(106) Similarly, benefit from elinzanetant vs. placebo was also found in an SR by Sobral et al. 2025 (107) which included 3 RCTS (OASIS 1 (108), OASIS 2 (108), SWITCH 1 (109)). Elinzanetant also showed a reduction in the frequency and severity of VMS. There was no statistically significant difference in adverse events with either fezolinetant or elinzanetant compared to placebo, but the follow-up only extended to 12 weeks. While not statistically significant, elinzanetant had more adverse events than placebo, most commonly headache, fatigue, somnolence, and diarrhea. While not considered in the strength of the recommendation, both agents had evidence supporting a beneficial effect on sleep.

For this recommendation, the Work Group only reviewed evidence to support the effectiveness of these agents. Literature to show the comparative effectiveness of these agents to MHT was not reviewed, and a recommendation was not made on this topic.

While evidence supporting the use of elinzanetant was deemed to be high, our enthusiasm was dampened due to the limited follow-up data available to date from which to judge long-term safety (FDA approval occurred in October 2025). Support for the use of fezolinetant was limited by the quality of existing data and the limited data to define potential long-term risks. Fezolinetant has potential for drug-drug interactions due to interaction via the cytochrome P450 family 1 subfamily,

a member 2 (CYP1A2) pathway (primary metabolism mechanism), and is thus contraindicated in those on CYP1A2 inhibitors. Potential drug interactions with elinzanetant may occur via the cytochrome P450 family 3 subfamily A member 4 (CYP3A4) pathway. Concerns for liver toxicity with fezolinetant require measuring liver enzymes prior to initiation, monthly for the first 3 months, then at 6 months and 9 months, or anytime patients have symptoms of acute liver injury, while elinzanetant requires baseline liver enzymes assessment. This additional testing could be burdensome to some patients, and the existence of baseline cirrhosis or markedly elevated liver enzymes is a contraindication to use. While these agents are likely appealing for those who cannot or who are not interested in the use of hormone-based therapy, the lack of long-term use and familiarity may make some patients less enthusiastic. Access to these agents may be limited due to cost, formulary restrictions, and availability. See Recommendation 11 for comparative effectiveness with hormone-based treatments.

While not within the scope of our review due to publication date and thus not considered in the strength of our recommendation, we are aware of a more recently published phase 3 trial comparing Elinzanetant for 52 weeks vs placebo for 12 weeks followed by elinzanetant for 40 weeks among women on endocrine therapy for a history of hormone receptor (HR)-positive breast cancer or its prevention (n=474; randomization at 2:1 ratio).(110) This trial reported a beneficial effect on VMS frequency and severity. During the first 12 weeks, similar proportions of participants in each arm experienced adverse events, most commonly headache, fatigue, and somnolence. Of note, this trial offered participants the opportunity to continue the intervention for an additional 2 years, and safety data will be collected on these individuals, though not available at the time of this publication .

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation from an SR by Sobral et al. 2025 (107) with 3 RCTs comparing elinzanetant to placebo and an SR by Ismail et al. 2025 (106) with 7 RCTs comparing fezolinetant to placebo. Therefore, it is categorized as Reviewed, New-added. The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was High for elinzanetant and Low for fezolinetant. The body of evidence had some limitations, including concerns for risk of bias related to randomization, deviations from assigned interventions, and measurement of outcomes among RCTs evaluating fezolinetant.(106) A limitation in the body of evidence for elinzanetant was the limited follow-up in the currently published data. The benefits of neurokinin receptor antagonists included reductions in VMS frequency, VMS severity, and improvements in sleep, which were felt to outweigh the potential harms primarily due to the possible risk of liver injury. Patient values and preferences are suspected to be varied due to concerns about newer agents and the frequency of required lab monitoring, while for others, these agents offer a more effective non-hormonal treatment alternative for patients with significant symptoms and for whom hormone-based therapies are not a good option. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation for both agents.

##### **_Recommendation_**

8. There is insufficient evidence to favor fezolinetant or elinzanetant versus hormone therapy for vasomotor symptoms.

##### **(Neither for nor against | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

The Work Group reviewed evidence evaluating the comparative effectiveness of fezolinetant and hormone therapy for menopausal vasomotor symptoms. Evidence informing this recommendation consists of a single network meta-analysis including 26 RCTs, with only indirect comparisons between fezolinetant and various hormone therapy regimens. No direct head-to-head trials comparing NK3 receptor antagonists with hormone therapy were identified in the evidence base.(111) At the time of this publication, there were no studies comparing elinzanetant to hormone therapy.

Across indirect comparisons, no consistent differences were observed between fezolinetant 45 mg and most hormone therapy regimens for reduction in vasomotor symptom frequency or severity over short-term follow-up (approximately 12 weeks).(111) Notably, the only hormone therapy regimens that showed significantly greater efficacy than fezolinetant on any outcome (tibolone 2.5 mg and CE 0.625 mg/BZA 20 mg) are not available at those doses in the U.S.(111) Fezolinetant 45 mg was significantly more effective than non-hormone therapies, including paroxetine 7.5 mg, desvenlafaxine 50-200 mg, and gabapentin extended release (ER) 1800 mg, in reducing VMS frequency.(111) Comparative safety outcomes between fezolinetant and hormone therapy, including cardiovascular events, venous thromboembolism, and malignancy, were not reported in this evidence base.(111)

The certainty of evidence was downgraded to Very low due to very serious indirectness inherent to network comparisons, heterogeneity of hormone therapy regimens, and short duration of followup.(111)

Patient values and preferences were judged to vary somewhat. Some patients prefer nonhormonal therapies to avoid hormone exposure or due to contraindications, while others prioritize treatments with longer clinical experience and established efficacy, such as hormone therapy.

While not reviewed by the CPG evidence synthesis, MHT is generally recognized as the most effective treatment for vasomotor symptoms and recognizes nonhormonal agents, including NK1 and NK3-receptor antagonists, as alternatives when hormone therapy is contraindicated or declined.(90,112-114) This guidance reflects usual clinical practice but did not influence the strength of this recommendation.

Feasibility and access considerations include potential formulary restrictions and cost barriers for newer agents such as fezolinetant and elinzanetant within VA and DOD systems. Equity considerations include variability in access to specialty menopause care and laboratory monitoring requirements for fezolinetant.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation, including a network meta-analysis with indirect comparisons between fezolinetant and/or elinzanetant and hormone therapy identified through the systematic evidence review.(111) Therefore, this recommendation is categorized as Reviewed, New-added. The Work Group’s confidence in the

quality of the evidence was Very low. The body of evidence had limitations, including very serious indirectness, heterogeneity of comparators, and short follow-up duration.(111) The benefits and harms of fezolinetant relative to hormone therapy were balanced, and comparative safety outcomes were not available. Patient values and preferences varied somewhat, with some patients preferring nonhormonal therapies and others prioritizing established hormone therapy. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Neither for nor against_ recommendation.

##### **_Recommendation_**

9. We suggest selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs)/serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) or gabapentin as non-hormonal options for menopausal vasomotor symptoms based on patient characteristics and preferences. **(Weak for | Reviewed, New-added)**

10. For menopausal vasomotor symptoms, in patients without a contraindication to hormone therapy, we suggest hormone therapy over either venlafaxine or gabapentin. **(Weak for | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

Patients unable to receive or who are not interested in hormone-based treatments for menopausal VMS, may be considered for alternative pharmacologic options that demonstrate efficacy in treating VMS.

_Serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs)/Serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs)_

Evidence supporting this recommendation came primarily from a large 2015 Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) SR by Grant et al which included data from 13 RCTs (n=4037) for this group of agents when compared to placebo.(115) As a group of pharmacologic agents, there was a small to moderate beneficial impact on vasomotor symptoms (SMD -0.35; 95% CI: -0.46 to -0.24) with duration measured up to 52 weeks.(115) Harms for this group of therapeutic agents were not well reported by studies included in this SR, but included adverse effects on metabolism, skin, and the nervous system, among others. Of note, numerous antidepressant agents were included within this analysis at dosages commonly used in practice for mood disorders (e.g., desvenlafaxine, escitalopram, fluoxetine) but were considered as a group rather than evaluated individually. Moreover, there was no evidence comparing individual agents in this category against each other; selection should be based on clinical judgement and consideration of individual patient characteristics.

For women who are unable to or not interested in taking hormone therapy, or for those with other indications for antidepressants, medications such as SSRIs/SNRIs may be an appealing, widely available, and low-cost option that could reduce polypharmacy and address multiple co-occurring symptoms simultaneously. Specifically, anxiety and/or depressive symptoms are commonly observed in perimenopause and menopause, and women Veterans have a high prevalence of mental health conditions such as major depressive disorder and post-traumatic stress disorder, which can be effectively treated with SSRI/SNRIs. Thus, if a patient has bothersome vasomotor symptoms and depression, selecting an SSRI or SNRI rather than the addition of MHT may allow

for treatment of both conditions with a single medication. Other considerations factored into this recommendation were known side effects of this class of therapeutic agents from extensive clinical experience with these agents and other literature. Specifically, these agents are known to have potential adverse effects, including weight gain and sexual dysfunction, which can vary across agents and which are often already concerns for peri- and post-menopausal women. We note that some of these agents, such as paroxetine, may be effective for VMS at lower dosages than are typically associated with unwelcome side effects like weight gain. Some women may be disinterested or unwilling to take medications well-known as mental health medications or may be reluctant to add an additional medication for a relatively small potential benefit. However, for example, if a patient has bothersome vasomotor symptoms and depression, selecting an SSRI or SNRI may allow for treatment of both conditions with a single medication. Of note, many conditions common among the Veteran and active duty Service member populations were excluded from trials contributing evidence to this recommendation, including those with preexisting conditions such as fibromyalgia, CVD, or prior use of similar agents.

Importantly, for women with a history of breast cancer on tamoxifen, the choice of agent should be undertaken carefully due to the potential effect of cytochrome P450 2D6 (CYP2D6) and the unintentional reduction in effective levels of tamoxifen (specifically caution with agents such as paroxetine and fluoxetine) (See Sidebar 3).

##### _Venlafaxine_

With respect to the use of SSRI/SNRI compared to MHT, we found evidence specific to estradiol versus venlafaxine from the pooled analyses from the Menopause Strategies: Finding Lasting Answers for Symptoms and Health (MsFLASH) trials. In these studies, estradiol was associated with greater improvement in menopause-related quality of life and vasomotor symptom outcomes compared with venlafaxine after 8 weeks. For total menopause-related quality of life measured by the MENQOL scale, estradiol showed a mean decrease of 0.3 to 0.5 points relative to control, while venlafaxine did not show significant improvement in total MENQOL scores in the pooled analysis.(116) While venlafaxine did demonstrate significant improvement in the psychosocial subscale, it did not significantly improve the vasomotor subscale, where estradiol showed the largest improvement at -1.2 points.(116) Notably, a reduction of ≥0.9 points in total MENQOL score and ≥2.0 points in the vasomotor domain are considered clinically meaningful thresholds; thus, while estradiol's improvements were statistically significant, they approached but did not clearly exceed these thresholds for clinically perceptible benefit. Comparative serious harms were not reported, and the trials were not designed to assess long-term safety outcomes. We found no other studies evaluating the use of other SSRIs/SNRIs vs MHT.

##### _Gabapentin_

Gabapentin is a gabapentinoid non-hormonal pharmacologic agent studied for the treatment of vasomotor symptoms in peri- and postmenopausal women that holds appeal for use among women who are not candidates, do not desire, or are intolerant to hormone-based treatments. Evidence for this recommendation came from 2 SRs with a total of 11 fair quality RCTs in one Shan et al. 2020 (117) and 5 fair quality RCTs in the other Grant et al. 2015.(115) In one analysis

with 447 patients across 2 RCTs, Shan et al reported a small beneficial effect on VMS frequency with an estimated reduction of 1.43 fewer daily hot flashes (95% CI: -2.56 to -0.29) after 24 weeks of treatment. Data at 12 weeks (7 RCTs, n=1913) found a larger reduction in hot flashes with 2.77 fewer daily hot flashes (95% CI: 4.29 to 1.24). A moderate to large effect on VMS severity was also reported, as well as a small to moderate benefit on sleep disturbances.

Enthusiasm for the use of gabapentin in this setting is dampened by the fact that the dosages of gabapentin studied ranged from 900 mg to 3000 mg daily, which often lead to intolerable side effects including somnolence, dizziness, peripheral edema, and cognitive impairment, which may be particularly unappealing in menopausal women experiencing brain fog and difficulty concentrating. In addition, gabapentin can be associated with weight gain, respiratory depression, and the potential for misuse. For women who are already taking gabapentin for other reasons (e.g., pain) or who have significant issues with sleep, leveraging its beneficial effects on vasomotor symptoms may be appealing without adding MHT and thereby limiting polypharmacy. In addition, it is a low-cost and readily available treatment option. Of note, this medication requires dose adjustment for renal impairment.

Of note, the Shan et al. 2020 (117) review also included comparisons between gabapentin and estrogen and antidepressants based on limited data from two small RCTs. Across these trials (total n=129; follow-up 4-12 weeks), estrogen therapy was more effective than gabapentin in reducing hot flash frequency (mean difference [MD] 2.26 per day; 95% CI: 0.15 to 4.38).(117) No statistically significant difference was found for hot flash severity between gabapentin and estrogen (SMD 0.29; 95% CI: -0.06 to 0.64), though estimates favored estrogen. Gabapentin improved symptoms compared with baseline but was consistently inferior to estrogen therapy.(117) Comparative adverse events, including discontinuation due to adverse effects and fatigue, were not clearly different between groups; however, estimates were imprecise due to small sample sizes. They reported some evidence that estrogen was more effective at reducing VMS frequency than gabapentin, and there was no statistically significant difference between gabapentin and antidepressants on VMS severity.

While not included in the CPG evidence review, professional society guidance from ACOG, the Menopause Society, and the Endocrine Society consistently identifies hormone therapy as the most effective treatment for menopausal vasomotor symptoms and recommends neurokinin receptor antagonists, SSRIs, SNRIs, and gabapentin as alternative options when hormone therapy is contraindicated or declined.(90,112-114) This guidance reflects usual clinical practice but did not influence the strength of this recommendation. While other guidelines discuss oxybutynin, the WG decided that the risks outweigh the benefits.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to Recommendation 9 from the fair quality AHRQ SR by Grant et al. 2015 (115) which included 13 RCTs and a total of 4037 participants, and Shan et al. 2020 (117). Therefore, it is categorized as Reviewed, New-added. The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Moderate. The body of evidence had some limitations, including concerns about allocation concealment, maintenance of comparable groups, and unclear use of intention-to-treat analysis. The benefits of antidepressants or gabapentin on vasomotor symptoms were felt to slightly outweigh the potential harm (e.g.,

weight gain, sexual dysfunction, or sedation with gabapentin. Findings from the patient focus groups suggested that some patients may be reluctant to use antidepressant medications because of the perceived stigma of these medications initially intended for treatment of other conditions, such as mental health diagnoses and potential unwillingness to add pill burden for relatively small benefit; however, they may be helpful for women with other indications for this class of agent and/or those who cannot or prefer to avoid hormone-based therapy. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to Recommendation 10, including pooled analyses of RCTs comparing estradiol with venlafaxine and an SR and meta-analysis comparing gabapentin with estrogen therapy identified through the systematic evidence review.(116,117) Therefore, this recommendation is categorized as Reviewed, New-added. The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Low, with limitations including short follow-up duration, small sample sizes, and imprecision. The benefits of hormone therapy, reflected by greater reductions in VMS, slightly outweighed the potential harms, which were incompletely characterized in the available evidence. Patient values and preferences varied somewhat, with some patients prioritizing maximal symptom relief and others preferring nonhormonal options. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

##### **_Recommendation_**

11. There is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against the following complementary and integrative health (CIH) interventions for menopausal vasomotor symptoms:

 - Acupressure

 - Acupuncture

 - Tai Chi/Qigong

 - Yoga

##### **(Neither for nor against | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

Patients in the VA/DOD Menopause Patient Focus Group expressed the importance of holistic approaches in the treatment of menopause. The Menopause Work Group therefore felt it was important to review evidence related to CIH interventions for the management of menopausal VMS. The evidence review examined several studies on acupressure, acupuncture, qigong, yoga, and slow-paced guided respirations. Overall, the literature-based review was characterized as mostly very low strength of evidence by small sample sizes, high attrition rates, and lack of blinding.

##### _Acupressure_

Acupressure is an approach based on principles of traditional Chinese medicine that involves applying manual pressure to specific points on the body, similar to acupuncture, but without needles. This type of treatment stimulates the body’s natural health abilities and restores balance to the body’s energy pathways. The evidence review looked at two RCTs that showed possible improvement in menopausal VMS with acupressure compared to sham or no treatment.(118,119)

However, these studies were of poor quality due to a low number of participants, selective reporting, high attrition, and lack of blinding. Additionally, the studies utilized different acupressure point locations (auricular and extremities) and commercialized acupressure patch vs. providerapplied pressure.

##### _Acupuncture_

Acupuncture is a technique that involves inserting very thin metal needles into the skin at precise points on the body to balance energy channels, with the aim of restoring and maintaining health. The placement of insertion is chosen based on a complex network of lines of energy known as meridians. Acupuncture is a mainstay of traditional Chinese medicine, which has been practiced for thousands of years. Acupuncture stimulates the central nervous system, signaling the body to release various substances, including endorphins, immune system cells, neurotransmitters, and neurohormones. Acupuncture may also influence involuntary central nervous system functions, such as blood pressure, blood flow, and body temperature regulation. The evidence review looked at one RCT and one umbrella review, which included three SRs and four RCTs. These studies found possible improvements in the severity and frequency of VMS with acupuncture compared to waitlist, but not compared to sham.(120-122) The overall strength of evidence of the umbrella SR was fair.

##### _Tai Chi/Qigong_

Qigong comprises a diverse set of practices that coordinate body, breath, and mind. Practices include moving and still meditation, massage, chanting, sound meditation, and non-contact treatments performed in a broad array of body postures. The evidence review examined one RCT, which reported improvement in overall score on the Menopause Rating Scale (MRS) for qigong compared to no treatment. (123) This study was of poor quality due to low number of participants with no ethnic diversity, selective reporting, high attrition, and lack of blinding.

##### _Yoga_

Yoga is a mind-body practice that combines physical postures, breathing techniques, and meditation to promote overall well-being and self-discovery. There are many different styles of yoga, each with its unique focus and approach. More than just physical exercise, yoga is considered a holistic practice that encourages self-awareness, personal growth, and a deeper connection with oneself. The evidence review looked at an SR with three RCTs (124) comparing yoga interventions to waitlist, which found no difference in hot flash severity for yoga versus control, and an RCT (119) comparing laughter yoga intervention to no treatment, which found improvement in VMS for intervention compared to control. The strength of evidence of these trials was very low due to small sample sizes, selective reporting, high attrition, and lack of blinding.

Overall, the harms of some of these CIH interventions are minimal, including mild soreness, bruising, lightheadedness, tingling or heightened sensitivity, emotional release, headache or fatigue, with the most significant harms being infection and needle-related injuries. This results in a balance of possible benefits vs. harm.

Additionally, the Work Group searched for evidence on aromatherapy, biofeedback, chiropractic interventions, hypnosis, low-frequency sound-wave therapy, meditation, mindfulness, pharmacopuncture, and tai chi, which resulted in either no RCTs or SRs for the defined patient population or single RCTs that were of significantly poor quality.

Patients in the VA/DOD Menopause Patient Focus Group expressed the importance of holistic approaches in the treatment of menopause and may be willing to try CIH options with minimal evidence for improvement of their VMS of menopause given the minimal risks and harms of these interventions.

CIH interventions within the VA and DOD may have limited availability of trained providers, and access may be limited, especially in rural areas.

Underlying conditions, such as physical limitations, which are common in Veterans and active duty personnel, as well as cost to the healthcare system or patients should be taken into consideration.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation, including three SRs and seven RCTs described in 11 publications.(118-124) Therefore, it is categorized as Reviewed, New-added. The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low. The body of evidence had some limitations including small sample sizes, selective reporting, high attrition, and lack of blinding. The benefits of CIH interventions showed some improvement in menopausal VMS and were balanced with some of the potential minimal harms including mild soreness, bruising, lightheadedness, tingling or heightened sensitivity, emotional release, headache or fatigue, with the most significant harms being infection and needle-related injuries. Patient values and preferences have some variation as some patients may be interested in trying holistic approaches including CIH options. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Neither for nor against_ recommendation.

##### **_Recommendation_**

12. There is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against the following supplements and nutraceuticals for menopausal vasomotor symptoms:

 - Amino Acids

 - Black Cohosh

 - Danggwijagyaksan

 - Fenugreek

 - Giant Fennel

 - Ginseng

 - Kamishoyosan

 - Maca

 - Magnesium

 - Melatonin

 - Probiotics

 - Red Clover

 - Resveratrol + Equol

- Salvia

- Soy Supplements

- Succinate

##### **(Neither for nor against | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

A wide variety of supplements and nutraceuticals are available without prescription and are marketed directly to consumers as being useful for the management of menopausal VMS. In the United States, these supplements are not regulated by the FDA as long as they do not claim disease benefit. The evidence review examined 23 studies, including seven SRs and 16 RCTs, with methodological quality ranging from good to poor based on USPSTF criteria. 10 of the 16 RCTs were rated poor quality due to, in many cases, incomplete outcome data, and no clear reporting on allocation concealment nor assessor blinding, and selective reporting. All SRs and all but one RCT compared the supplement of interest to placebo; one RCT compared the supplement to no intervention. Black cohosh, ginseng and soy had the most developed evidence bases in terms of number of SRs and included RCTs, whereas many of the other supplements were examined in smaller SRs and RCTs. Overall, the literature base reviewed was characterized by small sample size, shorter duration of follow-up (often weeks rather than months or years), and a multitude of different scales and approaches to measure change in VMS, with a majority of studies conducted outside the United States. Studies frequently lacked consistency in supplement and nutraceutical formulation, format, and dosing, and included limited to no information regarding possible harms with use. Many of the supplements studied were proprietary blends that may not be available in the US, limiting applicability to VA and DOD populations. Furthermore, a majority of reviewed studies excluded individuals with many common comorbidities including CVD and mental health diagnoses, again limiting applicability to VA and DOD populations. Vasomotor symptom outcome measures varied widely across studies and few, if any, minimal clinically indicated difference thresholds were defined, making assessments of impact on VMS difficult. Additionally, a substantial number of studies included in the SRs and others reviewed individually were funded, at least in part, by manufacturers of the supplement under consideration (see, for example, Lee et al. 2022 (125) [ginseng]; Wilfried et al. 2021(126) [salvia]; and Kanadys et al. 2021(127) [red clover]). For these reasons, the Work Group determined that there was insufficient evidence to recommend for or against the reviewed supplements and nutraceuticals. We provide brief overviews of the literature for those supplements that are commonly available in the US as well as those whose evidence base was more substantial. We also include a brief summary statement on the remainder of the supplements reviewed.

##### _Black Cohosh_

Black cohosh is widely marketed as an over-the-counter supplement for menopausal VMS.(128) Although the literature reviewed did not directly address the mechanism of action, prior literature suggests possible interactions with serotonergic, dopaminergic, and adrenergic pathways.(129,130) The evidence review looked at one SR (131), which included 15 RCTs, and a much smaller RCT of only 50 participants in each arm.(132) While the SR had Moderate strength of evidence, and both the SR and stand-alone RCT showed an effect favoring intervention, included studies used varying doses of black cohosh, differing measures of change in VMS, and

contained scant information about harms. Importantly, case reports outside of the identified literature review described significant liver adverse events.

##### _Ginseng_

Ginseng (Panax ginseng) is a root native to the Far East that has been used in traditional medicine systems for various symptoms. The Work Group reviewed one SR and one RCT examining ginseng interventions on menopausal VMS; the overall strength of evidence was low. The SR (125) included three RCTs with 258 patients in the intervention arms and 257 patients in the placebo arm, with 12 weeks of follow-up. The meta-analysis of these three RCTs found a reduction in menopausal symptom score and in hot flashes in the ginseng intervention group. However, the RCTs were conducted outside the US, with varying formats and dosing of ginseng and minimal details of harms. Hyun et al. 2022 (133), a small RCT looking exclusively at the safety of using ginseng along with a combination herbal extract of multiple other herbs, found no serious adverse events over 12 weeks, but this formulation was distinct from the ginseng interventions reviewed in the SR.

##### _Soy_

Soybeans are a common part of dietary patterns in many cultures, particularly those in East Asia. Soy protein and soy isoflavones may have health effects. Soy isoflavones in particular have been marketed in various formats as menopausal symptom remedies.(134) The Work Group reviewed two SRs regarding soy supplementation. While the strength of evidence of the included two SRs was rated Moderate, the effect on VMS was mixed. In the Gençtürk et al. 2024 (135) SR, a metaanalysis of five RCTs found no difference in VMS between the intervention and placebo arms. A subset of four of the RCTs included in the SR by Kang et al. 2022 (136) noted an improvement in the overall Kupperman index score (a menopause symptom severity scale developed in the US in the 1950s).(137) However, a meta-analysis of six RCTs within the same SR found no difference in hot flash scores vs placebo.(136) Studies included in the reviewed SRs had widely varying doses of soy supplementation, used a multitude of soy formulations (for example, encapsulated soy, fermented soy, soy drinks, all of different dosing and strengths), and had minimal mentions of possible harms.(135,136)

##### _Resveratrol + Equol_

Resveratrol (polyphenolic phytoestrogen) + equol (end product of daidzein, a major soy isoflavone) is a nutraceutical combination marketed over the counter for menopause VMS. The Work Group reviewed one RCT (n=60) where participants took a combined soy + additional isoflavones plus resveratrol and equol proprietary blend (138) and a second pilot non-blinded efficacy study (139) of a different Italian proprietary blend that contained resveratrol + equol. While the RCT suggested a reduction in VMS greater than the reduction experienced in the placebo group, as did the pilot study, both studies were Low quality, had limited outcomes data details, and both utilized blends that contained other compounds that could themselves be the drivers of the VMS effects seen. No harms were noted. Strength of evidence was Low, and balance of harms and benefits could not be well ascertained.

##### _Red Clover_

Red clover is a member of the legume family that contains isoflavone compounds and in various cultures has been used for menstrual and menopausal concerns.(140) The Work Group reviewed two SRs. One, Kanadys et al. 2021 (127), examined the impact of red clover on VMS, and findings were mixed, with a decrease in hot flush daily frequency in seven RCTs looking at postmenopausal women, but without a significant difference on the same measure in the metaanalysis of three RCTs that included both perimenopausal and postmenopausal women. In the same SR, there was improvement in the Kupperman Index in three RCTs, but not in the Greene Climacteric Scale (GCS) in six RCTs. A second SR (141) looked at significant adverse events across four RCTs and found no difference between intervention and placebo groups, and no serious adverse events (SAEs) were reported with Red Clover use. Many of the RCTs included were funded by makers of red clover supplements.

##### _Magnesium_

Magnesium is a mineral relevant for multiple body functions, and various formulations of magnesium supplements are available over the counter marketed for various conditions including sleep disturbance. The Work Group reviewed one RCT (142) focusing on magnesium oxide for VMS in postmenopausal women with a history of breast cancer. The strength of evidence was rated Low. The small RCT (n=184) tested 800 mg and 1200 mg doses and assessed both hot flush frequency and severity at eight weeks for both doses and found no benefit in reduction of VMS vs placebo, with a noted side effect of diarrhea in the intervention group.

##### _Melatonin_

Melatonin is a hormone produced in the brain that can impact circadian rhythms.(143) The Work Group reviewed one SR (144) that attempted to review RCTs looking at melatonin supplementation of varying doses, both alone and combined with other supplements, as an intervention for menopausal VMS and sleep quality. One RCT from Iran contributed 75% of the participants to the meta-analysis of four RCTs. No difference between melatonin and placebo was noted for both VMS and sleep quality, and the overall strength of evidence was rated very Low.

##### _Other Supplements_

The Work Group reviewed literature on a number of other supplements including: amino acids (145), giant fennel (146), fenugreek (147), maca (148,149), probiotics (150), salvia (126), succinate (amberen) (151,152) as well as two herbal mixtures, danggwijagyaksan (153), and kamishoyosan (154). The literature base for these supplements was characterized by small sample size, Low quality for reasons including unclear recruitment, baseline differences, no reference to allocation concealment and unclear blinding, as well as a lack of clarity on the use of intention-to-treat analyses. Several of these small studies were sponsored by a manufacturer of the supplement under study. The literature included minimal information on harm, although in some instances, including giant fennel (bleeding,(155)) and kamoyishan (liver damage,(156)) there are case reports of adverse effects associated with use.

For all supplements reviewed, the strength of evidence reviewed by the Work Group was rated overall Very low due to multiple factors including limited generalizability due to use of varied formulations, doses and proprietary mixes, study populations from outside the U.S. with very strict eligibility criteria not applicable to U.S. populations, limited to no data on study concealment methodology, and almost no reporting on adverse events. Most studies demonstrated unclear to no benefit. For all of these reasons, the Work Group decided on an insufficient to recommend for or against recommendation.

The Work Group did consider other factors in addition to the literature reviewed. Patients in the VA and DOD focus group did note a preference for holistic management of menopause. For some patients, this may include a desire to use herbal or nutraceutical interventions to manage VMS. Other patients may be reluctant to use unregulated supplements with unclear benefits and harms. Clinicians counseling patients on menopausal symptoms and available treatments should thoughtfully solicit patient concerns and share information regarding the evidence basis for available menopause therapies. This discussion should include the robust evidence basis for prescription menopause pharmacotherapies and the paucity of high-quality evidence for the benefits and harms of most nutraceuticals. Furthermore, supplements may not be a covered benefit and may result in substantial out-of-pocket cost to patients. Additionally, many of the supplements reviewed were studied in other countries and may not be commercially available in the US, limiting applicability to VA and DOD populations. Many of the reviewed studies also included stringent exclusion criteria, excluding patients with many common comorbidities, including mental health diagnoses, which further limits the generalizability to VA and DOD populations. These factors contributed to the Work Group’s decision for insufficient evidence to recommend for or against the reviewed supplements.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation, including 23 studies (125-127,131-133,135,136,138,139,141,142,144-154), seven of which were SRs and 16 individual RCTs. The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Low. The body of evidence had some limitations, including small sample size and confounders in the analysis. Given the limitations of the evidence base, the Work Group was not able to effectively assess the balance of benefits and harms regarding the use of supplements for menopausal VMS and, in many cases, to ascertain a standard dose or formulation. Additionally, patient preferences for using such supplements with limited clarity on benefits and harms would also vary. Some patients seek out complementary and alternative treatment options for menopausal symptoms, and some may be interested in these supplements that are available over the counter and not FDAregulated, despite the lack of quality evidence due to inconsistent dosing, outcome description, dosing, and study design. Other patients may be reluctant to consider unregulated supplements with unclear evidence basis, especially when other prescription therapeutic options with a wellestablished evidence base are available. For all of these reasons, the Work Group decided upon a _Neither for nor against_ recommendation.

#### **_b. Sleep Symptoms_**

##### **_Recommendation_**

13. There is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against menopausal hormone therapy used solely for sleep symptoms. **(Neither for nor against | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

Sleep disturbance is one of the distinctive features of menopausal symptoms. Self-reported sleep problems are reported to increase by 2 to 3.5 times in women during the menopausal transition.(157) Sleep is essential for optimal health and functioning. Persistent sleep disturbances across menopause can negatively impact quality of life, including daytime functioning and productivity, and increase the risk for mental and physical health conditions.(158) The pathophysiology of menopause-related sleep disturbance appears to be complex. In particular, VMS such as hot flashes are known to adversely affect sleep quality and severe hot flashes are strongly associated with chronic insomnia in menopausal women.(159) Patients and providers are eager to find ways to improve sleep efficiency as long-term sleep disturbances in menopausal women are closely related to cardiovascular and metabolic disorders, as well as cognitive impairment.(157)

Overall, the evidence comparing MHT to placebo for improving sleep efficiency was limited to one SR containing five RCTs. The strength of evidence was Low. The evidence from the literature review found no difference in sleep efficiency measured by polysomnography. Sleep quality, assessed by subjective self-reported questionnaires, indicated improvement in the MHT group compared to the placebo group. Several MHTs were evaluated, including estrogen and estrogenprogestogen (157), as well as two of the more recently developed SERMs, raloxifene and BZA.(159) Additionally, a subgroup analysis favored MHT, in particular 17β-estradiol (E2) and CEE for estrogen as well as MP and MPA for progestogen. The results indicated that oral and transdermal regimens positively impacted sleep disturbance to some extent, and transdermal administration of both 17β-estradiol combined with MP for at least 6 months in menopausal women with sleep disturbance was more effective at improving sleep quality (important outcome). Future research priorities would benefit from more comparative effectiveness studies.

After discussing possible benefits in sleep quality but not efficiency (critical outcome) and how improvement in sleep quality can impact cardiovascular health as well as metabolic disorders, cognitive and immune function, the Work Group determined that the benefits and harms/burden were balanced. This determination was made after discussing usual harms for MHT, including but not limited to vaginal bleeding and breast tenderness.

In addition, patients with other indications for MHT, such as moderate to severe VMS, may wish to take MHT as it is associated with improvements in sleep quality, likely mediated through more effective control of nocturnal VMS. MHT may indirectly improve sleep in menopausal women when VMS are present, as a reduction in nocturnal hot flashes and night sweats can decrease nighttime awakening. See Recommendation 2 regarding MHT use for VMS.

Sleep disturbance during the menopausal transition is typically not an isolated symptom. Rather, it commonly occurs as part of a broader and more complex cluster of VMS associated with menopause. As such, sleep disturbance in this context is often secondary to, or exacerbated by, underlying VMS rather than representing a standalone sleep disorder. MHT may be beneficial for appropriately selected women, particularly where VMS are a primary driver of sleep disruption, by improving symptom control and thereby contributing to improved sleep quality.

Current practice guidelines recommend CBT as a low-risk treatment to manage VMS that can significantly impact sleep1 , functioning, and overall quality of life. See Recommendation 6 regarding CBT to manage VMS.

The Work Group recognizes that there is some variation in patient values and preferences. This includes patient willingness to take MHT without strong evidence to support benefits in sleep efficiency and also patient willingness to trial MHT due to potential side effects or personal beliefs. In terms of other implications, the Work Group looked at feasibility and resource use, including availability in VA/DOD formulary and cost.

The Work Group reviewed evidence related to this recommendation, and only two SRs that compared various MHTs to placebo for improving sleep symptoms due to menopause were identified. Pan et al. 2022 (157) was identified as the main source of evidence; however, it did not include the SERMs, so a second earlier network meta-analysis was solely reviewed to include the effects for the SERMs, BZA, and raloxifene (159) therefore there is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against the use of menopausal hormonal therapy solely for sleep symptoms.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(157,159) Therefore, it is categorized as Reviewed, New-added. The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Low. The body of evidence had some limitations, including small sample size and wide CIs.(157) The benefits of the use of MHT solely for sleep symptoms were balanced with the potential harms. Patient values and preferences varied somewhat because some patients may not be willing to take MHT without strong evidence to support the benefits in sleep efficiency and potential side effects or personal beliefs. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Neither for nor against_ recommendation.

#### **_c. Depressive and Anxiety Symptoms_**

**Introduction to 2026 Menopause Recommendations for Depressive and Anxiety Symptoms**

Perimenopause is a high-risk time for the development of depressive and anxiety symptoms. Compared to pre-menopause, women in perimenopause are about twice as likely to experience depressive symptoms, and symptoms are more severe on average.(160) The risk of new-onset anxiety symptoms or anxiety disorder increases.(87) Depression during perimenopause correlates with increased variability of estrogen, FSH, and luteinizing hormone (LH), but not with absolute

levels.(161) Other influences on perimenopausal mental health include physical activity, chronic pain, comorbid medical illnesses, midlife stressors and role transitions, and social support.(162,163)

During perimenopause and menopause, physical and mental health conditions interact and need coordinated assessment and treatment. Depressive and anxiety symptoms, VMS, reduced physical activity, sleep disturbance, and sexual functioning influence one another.(164-167) Improvements in one domain can foster improvements in others.

##### **_Recommendation_**

14. We recommend cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) to treat depressive and/or anxiety symptoms during perimenopause and menopause. **(Strong for | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

CBT is a brief, structured psychotherapy that helps people strengthen adaptive thought patterns and behaviors. Some versions of CBT specifically target menopausal symptoms, including depression, anxiety, VMS, sleep disturbances, sexual dysfunction, and cognitive changes. Women going through the menopausal transition often experience adverse physical changes and mood disturbances such as anxiety and depressive symptoms that can significantly impact functioning and overall quality of life. CBT has been proposed as a low-risk treatment for menopausal symptoms to be used as a stand-alone or an adjunct to hormonal therapy.

The SR covered a variety of CBT protocols, including but not limited to CBT-Meno. As there were no direct comparison studies of CBT-Meno and other forms of CBT, the recommendation encompasses all forms of CBT. There is evidence of improvement in menopause-related depressive and anxiety symptoms with CBT over a waitlist control group.

The overall strength of evidence is Moderate. The SR, which included eight RCTs (104), was determined to be of fair quality based on risk of bias. Approximately 90% of the RCTs were judged to be at high risk of performance bias. The quality of the included study(105) is fair, as the study was not powered for beliefs outcomes.

The benefits of CBT outweigh the potential harm. Treatment burden, or the time and effort the patient would need to invest in CBT, would be the only potential harm.

Patient values and preferences regarding CBT for menopausal symptoms showed a large variation. While some patients value nonpharmacologic interventions, others preferred pharmacologic options. Some participants cited challenges with access to CBT for menopausal symptoms. Focus group participants disliked care fragmentation, and CBT integrates treatment of multiple menopausal symptoms in one intervention.

Although CBT is considered an acceptable treatment by providers, limited availability of CBT therapy for menopause may be a limiting factor in offering this treatment modality to patients.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation in Ye et al. 2022 (104) and Donegan et al. 2022.(105) Therefore, it is categorized as Reviewed, New-added. The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Moderate. The benefits of CBT to treat depressive and/or anxiety symptoms related to menopause outweighed the potential harm, which only includes the treatment burden. Patient values and preferences varied largely, because overall, participants valued nonpharmacological interventions for the management of their menopausal symptoms but cited challenges with access. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Strong for_ recommendation.

#### **_d. Cognitive Symptoms and Dementia_**

##### **Introduction to 2026 Menopause Recommendations for the Management of Cognitive Symptoms and Dementia**

During the menopause transition, up to two-thirds of women will experience cognitive symptoms, including memory problems and/or attention and language deficits.(177) Women will often describe these symptoms as forgetfulness, difficulty with word finding, or a general sense of “brain fog.” While these symptoms are typically transient during the menopause transition, they can be very distressing to individuals experiencing them.

Dementia is a disease of substantial public health significance, with recent studies demonstrating that women have an approximate 48% lifetime risk of developing dementia (compared to 35% for men).(178) While substantial biologic evidence supports the importance of estrogen to cognitive function and there is mounting observational evidence that MHT initiated in midlife (within five years of menopause), the role of menopausal hormone therapy in the management and/or prevention of cognitive symptoms and/or dementia has not yet been established.(179)

##### **_Recommendation_**

19. There is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against menopausal hormonal therapy (MHT) used solely for the prevention of dementia. **(Neither for nor against | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

Our evidence review identified one SR (97) which examined the evidence addressing the impact of MHT, both estrogen-progestogen and estrogen monotherapy, on the prevention of dementia in perimenopausal and menopausal women. Most RCTs examined in this SR did not demonstrate a benefit to the prevention of dementia with the use of MHT (compared to placebo). One study, however, favored the use of estrogen monotherapy (over placebo) in the prevention of Alzheimer’s or other dementia related mortality in the post intervention follow up (mean of 10.8 years from onset of therapy).

The overall lack of evidence supporting direct benefit in the prevention of dementia with the use of MHT, compared with the standard, potential harms of MHT initially led to the consideration of a suggestion against the use of MHT solely for the prevention of dementia. However, one study comparing 5,310 women using estrogen monotherapy to 5,429 women on placebo showed the potential benefit of estrogen monotherapy on dementia associated mortality when evaluated at a mean of 10.8 years after initiation of therapy (post-intervention follow-up, Hazard Ratio [HR]: 0.73; 95% CI: 0.58 to 0.92; p=0.008). For this reason, the final recommendation regarding MHT for the prevention of dementia is that there is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against MHT

solely for the prevention of dementia. Patients value taking medications that clearly demonstrate efficacy, and given the majority of evidence demonstrating no difference between MHT and placebo, would direct patients away from MHT solely for prevention of dementia. However, there is a paucity of other options for the prevention of dementia, and it has a significant impact on future quality of life. These factors, along with a single study demonstrating benefit to estrogen monotherapy, may lead patients to consider MHT if they have significant concerns regarding prevention of dementia related mortality.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation (97). Therefore, it is categorized as Reviewed, New-added. The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Low. The body of evidence had some limitations, including studies with large CIs and one in which no events were recorded, impacting the precision of the results. The benefits of utilizing MHT for the prevention of dementia were balanced with the potential harms (standard harms) associated with this treatment. Patient values and preferences demonstrated some variation because patients desire to use therapies that clearly demonstrate efficacy; however, there are very limited options for prevention of dementia or dementia related mortality, which can have a significant impact on future quality of life. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Neither for nor against_ recommendation for the use of MHT solely for the prevention of dementia.

#### **_e. Genitourinary Syndrome of Menopause (GSM) and/or Sexual Dysfunction Symptoms_**

##### **Introduction to 2026 Menopause Recommendations for the Management of GSM and Sexual Dysfunction**

For most midlife women (67%), sex remains moderately to extremely important, and they wish to remain sexually active.(183) However, this period is often marked by a decline in sexual function. Significant hormonal shifts occur alongside a higher incidence of comorbid conditions (such as obesity, hypertension, diabetes, anxiety, and depression), increased medication use, genital atrophy, and an evolving personal identity, all of which can impact sexuality. There is an increased prevalence of sexual dysfunction during and after the menopausal transition, with over half of women acknowledging at least one sexual concern, though these issues are often underreported to healthcare providers.(184,185) Despite these challenges, most women between the ages of 57

and 64 remain sexually active, with one-third engaging in partnered sexual activity at least annually.(186)

Sexual dysfunction is a complex condition affected by psychosocial, hormonal, physiological, and anatomic factors.(184,187) The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM)-5 classifies distressing sexual symptoms related to interest, arousal, orgasm, or pain into specific disorders, such as female sexual interest/arousal disorder (FSIAD), female orgasmic disorder, and genito-pelvic pain/penetration disorder, among others.(187,188)

Clinicians, especially OBGYNs, should routinely screen for sexual dysfunction during well-woman visits, with special attention paid to menopausal women.(184) The initial evaluation for sexual concerns involves a comprehensive history, including medication review and physical examination.(187) Laboratory evaluation is reserved for patients with a suspected medical etiology.(187) Hormone concentration studies (i.e., FSH, estradiol, testosterone) are not recommended in menopausal patients, especially as serum levels do not reliably correlate with sexual function in menopausal women.(184)

Treating sexual dysfunction involves a multidisciplinary approach that may include lifestyle interventions, counseling, sex therapy, physical therapy, and/or medication. Nonpharmacologic options are often favored as first-line therapy. Psychological interventions, including sexual skills training, CBT, mindfulness-based interventions, and couples therapy, are a mainstay of treatment and can address symptoms related to interest, arousal, pain, and/or orgasm. For patients with myofascial pain or pelvic organ prolapse, pelvic floor physical therapy is also an effective option.

While these treatment modalities can be employed in women of all ages, it is critical for clinicians to understand specific considerations for the treatment of sexual dysfunction in menopausal women. Pain during intercourse (dyspareunia) is a frequent complaint, often related to hypoestrogenic changes like tissue thinning, loss of vaginal elasticity, reduced lubrication, and musculoskeletal issues.(186,187) Repeated associations between intercourse and painful stimuli can cause avoidance, situational anxiety, diminished arousal, and increased musculoskeletal tension.(185,189) Therefore, treating dyspareunia associated with genitourinary syndrome of menopause should be a priority, either as first-line or alongside other interventions.

The following recommendations (Recommendations 21 and 26-27) review pharmacologic interventions for the treatment of sexual dysfunction during menopause. The broader treatment of genitourinary syndrome of menopause is discussed in Recommendations 22-25 and 28-29.

##### **_Recommendation_**

21. We suggest local (non-systemic) vaginal and/or systemic estrogen therapy, for the management of sexual dysfunction in menopause, especially related to vaginal dryness and dyspareunia.

##### **(Weak for | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

The evidence review identified three SRs (115,190,191) that indicate that both local vaginal and systemic estrogen improve various measurements of sexual function for postmenopausal women. Evidence from 30 RCTs across these reviews confirms that estrogen provides a benefit over

placebo for painful intercourse for as long as 260 weeks. The reviews also show that estrogen improves global measures of sexual function, sexual satisfaction, and lubrication for up to 52 weeks.

In contrast, the impact of estrogen therapy on measures less directly related to the genital tract was mixed. For instance, studies on arousal and orgasm did not demonstrate improvement compared to placebo.(190,191) Similarly, evidence from 18 RCTs across all three reviews showed mixed findings for the effect of estrogen on sexual desire and interest.(115,190,191)

Systemic estrogen therapy carries several potential harms (See Sidebar 2), whereas local estrogen has minimal systemic absorption and thus offers a superior safety profile regarding cancer and cardiovascular risk. While all three reviews evaluated local (vaginal) and systemic (oral or transdermal) estrogen, only Meziou et al. 2023 (191) directly compared the two routes’ effects on sexual function, finding no significant difference. The study authors acknowledge that this analysis was limited by study design and was not suited for a head-to-head comparison. It is worth noting that local vaginal estrogen can be prescribed alone or co-prescribed with systemic estrogen. For isolated genitourinary concerns, local vaginal estrogen is the preferred first-line therapy given its lower risk profile. In patients with both genitourinary concerns and systemic menopausal symptoms, local vaginal estrogen is a safe, effective addition to systemic estrogen MHT.

The reviewed studies exhibited significant heterogeneity, making it difficult to determine the precise mechanism by which estrogen improves sexual function. The RCTs included a diverse population of postmenopausal women, including those with and without baseline genitourinary concerns. (190,191) Furthermore, the SRs collectively evaluated treatment with estrogen that was administered in a local and systemic fashion. (190,191) This complicates efforts to determine whether the intervention benefits all women with sexual concerns or primarily those with genitourinary issues.

In addition to estrogen, Lara et al. 2023 (190) and Meziou et al. 2023 (191) also assessed SERMs for the treatment of sexual dysfunction in menopause. As a class, there was not sufficient data to support their use. Assessing these medications as a group is challenging due to their distinct mechanisms and tissue-specific binding patterns. For example, evidence from three RCTs suggests ospemifene, an estrogen agonist in vaginal tissue, improves painful intercourse in women with GSM. However, other SERMs like BZA, a tissue-selective estrogen complex dosed with CE, and raloxifene, an estrogen antagonist in the vaginal tissue, have not demonstrated a consistent positive vaginal effect.

In postmenopausal women, hypoestrogenic changes within the genital tract directly impact sexual function. While systemic changes in gonadotropins, estrogens, and androgens throughout the menopausal transition may also impact sexual interest, these relationships are less well understood. Estrogen is the standard of care for treating GSM and its related sexual symptoms, as it locally improves lubrication, sensation, and blood flow. Addressing sexual health concerns is an important part of holistic menopause care, a priority identified by focus group members.

Local vaginal estrogen can be initiated for menopausal women of any age and continued indefinitely, whereas systemic therapy requires consideration of a patient’s age, comorbidities, and time since menopause onset (see Module B). Practical factors, such as a patient's mobility and dexterity, may pose limitations to using certain vaginal estrogen formulations like creams, tablets, or rings. Local estrogen may serve as monotherapy or be combined with systemic estrogen when genitourinary symptoms are not adequately improved. Patients often prefer systemic therapy when it addresses multiple concerns simultaneously, such as vasomotor and sexual symptoms. However, some patients may prefer to avoid hormonal therapies and focus instead on psychological interventions and physical therapy.

Service members and Veterans with sexual dysfunction and a history of sexual trauma, mood disorders, complex pelvic pain, and/or relationship discord may still derive benefit from hormonal treatment to target GSM-related sexual concerns. However, these patients will likely require multidisciplinary adjuncts to pharmacologic treatment incorporating behavioral health interventions, physical therapy, and/or gynecologic assessment.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(115,190,191) Therefore, it is categorized as Reviewed, New-added. The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Low. The body of evidence included a large composite sample size; however, the evidence was downgraded for study quality (very serious limitations) and serious inconsistency (for sexual function composite score). The benefits of local estrogen therapy, including improved global sexual function, satisfaction, lubrication, and pain, outweigh the potential risk of local therapy, which is small. Conversely, the risks of systemic estrogen may not outweigh the benefit when compared to local treatment solely for this indication. Patient values and preferences varied somewhat because many patients may be prescribed systemic estrogen for various indications during the menopausal transition, while others may prefer to limit systemic hormone exposure and use local therapy, which may be safely used in menopause at any age. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

##### **_Recommendation_**

22. For genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM), we suggest treatment with local (nonsystemic) vaginal estrogen. **(Weak for | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

GSM is a group of symptoms that can include vaginal dryness, vaginal burning, dyspareunia, urinary frequency, urinary incontinence, and/or recurrent urinary tract infections (rUTIs).(192) These symptoms can affect the vagina, vulva, urethra, and/or bladder and can be with or without sexual dysfunction.(193) Evidence from one RCT and seven SRs composed of a total of 55 RCTs indicates that local vaginal estrogen treatment provides statistically significant improvement in all aspects of GSM, including vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, vulvovaginal pruritus, and rUTIs.(115,190-192,194-196) In addition, on physical exam, patients showed objective signs of clinical improvement.(192) Local vaginal estrogen has relatively low harms due to its localized effects and potential great benefit due to improvement in quality of life, sexual health, and

potentially reduced urgent care and emergency room visits due to rUTIs.(196) Lethaby et al. 2016 (192) showed that all forms of vaginal estrogen, compared with placebo, improved all components of GSM.

When selecting a local vaginal estrogen formulation, it is important to consider patient values and preferences. Several safe and effective options are available:

- Low-dose vaginal estrogen ring (2 mg of estradiol delivering 7.5 mcg of estradiol per day): Remains in place for three months, offering convenience and consistent relief.

- Estradiol tablets: Easy to use and well-tolerated.

- Estradiol creams and conjugates estrogen: Provides flexible dosing and application tailored to individual needs. Allows patients to place vaginally but also apply locally to vulvar, urethral, and/or clitoral areas.

All of these therapies are FDA-approved and have been shown to improve GSM symptoms significantly. The choice of therapy should be guided by the patient’s lifestyle, preferences, and specific needs.

Local vaginal estrogen creams and tablets are generally more cost-effective. Patients with dexterity or mobility issues or a history of trauma might prefer a vaginal ring, as it requires less frequent insertions. Evidence supports the use of vaginal estrogen in patients with a history of breast cancer due to its localized effects (see Recommendation 23).(197)

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(115,190- 192,194-197) Therefore, it is categorized as Reviewed, New-added. The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low. The body of evidence had some limitations, including serious inconsistency and imprecision across studies, varied inclusion/exclusion criteria, high heterogeneity of findings, and effect size was not always clearly stated. The benefits of local vaginal estrogen on GSM symptoms outweighed the potential harm of adverse events, which was small. Patient values and preferences were similar in favoring effective, lower-risk, localized treatment despite some minor variation in preference for rings or vaginal tablets over cream due to the messiness of application. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

##### **_Recommendation_**

23. For breast cancer survivors, we suggest local (non-systemic) vaginal estrogen therapy for the treatment of genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM).

 - **(Weak for | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

Breast cancer is the most common cancer among American women, with approximately 300,000 women being diagnosed annually.(198) The five-year survival rate for early-stage breast cancer (Stages one and two) is an encouraging 99%, and there are currently 3.8 million breast cancer survivors in the United States. Notably, breast cancer survivors account for four in 10 female cancer survivors in the U.S.(198) While survival rates are improving, breast cancer treatment often leads to premature menopause or menopausal side effects, which can cause significant

sexual dysfunction, dyspareunia, vaginal dryness, and bladder concerns. These symptoms, collectively referred to as GSM, are further exacerbated by chemotherapy and adjuvant therapies such as AIs. These treatments significantly reduce estrogen levels, leading to profound GSM symptoms that can severely impact the quality of life for breast cancer survivors.

The Work Group reviewed the literature on the use of local vaginal estrogen to treat GSM symptoms in breast cancer survivors. Unfortunately, no RCTs were identified. The evidence team reviewed one SR of observational studies, which included six studies examining breast cancer recurrence and four studies assessing breast cancer mortality.(197)

The findings showed no difference in breast cancer recurrence or mortality among breast cancer survivors who used local vaginal estrogen, with follow-up periods of up to nine years. However, the strength of evidence was considered Very low due to several limitations. All studies were observational, potentially contributing to bias. The studies did not stratify results by receptor status, stage, or cancer treatment. There was likely a selection bias, as healthier women with lower-stage breast cancer were more likely to seek assistance for sexual activity and vaginal health.

Despite the limitations, there was no evidence of increased harm, and local vaginal estrogen was shown to improve GSM symptoms significantly (see Recommendations 23-26 and 29-30 on GSM treatment).

The Work Group noted that patient preference for local vaginal estrogen is likely variable, with some patients feeling comfortable using it while others are afraid of using any estrogen products despite their safety profile. Studies have shown that maintaining sexual activity is an important factor in quality of life for cancer survivors.(199) Provider and patient acceptability remains a challenge. Providers may lack understanding that vaginal estrogen has minimal systemic absorption, which may contribute to hesitancy in prescribing it. The removal of the FDA black box warning for local vaginal estrogen preparations in 2025 may improve provider confidence and acceptability.

The Work Group emphasizes the importance of shared decision-making when managing sexual dysfunction and GSM in breast cancer survivors. Recommendations should be tailored to the patient’s unique situation. Local vaginal estrogen appears to be a safe and effective option for managing GSM symptoms, with minimal risk of harm. Educating providers on the safety of local vaginal estrogen and addressing patient preferences will be critical to improving care for this population. Any of the previously mentioned local vaginal estradiol treatment options in Recommendation 23 would be considered reasonable options in breast cancer survivors.

Effective management of GSM symptoms can restore comfort, enhance sexual health, and reduce bladder-related issues, empowering survivors to regain a sense of normalcy and wellbeing. By providing safe and individualized treatment options, healthcare providers can help breast cancer survivors navigate the challenges of GSM and improve their physical and emotional health. See Breast Cancer Risk for more.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(197) Therefore, it is categorized as Reviewed, New-added. The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low for support for local vaginal estrogen. The body of evidence had several limitations, most notably the lack of RCTs. The observational studies that we did find included a heterogeneous population of breast cancer survivors that had varied stages, hormone status, and treatments. In addition, there was likely a healthy patient bias included in the studies. The benefits of managing GSM outweighed the potential harm of breast cancer recurrence and mortality, as no outcome differences were noted. Patient values and preferences varied only slightly as local vaginal estrogen is considered nonsystemic and no harm was seen. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

##### **_Recommendation_**

24. For dyspareunia, we suggest local (non-systemic) vaginal estrogen and/or vaginal dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA). **(Weak for | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

Perimenopausal and postmenopausal women are at an increased risk for female sexual dysfunction due to menopausal symptoms, including GSM and dyspareunia.(200) Dyspareunia can have multiple causes, often necessitating an interdisciplinary approach for proper evaluation.(200) The evidence review showed statistically significant improvement in hormonally mediated dyspareunia with the use of local vaginal estrogen or vaginal DHEA.(192,201) Since vaginal DHEA is more expensive and requires daily application, it is generally recommended to first try a course of vaginal estrogen. Additionally, for Service members stationed overseas, accessing vaginal DHEA may be more challenging at the time of publication. Both vaginal estrogen and vaginal DHEA present relatively low risks due to their localized effects and offer substantial benefits in enhancing sexual health. Local vaginal estrogen and vaginal DHEA are effective treatment options that are accessible to patients through the VA/DOD formulary.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(192,194,196,200-202) Therefore, it is categorized as Reviewed, New-added. The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low. The body of evidence had some limitations, including serious inconsistency and imprecision across studies, and the effect size was not always clearly stated.(192,194,201,202) The benefits of local vaginal estrogen and DHEA on dyspareunia outweighed the potential harm of adverse events. Patient values and preferences for vaginal estrogen and DHEA were varied due to DHEA accessibility and desire for a low-maintenance treatment plan. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

##### **_Recommendation_**

25. For vaginal dryness, we suggest local (non-systemic) vaginal estrogen, vaginal DHEA, vaginal moisturizers, or ospemifene.

**(Weak for | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

Evidence from one SR composed of four RCTs favored vaginal moisturizers compared to placebo for improving vaginal dryness.(201) There were two trials in the SR that showed hyaluronic acid and lactic acid vaginal moisturizers improved vaginal dryness over placebo.(201) Another SR composed of six RCTs, with moderate strength of evidence, favored ospemifene compared to placebo for improvement in vaginal dryness.(203) One SR showed that four of seven RCTs favored vaginal estrogen over placebo for vaginal dryness; however, a second SR with a metaanalysis composed of four RCTs showed no difference between local vaginal estrogen and placebo.(194,201) One SR composed of four RCTs all showed statistical significance when using vaginal DHEA over placebo for vaginal dryness.(201) There were no comparative effectiveness studies for the treatment of vaginal dryness in the evidence review.

In the evidence reviewed, there were no direct comparative studies between vaginal estrogen, vaginal DHEA, vaginal moisturizers, or ospemifene. Clinical context and patient preferences are important to consider when identifying which of these treatment options to try. Lower-risk and lower-cost agents may be preferable, such as local vaginal estrogen, vaginal DHEA, and vaginal moisturizers, over ospemifene. Subsequently, if a patient does not have an adequate response and is bothered by the symptoms, a higher-cost and/or risk option may be trialed. For example, in patients who have a history of thromboembolism or who are at high-risk for thromboembolism, a trial of ospemifene may not be appropriate. In contrast, local vaginal estrogen, vaginal DHEA, and vaginal moisturizers all have a low risk of harm and, due to their localized effects, can be used in most patients.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(194,201,203) Therefore, it is categorized as Reviewed, New-added. The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low. The body of evidence had some limitations, including high heterogeneity in the Ali et. al 2024 meta-analysis, inconsistent findings, and unclear effect size.(194,201,203) Specifically with local vaginal estrogen, variations between the studies included hormone types (estriol and estradiol), hormone formulations (rings, gels, creams, or tablets), dosages (0.005% ultra-low dose estriol versus conventional U.S. dosages of vaginal estradiol), and varied intervention length (follow-up ranged from 8 weeks to 4.1 years), which contributed to the Very low quality of evidence. The benefits of local vaginal estrogen, vaginal DHEA, vaginal moisturizers, or ospemifene slightly outweighed the potential harm. Patient values and preferences varied somewhat because some patients may prefer non-oral compared to oral medications. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

##### **_Recommendation_**

26. We suggest transdermal testosterone for the treatment of sexual dysfunction in menopause, especially related to interest, desire, and/or arousal. **(Weak for | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

Few pharmacologic therapies are effective in treating female sexual dysfunction related to interest, desire, and arousal, yet patients avidly seek treatment for these concerns. Testosterone

is known to modulate sexual behavior and has shown efficacy in addressing these symptoms in select populations.

The evidence review identified two SRs (204,205) that addressed the impact of testosterone on sexual dysfunction in postmenopausal women. Most studies focused on postmenopausal women with Hypoactive Sexual Desire Disorder (HSDD, reclassified in the DSM-5 as FSIAD), though some of the RCTs in Islam et al. 2019 (204) also evaluated more diverse populations, including those without HSDD and premenopausal women. Both reviews assessed systemic testosterone doses that approximate physiological testosterone concentrations for premenopausal women with and without estrogen co-treatment. The findings should not be generalized to injectables, pellets, or other compounded preparations, as these formulations were not evaluated. A positive treatment effect was noted in postmenopausal women with sexual concerns both with and without a diagnosis of HSDD. Studies in premenopausal women were limited and demonstrated minimal significant treatment effect. No literature was identified specifically addressing treatment in perimenopausal women.

Among postmenopausal women with HSDD, transdermal testosterone was superior to placebo at 24 weeks for improving sexual desire, orgasm, the number of satisfying sexual episodes, and personal distress at 24 weeks.(205) Similarly, Islam et al. 2019 (204) found that testosterone provided benefits over placebo for most sexual function outcomes, including satisfying sexual events, sexual desire, arousal, orgasm, pleasure, sexual self-image, and sexual distress, for up to 52 weeks in postmenopausal women. It is noteworthy that the average increase in satisfying sexual experiences was less than one per month (Achilli: MD 0.92, 95% CI: 0.65, 1.19, P<.00001; Islam: MD 0.85, 95% CI: 0.52, 1.18, p=0.014).(204,205)

The primary harms identified were acne (Islam: Risk Ratio (RR): 1.46, 95% CI: 1.11 to 1.92; Achilli: RR 1.41, 95% CI: 1.05 to 1.88) and increased hair growth (Achilli: RR: 1.56, 95% CI: 1.17 to 2.09).(204,205) Importantly, no significant difference was found between testosterone and placebo regarding the risk of vocal changes, alopecia, clitoromegaly, breast cancer, deep vein thrombosis, stroke, or MI in studies lasting up to 52 months.(204,205)

Transdermal testosterone, in doses that approximate physiologic premenopausal levels, is recognized by the Menopause Society, ACOG, and other professional societies as an effective treatment for postmenopausal sexual interest and arousal disorders.(187,206,207) This consensus has improved its acceptability among providers. However, hesitancy in prescribing persists due to its modest benefit and the lack of long-term safety data. Regardless, testosterone is a highly publicized treatment that patients are eager to understand and obtain, whether from licensed healthcare professionals or alternative providers.

Prescribing testosterone to women also presents significant practical challenges. It is not FDAapproved for this indication, and female-dosed preparations are unavailable in the U.S. This necessitates the off-label use of male formulations in reduced dosage, which requires explicit patient counseling and consent. To ensure dosing remains within the physiological range, it is recommended to follow serum total testosterone concentrations at six-month intervals and discontinue treatment if a trial period shows no favorable response.(187,206) Furthermore, common side effects like acne and hair growth can be a deterrent for patients. See Appendix H for

testosterone dosing. Despite these barriers, testosterone is one of the few treatments that consistently improves sexual arousal/desire for menopausal women. Although the benefits are modest, it can be an appropriate treatment for a well-counseled patient. Pharmacotherapy for sexual dysfunction should always be considered alongside psychological interventions, such as sexual skills training, cognitive-behavioral therapy, mindfulness-based interventions, and couples therapy.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(204,205) Therefore, it is categorized as Reviewed, New-added. The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Low. The body of evidence had some limitations including population and intervention heterogeneity, and both reviews were downgraded for study quality (very serious limitations). The benefits of testosterone (including improved sexual desire, pleasure, orgasm, number of satisfying sexual episodes, and personal distress) slightly outweighed the potential harms to include acne, hair growth, and the unknown risks of long-term use. Patient values and preferences varied somewhat because of modest impact of treatment with unknown risk, however, there are few alternatives for a distressing condition. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

##### **_Recommendation_**

27. For the treatment of Hypoactive Sexual Desire Disorder (reclassified in the DSM-5 as Female Sexual Interest/Arousal Disorder) in perimenopause and menopause, we suggest flibanserin.

**(Weak for | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

The evidence review identified one SR of two RCTs (n=1585) evaluating flibanserin for the treatment of HSDD in postmenopausal women.(208) The DSM-IV diagnosis of HSDD has been replaced by FSIAD in the DSM-5; however, we use HSDD here to acknowledge the language of the original research.

Evidence from two RCTs indicated that flibanserin provided benefit over placebo for most outcomes at 24 weeks, including satisfying sexual events, desire, and distress.(208) However, similar to testosterone, the mean increase in satisfying sexual events per month was small (less than one) though statistically significant (Kamrul-Hasan: MD: 0.37; 95% CI: 0.14 to 0.61; p=0.002).(208)

Adverse events, including dizziness, fatigue, nausea, somnolence, and insomnia, were more common in premenopausal and postmenopausal patients treated with flibanserin compared to placebo. There was no statistically significant difference in serious adverse events between the treatment groups.

Flibanserin was approved by the FDA in December 2025 for the treatment of HSDD in postmenopausal women under age 65, making it the first approved therapy for this group. It had previously been available for premenopausal women since 2015. Provider acceptance for postmenopausal use will likely grow with awareness of this expanded indication. Nonetheless, the high cost of flibanserin presents a barrier to equitable access. This drug is not presently on the

formulary within the VA. While the drug is included on the formulary for the DOD, the prior authorization remains limited to pre-menopausal females. In addition, the Work Group did not review bremelanotide, as it is only FDA-approved for use to treat HSDD in premenopausal women, therefore outside of the scope of this CPG.

Although flibanserin is the only non-hormonal prescription therapy for postmenopausal HSDD, its efficacy in improving satisfying sexual experiences is less than half that observed in premenopausal patients. While this improvement is marginal, other sexual function indices favor flibanserin over placebo, and the value of even a small benefit varies by patient.

However, the drug's modest benefit becomes less pronounced when considering its adverse effects. Flibanserin has a black box warning for hypotension and syncope, with risks amplified by alcohol or CYP3A4 inhibitors. This is an important concern for older adults who are at a higher risk of falls and for those unwilling to abstain from alcohol within two hours of the nightly dose. Furthermore, the high cost presents a barrier to equitable access. Efficacy has only been established for women diagnosed with HSDD.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(208) Therefore, it is categorized as Reviewed, New-added. The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Moderate. The body of evidence had some limitations, including study quality (serious limitations). The benefits of flibanserin (e.g., improved satisfying sexual events, sexual function, and distress scores) were balanced with the potential harm of adverse events such as hypotension, dizziness, nausea, and sleep disturbance. Patient values and preferences would be expected to vary largely because of variation in the degree of distress induced from HSDD and willingness to risk treatment harms for marginal benefit. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

Evidence from one SR composed of four RCTs (n=338) did not show a statistically significant difference between vaginal moisturizers and placebo for improving dyspareunia at 12 weeks.(201) Vaginal moisturizers included in the evidence review included lactic acid, polycarbophil, and hyaluronic acid gels.(201) Notably, one RCT within the SR (n=98) found that lactic acid vaginal moisturizers applied 3 times weekly improved dyspareunia compared to placebo.(201)

Through shared decision-making and based on patient preference, patients may initially prefer nonpharmacological management of dyspareunia and may wish to try over-the-counter vaginal moisturizers. ##### **_Recommendation_**

29. For genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM), we suggest against the use of vaginal lasers.

##### **(Weak against | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

Evidence from two SRs (n=5,556) and two small RCTs (n=75) indicated no statistically significant difference between vaginal laser therapy and placebo for vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, vaginal burning, dysuria, pruritus, or urinary frequency.(209-212) The laser therapies included in the evidence synthesis were carbon dioxide (CO2), mixed wavelength, and yttrium aluminum garnet (YAG) lasers.(209-212)

There was Moderate strength of evidence that showed no difference between CO2 lasers and placebo for treating vaginal burning, pruritus, or dysuria.(209) In comparison, 1 small RCT showed improvement with mixed wavelength laser therapy over placebo for dyspareunia(212), and another small RCT showed improvement with YAG laser therapy over placebo for vaginal dryness.(211) Overall, the evidence showed no significant benefit with laser therapy compared to sham laser or placebo. While no serious adverse events were reported in the reviewed evidence (209), there are known risks to vaginal laser therapy, including pain, scarring, vaginal/vulva burns, erythema, edema, tissue damage, decreased vaginal sensation, and infection.(213)

Vaginal laser therapy is not widely available for patients within the VA/DOD, and it is not FDAapproved for GSM. Laser therapy can be a burden for patients due to the number of appointments required for treatment, costs, and treatment discomfort.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(209-212) Therefore, it is categorized as Reviewed, New-added. The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low. The body of evidence had some limitations, including serious inconsistency and imprecision due to wide CIs, high heterogeneity, and small sample size.(209- 212) The harms of vaginal laser therapy outweighed the potential benefit due to potential adverse events. Patient values and preferences varied somewhat because some patients may prefer nonpharmacological treatment options. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak against_ recommendation.

#### **_f. Bone Health_**

**Introduction to 2026 Menopause Recommendations for the Management of Bone Health** Osteoporosis is particularly prevalent in older postmenopausal women and significantly increases the risk of fractures, which are often associated with substantial morbidity and mortality. Postmenopausal bone loss, primarily driven by estrogen deficiency, is a key contributor to the

development of osteoporosis. Preventing the initial bone loss that occurs during the menopausal transition is critical to reducing long-term fracture risk.

The Work Group recognizes the profound impact osteoporosis can have on women's health and quality of life. It is accepted that in women without known osteoporosis, estrogen therapy (with or without a progestogen) effectively reduces bone loss and fracture, particularly for perimenopausal women with low bone density and additional risk factors. Therefore, no recent studies on this matter were captured in the literature search.

In this section, we review the available evidence on therapies that improve bone mineral density and decrease fracture risk, providing guidance on the most effective strategies for maintaining bone health in postmenopausal women.

##### **_Recommendation_**

30. In post-menopausal women with risk factors for osteoporosis, we suggest conjugated estrogen/bazedoxifene as an option to increase bone mineral density based on patient characteristics and preferences.

**(Weak for | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

CE/BZA is FDA-approved for women with a uterus for the treatment of moderate to severe VMS. While the medication also contains an FDA approval for prevention of osteoporosis in postmenopausal patients, it is noted that when prescribing solely for the prevention of postmenopausal osteoporosis, therapy should only be considered for women at significant risk of osteoporosis, and non-estrogen medication should be carefully considered.

Gallagher et al. 2016 (214) was an SR containing two RCTs of 1,172 patients sponsored by the drug product’s manufacturer. The patient population was women at increased risk of osteoporosis, based upon a BMD score between one and 2.5 at the lumbar spine and hip and at least one additional osteoporosis risk factor. Women received either CE 0.45 mg/BZA 20 (n=381), CE 0.625 mg/BZA 20 mg (n=394), or placebo (n=397). Comparisons favored CE/BZA over placebo, and while follow-up was limited to one year, improvement in BMD was noted in the lumbar spine, total hip, and femoral neck.

The specific treatment selected to address VMS in menopause must be individualized. CE/BZA appears to be an optimal selection for women with both VMS and increased risk of osteoporosis, given the favorable impact on BMD. However, there are no direct studies of comparative effectiveness between CE/BZA and other estrogen-containing menopause hormone therapies. For women with a uterus, there may be hesitancy to receive estrogen and progestogen hormone therapy because of the potential increased risk of breast cancer. In the case of CE/BZA, the use of BZA, a selective estrogen receptor modulator with antiestrogenic effects on uterine tissue, allows for CE to be administered without the need for progestogen. Thus, CE/BZA is an attractive treatment option for women desiring menopause hormone therapy but with concerns about increased cancer risks of breast cancer from estrogen and progestogen. There were no available studies reviewed that compared CE/BZA to other common treatments for the prevention of osteoporosis, such as calcium, Vitamin D, and weight-bearing exercises. These alternate non-

pharmacologic treatments are low risk and may provide additional benefits beyond just prevention of osteoporosis. Additionally, while women looking to avoid a progestogen may be optimal candidates for CE/BZA, there are some women who benefit from progestogens in the perimenopausal and menopausal period for bleeding management and sleep benefit. It is also worth noting that this medication is non-formulary for VA, thus creating the additional burden of a prior authorization for patient use. Given the pros and cons of CE/BZA use compared to other menopausal hormone therapies and other preventative strategies for osteoporosis, the overall patient characteristics and preferences are critical in making the decision to use CE/BZA for women at risk for osteoporosis.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(214) Therefore, it is categorized as Reviewed, New-added. The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Moderate. The body of evidence had some limitations, including potential bias given sponsorship from the drug manufacturer, small sample size, and inconsistency, with slightly different study designs in the two included RCTs. The benefits of improved BMD for those at increased risk for osteoporosis outweighed the potential harm related to the administration of oral estrogen. Patient values and preferences varied largely because some may not value improvement in BMD since this is not a direct clinical outcome and because women have variable beliefs, priorities, and concerns that impact their decisions on menopause hormone therapy. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

##### **_Recommendation_**

31. In post-menopausal patients, we suggest dietary calcium and/or calcium supplementation to increase bone mineral density.

##### **(Weak for | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

The use of calcium to support overall bone health in post-menopausal patients has been a longstanding recommendation from multiple leading medical societies and endorsed by the National Osteoporosis Foundation.(215) Calcium serves as both a structural component and a signaling molecule in bone formation. Wu et al. 2017 (216) performed an SR of 17 RCTs evaluating the impact of calcium doses ranging from 250 to 2000 mg/day alone or with Vitamin D3 on the change in BMD. An improvement in BMD (1.1% change) was seen in those receiving calcium as compared to the control group (total n=2537). However, it is important to note that a recent SR and meta-analysis did not demonstrate a reduction in fracture risk with the use of calcium supplementation, though this study was not included in the reviewed evidence base.(217)

Calcium supplementation is an easily accessible, low-risk intervention. While there is the potential for decreased gastrointestinal motility resulting in increased risk of colon polyps and constipation, increased risk of nephrolithiasis, increased risk of CVD events (218), or interactions with other medications, the Work Group believed this is an intervention likely to be recognized and acceptable to both patients and clinicians. To date, since observational data from calcium supplementation trials are mixed, in patients who are at high risk of hypercalcemia or are taking ≥1,000 mg/day calcium supplementation, we suggest monitoring calcium levels.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(216) Therefore, it is categorized as Reviewed, New-added. The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Low. The body of evidence had limitations, including serious imprecision.(216) The benefits of calcium for improved BMD outweigh the potential harms, which are usually minor and manageable. Patient values and preferences are felt to be similar because of familiarity with increasing fracture risk while aging and the potential of significant long-term harms if a fracture occurs. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Weak for_ recommendation.

##### **_Recommendation_**

32. In post-menopausal patients without osteoporosis, there is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against Vitamin D3 for primary improvement of decreased bone mineral density or fracture.

**(Neither for nor against | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

Similar to calcium, the use of Vitamin D3 to support overall bone health in post-menopausal patients has been a long-standing recommendation from multiple leading medical societies and endorsed by the National Osteoporosis Foundation.(215) Unlike calcium, which has a direct role in bone mineralization and maintenance, Vitamin D in its active form increases intestinal calcium absorption and renal calcium reabsorption, thereby increasing calcium availability for bone remodeling. Kazemian et al. 2023 (219) performed an SR of twenty-three RCTs examining the impact of Vitamin D3 supplementation ranging from 300 to 10,000 IU/day on patients without known Vitamin D deficiency, osteopenia, or osteoporosis. The SR found no difference in BMD with Vitamin D supplementation as compared to placebo or no treatment.

The SR by Kahwati et al. 2018 (220) contained only a single RCT. The trial included only patients without a known history of Vitamin D deficiency, osteopenia, or osteoporosis and compared rates of hip and non-vertebral fractures in those receiving supplementation with calcium and Vitamin D3 to those receiving calcium supplementation alone. No difference in fracture rate was identified between the two groups.

Vitamin D3 is an overall low-risk intervention, with rare side effects including Vitamin D toxicity and complications from hypercalcemia such as nephrolithiasis or arrhythmias. Vitamin D3 is easily accessible with over-the-counter availability and is likely to be accepted by both clinicians and patients as a familiar and safe intervention. While there was no evidence to demonstrate Vitamin D3 as effective for the primary purpose of improving BMD in patients without Vitamin D deficiency, osteopenia, or osteoporosis, there were no SRs available for review in these higher-risk populations.

The Work Group systematically reviewed evidence related to this recommendation.(219,220) Therefore, it is categorized as Reviewed, New-added. The Work Group’s confidence in the quality of the evidence was Very low. The body of evidence had some limitations, including small sample size, with Kahwati et al 2018 (220) limited to a single RCT of only 232 patients. Kazemian et al. 2023 (219) was limited due to high risk of bias in at least one of the included studies, unclear risk of selection bias in half of the included studies, and heterogeneity in treatment with Vitamin D

dosing ranging from 300 to 10,000 IU/day across included studies. The benefits of Vitamin D3 related to BMD and fracture were balanced with the potential harms, which are rare and usually minor. Patient values and preferences were similar because patients often prefer interventions they are familiar with and perceive as low risk. Thus, the Work Group decided upon a _Neither for nor against_ recommendation.

##### **_Recommendation_**

33. For primary prevention of fracture in post-menopausal patients without documentation of osteoporosis or osteopenia, there is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against bisphosphonates.

##### **(Neither for nor against | Reviewed, New-added)**

##### **_Discussion_**

Current medical practice does not include the use of bisphosphonates for primary prevention of fracture in a low-risk population, instead reserving this class of medications for those with osteoporosis, with an individualized approach for those with osteopenia and high risk for fracture.

The evidence reviewed for bisphosphonates included four SRs. The SRs excluded patients with osteopenia or osteoporosis, instead looking at potential fracture risk reduction only in a baseline population.

Wen et al. 2020 (221) included 57 RCTs with a total of 29,642 patients and studied vertebral and non-vertebral fractures with the use of ibandronate 150 mg/month. Mean follow-up was slightly over two years. There was no difference detected in vertebral fractures or non-vertebral fractures with ibandronate use.

Wells et al. 2022 (222) included four RCTs evaluating the impact of risedronate 5 mg/day on vertebral fractures or wrist fractures. No difference was detected over one to two years of followup.

Gazoni et al. 2023 (223) consisted of four RCTs examining the effectiveness of zoledronate mg infusion for primary prevention of fracture compared to placebo (n=2,717). Zoledronate was more effective (RR 0.39) than placebo at reducing morphometric vertebral fractures, vertebral fractures, and all clinical fractures after 36 to 72 months of use in postmenopausal women. However, there was no difference in the risk of hip fracture between zoledronate and placebo.

Wells et al. 2025 (224) evaluated thirty-four RCTs containing over 15,000 patients who received alendronate 10 mg/day over one to five years of follow-up. While there was a reduction in vertebral and non-vertebral fractures, there was no reduction in fractures of the wrist or hip.

Overall, the data was mixed for bisphosphonates as a medication class impacting fracture risk. Ardebili et al. 2023 (225) consisted of an SR evaluating bisphosphonates (risedronate, alendronate, ibandronate, or zoledronate) as a class to placebo and found improved BMD with bisphosphonate use. However, the more meaningful clinical outcome of fracture risk reduction was only seen in studies evaluating zoledronate and alendronate.
