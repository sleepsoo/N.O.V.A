# **VA/DOD CLINICAL PRACTICE GUIDELINE FOR THE PREVENTION AND MANAGEMENT OF PRESSURE INJURIES**

**Department of Veterans Affairs Department of Defense**

## **Patient Summary**

#### **QUALIFYING STATEMENTS**

The Department of Veterans Affairs and the Department of Defense guidelines use the best and most recent information that is available at the time they are published. Guidelines provide information that providers, healthcare team members, and patients can use to provide better care for individuals with pressure injuries. They do not define a standard of care, and you should not use them in this way.

This Clinical Practice Guideline is based on a complete and organized review of both clinical studies and studies about how diseases affect the health and illness of groups of people. A panel of experts from a number of clinical fields developed this Guideline. The Guideline clearly explains how different care options relate to health outcomes. To do this, the experts rated both the quality of the clinical studies and the strength of the recommendations.

It is normal for providers to vary in how they plan to treat patients with pressure injuries because they take into account the needs of each patient, the available resources, and the limits that are unique to their healthcare setting or type of practice. Healthcare professionals should assess how well these Guidelines apply to each patient, and their clinical setting or situation. Patients can use the information in this Guideline to understand the different ways that pressure injuries can be managed.

These guidelines do not represent Department of Veterans Affairs or TRICARE policy. The Guideline recommendations for specific tests and/or treatments do not guarantee coverage of the patient’s care by civilian providers or healthcare facilities. You can find more information on current TRICARE benefits at www.tricare.mil or by contacting your regional TRICARE Managed Care Support Contractor.

- **I. What is a pressure injury?**

 - Pressure injuries, also called pressure sores, bed sores, or decubitus ulcers, are areas of skin damage. They can be painful, hard to heal, and may lead to longer hospital stays or the need to go to a nursing home.

 - These injuries happen when a person stays in one position for too long and does not move well. The pressure from lying or sitting in one spot cuts off blood flow to the skin. Without enough blood and oxygen, the skin can begin to die.

 - Sometimes the damage starts deep under the skin and looks like a dark purple bruise before an open sore appears. Other times the skin breaks open right away. Pressure injuries often form over bony areas like the heels or tailbone. They can also be caused by medical devices pressing on the skin.

 - Pressure injuries are more likely to happen when pressure is combined with shear, which is when the skin pulls one way while the body moves another way. A person’s nutrition, medical conditions, circulation, and overall health also affect how easily they can develop a pressure injury.

- **II. If I am at risk, what can I do to avoid pressure injuries?**

 - Skin care done by you or with help from a caregiver:

 **1.** Check your skin every day for potential pressure injuries. Finding changes early can prevent them from getting worse.

 **2.** Specifically, touch or look at the skin at your pressure points (e.g., elbows, buttocks, hips, heels/ankles), and/or under any medical devices for reddened areas or wounds. If you have pain in a bony area, relieve the pressure on it.

 **3.** Check for moisture or wet skin; skin that stays wet from sweat, urine, or stool breaks down faster.

 **4.** Gently clean the skin where you may have potential pressure areas (e.g., elbows, buttocks, hips, heels/ankles) and/or under any medical devices.

 **5.** Apply moisturizer to the skin at your pressure points (e.g., elbows, buttocks, hips, heels/ankles), and/or under any medical devices

 - Activity/mobility

 **1.** Stay active (i.e., walking, wheeling, reaching, leaning) when awake and exercise 30-60 minutes a day 1-2 times a week.

 **2.** When in bed, turn/change positions frequently as recommended by your provider.

 **3.** If in **a** wheelchair, make sure your equipment is properly fitted and in good working condition.

 - Pressure relieving techniques:

 **1.** Consider an air mattress or air overlay if at high risk for pressure injuries.

 **2.** Shift your weight about every 15-20 minutes or 3-4 times an hour when up in a chair, including wheelchairs. Consider weight shifting during commercial breaks when watching TV.

 **3.** If in a wheelchair **:**

 - Use pressure relief strategies that shift your weight off each area of your buttocks for at least 1-2 minutes each (e.g., side to side, stand if possible). If **your** wheelchair has tilt function, use it about every 2 hours.

 - Use a **pressure-relieving** cushion in your chair **;** physical therapy can help

you determine the best one for your needs. Avoid **non-pressure-relieving** devices **,** for example, donut cushions.

 - When traveling, plan for frequent stops or repositioning at least every two hours or as recommended to facilitate pressure relief.

 - Nutrition

 **1.** Eat a well-balanced diet, including whole grains, vegetables and fruits, healthy fats, dairy and protein.

 **2.** Choose high-quality protein options from a variety of sources like meat (beef, pork, poultry, seafood, wild game), dairy (cheese, cottage cheese, regular or Greek yogurt, milk), nuts and nut butters, eggs, beans, tofu and edamame.

 **3.** When appetite is low, consider a protein shake or protein bar.

 - Maintain good oral health.

 - Avoid tobacco

 - Make sure your home is safe to avoid falls

- Contact your provider if you have a change in your health status (e.g., difficulty breathing, pain, change in bowel or bladder function, appetite).

- **III. How do I identify a pressure injury?**

 - A pressure injury (also called a pressure ulcer, bed sore, or decubitus ulcer) is damage to the skin and the tissue under it. It happens when someone stays in one position for too long. This keeps blood from flowing well to the skin. Pressure or sliding (shear) can also harm deeper tissues that you cannot see at first.

 - How it happens: Pressure injuries often happen when a person’s skin slides across a surface (e.g., bed or wheelchair); the skin can stay in one place, while deeper tissues move, causing damage.

 - Here’s how you identify an early pressure injury:

 **1.** Check for early warning signs and look for skin changes over bony areas.

 - These areas include the heels, hips, tailbone, elbows, and the back of the head.

 - Pressure injuries can also happen under medical devices like oxygen tubing or catheters.

 - The skin may look red, and the redness does not turn white when you press on it.

 - In people with darker skin tones, redness may be harder to see. Instead, look for skin that feels different, warmer or cooler, harder or softer, or looks darker, purple, or brown.

 - If the skin is still closed but looks dark red, purple, or maroon, this may mean there is deeper tissue damage under the skin.

 - **_*If experiencing any of the above, please contact your provider immediately._** The skin may feel warmer, firmer, or softer than the skin around it.

 - The person may report the area is sore or painful.

 **2.** Watch for intact blisters or open sores because they may be the start of a pressure injury or a sign it is getting worse. It needs quick care.

### **IV. Who is most vulnerable for pressure injuries?**

People who are most vulnerable for developing pressure injuries are people with limited

mobility and other factors **,** which include but **are** not limited to:

- Spinal cord injury and disorders

- Bowel or bladder incontinence

- Peripheral vascular disease

- Diabetes Mellitus

- Dementia or Other Neurodegenerative Disorders

- Cardiac Disease

- Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)

- Edema

- Malnutrition (including low BMI)

- Burns

- Amputations

- Vascular procedures

- Surgery/operating room

- Intensive Care Unit (ICU)

- Sedentary lifestyle (obesity)

- Aging (including palliative or end-of-life care)

- Medical devices

### **V. How can pressure injuries be treated?**

The best treatment is prevention. However, if you do develop a pressure injury, there are important things you can do to treat it.

**1.** Relieve Pressure

 - The most important step is to relieve pressure off the wound or area of injury. This restores blood flow back to the skin so that healing can occur.

 - Use pillows and cushions to relieve pressure off the wound.

 - Your healthcare provider may recommend specialty mattresses and/or foam pads to help with relieving pressure

**2.** Keep the Wound Clean and Dry

 - Always wash your hands before dressing changes, use gloves, and wash your hands after removing gloves.

 - Gently clean the wound with each scheduled dressing change, if the dressing falls off, or when the dressing gets soiled.

 - Pat dry; do not rub the area

 - Use soap and warm water. Avoid using harsh cleansers (e.g., hydrogen peroxide or alcohol).

**3.** Use Appropriate Wound Treatments/Dressings

 - Your healthcare provider will recommend the right treatment and dressing change schedule for your wound.

 - The goal of local wound treatment is to keep the wound moist, clean, and covered

 - Common dressing types include:

 - Foams: dressings that provide cushion, protect the wound, and help absorb fluids

- Hydrocolloids: soft, flexible bandages that help keep the wound moist and encourage healing

- Hydrogels: used for dry wounds to provide moisture

o Alginates: highly absorbent dressings used to absorb excess fluid

 - Some pressure injuries may require more advanced treatments.

 **4.** Eat Nutritious Foods and Maintain Hydration

 - Eat protein-rich foods

 - Eat colorful vegetables and fruits for vitamins and minerals

 - Drink plenty of water/fluids

 - Ask to speak to a dietitian to learn what is best for you to help heal your wound

 **5.** Look for Signs and Symptoms of Infection

 - These can include redness, pain, pus or drainage, increased warmth, smell, or fever

 - Tell your healthcare provider right away if you notice any signs and/or symptoms of infection

- **VI. Debunking myths of pressure injuries and their treatments**

 **1. Myth:** Pressure injuries are just a part of normal aging.

 - **Truth:** Though aging can increase the risk, pressure injuries are not normal and are preventable.

 **2. Myth:** Over-the-counter antibiotic ointments should be used on a pressure injury. **Truth:** Overuse of antibiotic ointments can cause resistance and irritate tissue.

 **3. Myth:** It is best to leave the wound open to air to let it “dry out” and heal. **Truth:** Moist wound healing is best to expedite healing. By covering the wound, you prevent germs and debris from getting in the wound and allow the wound healing product to do the work.

**4. Myth:** Protein is the only nutrient that helps pressure injuries heal. **Truth:** Healing pressure injuries requires an overall balanced diet with adequate calories, from whole grains, vegetables, fruits, dairy, healthy fats, and high-quality protein sources. All of these nutrients work together to support wound healing.

**5. Myth:** Taking high doses of Vitamin C and zinc will heal pressure injuries faster. **Truth:** Vitamin C and zinc play important roles in wound healing; however, taking large doses does not accelerate healing. In the absence of a deficiency, taking large amounts is not necessary. Excess supplementation may interfere with absorption of other vitamins and minerals.

**VII. Other questions to ask your care team**

#### **Ask about anything that seems unclear to you. Some examples may include:**

#### **Prevention**

- What risk factors do I have for pressure injuries?

- What is your team’s approach to preventing and/or treating pressure injuries in my unique situation?

- How can I maintain a healthy lifestyle to prevent pressure injuries?

- What can be done regarding my nutrition to reduce my risk for pressure injuries?

- What can I do to reposition to prevent pressure injuries?

- How do I prevent pressure injuries caused by medical devices?

- What resources exist to help me understand more?

#### **Treatment**

- Who are the members of my healthcare team and what are their roles?

- How will you help me develop goals for my treatment plan?

- What are my treatment choices? What do you recommend and why?

- How often should I assess my skin and progress?

- What is my role in the treatment plan and how will it affect my daily life?

- How do I manage the medical devices that are recommended to treat the pressure injury?
