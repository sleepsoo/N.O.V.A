# **VA/DOD CLINICAL PRACTICE GUIDELINE FOR DIAGNOSIS AND TREATMENT OF HYPERTENSION IN PRIMARY CARE**

**Department of Veterans Affairs Department of Defense**

## **Patient Summary**

#### **QUALIFYING STATEMENTS**

The Department of Veterans Affairs and the Department of Defense guidelines use the best and most recent information available. Guidelines provide information that healthcare providers and team members and patients can use to provide better care for individuals with hypertension. They do not define a standard of care; rather, they provide guidance that should be tailored to the individual patient.

This Clinical Practice Guideline is based on a review of both clinical studies and studies about how different interventions affect peoples’ hypertension diagnosis or treatment. A panel of experts from a number of clinical fields developed this Guideline. The Guideline explains how different care options relate to important health outcomes. To do this, the guideline looked at the quality of the clinical studies and the strength of the recommendations.

It is normal for providers to vary in how they treat individuals with hypertension. This is because they take into account the needs of each patient, the available resources, and the limits that are unique to their healthcare setting or type of practice. Healthcare professionals should assess how well these Guidelines apply to each patient, and their clinical setting or situation. Patients can use the information in this Guideline to understand the different ways that hypertension can be treated.

These guidelines do not represent Department of Veterans Affairs or TRICARE policy. The Guideline recommendations for specific treatments do not guarantee coverage of a patient’s care by civilian providers or healthcare facilities. You can find more information on current TRICARE benefits at www.tricare.mil or by contacting your regional TRICARE Managed Care Support Contractor.

### **I. What is Hypertension (high blood pressure)?**

<mark>Your blood pressure is the force your blood exerts on the walls of your blood vessels. It is divided into SYSTOLIC (the pressure when your heart contracts) and DIASTOLIC (the pressure when your heart relaxes). Systolic is the higher number of the two, diastolic the lower. Hypertension (HTN) means high blood pressure. This is diagnosed when you have a systolic blood pressure (SBP) greater than or equal to 130 mmHg (millimeters of mercury) and/or a diastolic blood pressure (DBP) greater than or equal to 90 mmHg. If not treated, high blood pressure harms the blood vessels, which carry blood to all the different parts of your body. Untreated high blood pressure can result in strokes, heart attacks, kidney disease, and heart failure.</mark>

#### <mark>Did you know?</mark>

- Almost 1 out of 2 adults in the United States (U.S.) have high blood pressure.(1)

- Roughly 1 out of 4 (22.5%) of adults with high blood pressure have it under control.(1)

- About 1 out of 8 (13%) active-duty military personnel have high blood pressure.(2)

- Combat exposures can increase the risk for high blood pressure.(3)

- About 1 out of 2 Veterans has high blood pressure, making it the most common chronic condition for Veterans.(4)

### **II. Why do people have high blood pressure?**

We do not always know why people have high blood pressure. However, family history and genetics, diet, excess body fat, and not getting enough physical activity can raise blood pressure. Certain medications, illegal drug use, alcohol use, kidney diseases, and breathing problems can also raise blood pressure.

Some people are at risk of high blood pressure. For example:

- People older than 60 years

- People who are overweight or obese

- Smokers

- Men (versus women)

- African Americans

- People with diabetes or kidney disease

- People with a family history of high blood pressure

- Veterans with Agent Orange exposure

### **III. How do I know if I have high blood pressure?**

People cannot feel or sense their own blood pressure. In other words, they do not feel sick when their blood pressure is high. So, your provider will measure your blood pressure every time he or she sees you. If your blood pressure is high, your provider will measure it several times or ask you to come back to make sure it is correct.

Your provider may also ask you to measure your blood pressure at home. For example, he or she may ask you to check at home with a blood pressure cuff. This is a device that measures

your blood pressure and can either confirm the diagnosis or may show that you typically have controlled blood pressure. When you have high readings in a doctor’s office and normal readings at home, this is called White Coat Hypertension. This usually does not need to be treated with medications..

If you have high blood pressure, your provider will ask you additional questions and order some tests which may include:

- A urine test

- Blood tests

- An electrocardiogram (ECG)

### **IV. How can I manage my high blood pressure?**

Changing your diet and increasing physical activity can help you manage your high blood pressure. You may also need medication. High blood pressure is a chronic disease, so you may need treatment for a long time. When you start treatment, you may have to see your provider frequently to adjust the treatment. Even when you do not feel symptoms, or your blood pressure reads as normal, it is important to continue to take medication as prescribed by your provider.

Things you can do that may reduce blood pressure:

- Lose excess weight

- Be physically active

- Eat a healthy/balanced diet

- Limit salt and high-salt foods

- If you drink alcohol, do so in moderation

There are many different medications and types of medications to treat high blood pressure.. If you need medication, your provider will work with you to find the best one(s) for you. It is not unusual to need more than one pill, sometimes even three or more pills, to treat your high blood pressure effectively.

### **V. Frequently Asked Questions**

- I do not feel sick. Why do I need to take medication?

 - People with high blood pressure do not feel sick. However, having high blood pressure can lead to heart failure, stroke, and kidney disease. Because there are no symptoms, hypertension has been nicknamed the “silent killer.” Treating hypertension results in saved lives and improved health outcomes.

- Why do I need to see my provider so often?

 - It may take several months to adjust your treatment to reach your blood pressure goal and/or to find medications that work best for you without side effects.

- My blood pressure is controlled. Can I stop treatment?

 - No, treatment needs to continue to keep blood pressure under control. Most commonly, blood pressure medications need to be taken for life. Speak to your provider before discontinuing any part of your treatment.

- Why are there so many different numbers for diagnosing and treating high blood pressure? o Our understanding of an ‘ideal’ blood pressure changes as we learn more about this condition. Additionally, there are trade-offs whenever we target different blood pressure goals. Therefore, different agencies may settle on different blood pressure targets.

### **VI. Questions to Ask your Care Team**

Ask your provider about anything that seems unclear to you. Some questions you may want to ask include:

- What is my blood pressure goal?

- What side effects might I have from my medication?

- What should I be doing on my own to help manage my high blood pressure?

- Can I get help with losing weight or changing my diet?

- Should I measure my blood pressure on my own? If so, what is the best way?

- May I have a list of my blood pressure medications and doses?
