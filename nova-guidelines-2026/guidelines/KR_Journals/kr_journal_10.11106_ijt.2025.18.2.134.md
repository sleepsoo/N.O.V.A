# Serum Thyroglobulin Monitoring after Thyroid Lobectomy for Differentiated Thyroid Carcinoma: a Narrative Review and Contemporary Guidelines

## Abstract

Thyroid lobectomy has become increasingly common for low-risk differentiated thyroid carcinoma (DTC), yet the role of serum thyroglobulin (Tg) monitoring in this setting remains unclear. This narrative review synthesizes evidence from longitudinal cohort studies and systematic reviews published through January 2025, focusing on the diagnostic performance and clinical utility of serum Tg measurement after thyroid lobectomy. After lobectomy, the residual thyroid lobe continues to produce Tg physiologically, leading to measurable serum levels that often increase gradually over time even in the absence of recurrence. Multiple longitudinal studies demonstrate that Tg measurements have limited sensitivity and specificity for detecting recurrent disease after lobectomy, with considerable overlap between patients with and without structural progression. The presence of anti-Tg antibodies in a substantial proportion of patients further complicates reliable interpretation. Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses consistently report limited diagnostic performance of serial Tg monitoring in post-lobectomy surveillance. Current international guidelines do not endorse routine serial serum Tg testing after lobectomy and prioritize ultrasound-anchored surveillance. An early postoperative nonstimulated Tg may be obtained to screen for unexpectedly high values. Selective remeasurement of Tg can be considered if levels remain elevated or rise over time, particularly when ultrasound is negative. However, imaging with cytologic or histologic confirmation remains the cornerstone of clinical decision-making, with Tg serving as a selective adjunct only in specific clinical contexts.

## Introduction

Over the past decade, surgical management of differentiated thyroid carcinoma (DTC) has shifted from total thyroidectomy toward risk-adapted lobectomy for appropriately selected low-risk tumors. This evolution has coincided in several settings with greater use of hemithyroidectomy and a more selective approach to completion thyroidectomy. This transition is clinically attractive because lobectomy reduces the risks of permanent hypoparathyroidism and recurrent laryngeal nerve injury relative to total thyroidectomy, albeit with a somewhat higher overall recurrence in some series, often occurring in the contralateral lobe or regional lymph nodes.

Serum thyroglobulin (Tg) is foundational for surveillance after total or near-total thyroidectomy, where undetectable or very low Tg portends a low recurrence risk, whereas rising Tg typically signals persistent or recurrent disease; however, the interpretation of Tg after lobectomy is intrinsically more complex because a residual normal thyroid lobe physiologically produces Tg, and values are modulated by thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) management and anti-Tg antibody (TgAb) interference.

Against this backdrop, this review synthesizes contemporary data to clarify the biological and methodological determinants of Tg behavior after lobectomy and outline research priorities for standardized, risk-adapted follow-up pathways.

## Methods

This narrative review synthesizes contemporary evidence on serum Tg monitoring after thyroid lobectomy for DTC. We conducted a comprehensive literature search of PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane databases through January 2025, using combinations of the following search terms: “thyroid lobectomy,” “hemithyroidectomy,” “thyroglobulin,” “differentiated thyroid cancer,” “papillary thyroid carcinoma,” “surveillance,” and “recurrence.”

We prioritized longitudinal cohort studies reporting Tg kinetics after lobectomy, systematic reviews and meta-analyses evaluating Tg diagnostic performance, and contemporary clinical practice guidelines from major professional societies. Studies were included if they reported original data on post-lobectomy Tg measurements in patients with DTC, with particular attention to those examining the relationship between Tg levels and structural recurrence. We excluded case reports, studies focusing exclusively on total thyroidectomy cohorts, and non-English publications without accessible translations.

Given the heterogeneity in surgical approaches, follow-up protocols, Tg assay methods, and outcome definitions across studies, we conducted a narrative synthesis rather than quantitative meta-analysis. Key longitudinal cohort studies, systematic reviews, and international guideline documents were selected for in-depth review and critical appraisal. Evidence quality was assessed based on study design, sample size, duration of follow-up, completeness of outcome ascertainment, and methodological rigor. This narrative approach enabled integration of diverse evidence types—including biological, analytical, and clinical perspectives—to provide a comprehensive overview of Tg monitoring in the post-lobectomy setting.

## Biology and Kinetics of Tg after Lobectomy

After thyroid lobectomy, circulating Tg remains measurable because the residual thyroid lobe continues to synthesize and secrete Tg under TSH stimulation; consequently, absolute Tg values observed after total thyroidectomy with or without radioiodine ablation (RAI) (where low basal thresholds such as approximately 0.2 ng/mL can carry strong negative predictive value) are not physiologically transferable to the lobectomy setting.

Across contemporary lobectomy cohorts, baseline or early follow-up Tg commonly falls in the single- to low–double-digit range, with reports of approximately 7-8 ng/mL at approximately 1 year and approximately 12 ng/mL over longer follow-up, reflecting the mass and function of the remaining lobe.

Physiologically, Tg output from the residual lobe is modulated by pituitary drive and compensatory changes in thyroid tissue; accordingly, Tg is influenced by contemporaneous TSH, and ratio-based normalization (e.g., Tg/TSH) has been used to partially adjust for this dependence in longitudinal assessments.

Longitudinal studies demonstrate that, in patients managed with lobectomy, Tg often shows a gradual increase over time—on the order of about 10% per year—even in the absence of disease events, consistent with adaptive physiology rather than oncologic progression. Nevertheless, individual trajectories are heterogeneous, with sizable proportions showing stable or even declining Tg during early follow-up.

Coexisting thyroid conditions—such as Hashimoto’s thyroiditis or sizable contralateral nodules—can further elevate Tg and widen interindividual variability at any time point after lobectomy, underscoring the limited interpretability of single absolute values.

## Assay and Analytical Considerations

Assay selection should prioritize methods traceable to the human Tg certified reference material CRM-457 (also referred to as BCR-457), because traceability to this calibrator reduces—but does not eliminate—between-assay bias that can otherwise misclassify dynamic risk or response-to-therapy categories during longitudinal follow-up.

For every Tg draw, TgAb should be measured concurrently and interpreted as an analytic variable, because TgAb—present in roughly one-quarter of DTC patients—introduce method-dependent, predominantly negative bias in immunometric assay (IMA) and radioimmunoassays (RIA) via epitope masking and other mechanisms; trending TgAb over time is informative, but absolute Tg values from IMA/ RIA in TgAb-positive sera should be viewed as unreliable for disease quantification.

## Do We Measure Tg Routinely after Lobectomy?: Evidence Synthesis and Contemporary Guideline Positions

### Evidence from Longitudinal Studies and Systematic Syntheses

Multiple studies have shown that, after thyroid lobectomy, the kinetics of serum Tg largely reflect secretion from the residual thyroid tissue, thereby limiting its sensitivity and specificity for detecting recurrence. Although several studies nevertheless proposed absolute post-lobectomy Tg thresholds as risk flags, most notably a non-stimulated Tg ≥30 ng/mL embedded in dynamic risk stratification as a criterion for “biochemical incomplete response”, in a large longitudinal cohort, Tg and Tg/TSH rose gradually over time after lobectomy without discriminating between patients with and without recurrence; periodic Tg measurements were of limited predictive value. Similarly, a focused analysis concluded that Tg alone is of limited value for predicting or detecting recurrence following lobectomy, reinforcing the preference for ultrasound-based surveillance.

Recent evidence syntheses reached concordant conclusions. An American Thyroid Association (ATA) Guideline Committee–commissioned systematic review (2022) highlighted substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity across surgery/RAI strata and wide variability in the diagnostic accuracy of serum Tg after lobectomy or after total/near-total thyroidectomy without radioiodine, concluding that Tg alone is of limited value for detecting persistent, recurrent, or metastatic disease in these settings. Likewise, a 2022 meta-analysis by Giovanella and colleagues reported that, in hemithyroidectomy cohorts, circulating Tg is not a reliable marker for early response assessment or recurrence prediction, reinforcing that Tg alone is of limited utility.

Notwithstanding these overall limitations, selected clinical scenarios merit nuance. In patients managed with thyroid lobectomy who present with compelling clinical or radiologic concern for distant metastasis, markedly elevated non-stimulated serum Tg concentrations—while inherently confounded by physiologic remnant secretion and potential assay interferences—may serve as an adjunctive clue to corroborate suspicion; however, most quantitative cut-points for metastasis derive from post-total-thyroidectomy±radioiodine cohorts and should not be extrapolated to lobectomy without caution, and imaging with cytologic/histologic confirmation remains the diagnostic priority.

In addition, emerging cohort data indicate that a persistently ascending trajectory of non-stimulated Tg after lobectomy is associated with an increased risk of lateral neck recurrence, although overall discrimination by absolute Tg values remains modest and external validation is needed.

### Current Guidance on Post-Lobectomy Tg Monitoring

In light of this evidence, contemporary guidelines have updated their recommendations for Tg surveillance after lobectomy (Table 3). The 2025 ATA guideline, routine measurement of serum Tg during the initial follow-up after thyroid lobectomy is not recommended; this is an explicit graded statement (Recommendation 47-D). A single Tg assessment at 6-12 weeks postoperatively on thyroid hormone with a normal TSH is described as a prudent step to ensure that levels are not “unexpectedly elevated,” while no numerical cut-off is provided (Recommendation 30-B; Good Practice Statement). When that early Tg is not markedly elevated, additional serial Tg testing is not recommended in the initial surveillance period; imaging (especially neck ultrasonography) remains the principal modality for follow-up in this context.

Compared with the 2015/2016 ATA guideline framework, which primarily centered Tg-based surveillance on patients treated with total thyroidectomy (±RAI) and acknowledged interpretive limitations when residual thyroid tissue is present, the 2025 guideline adds explicit lobectomy-specific directives: a one-time early Tg for outlier detection, no routine serial Tg thereafter, and reliance on imaging for surveillance. This clarifies prior ambiguity and aligns graded recommendations with the cumulative post-lobectomy literature.

Within the 2024 Korean Thyroid Association (KTA) follow-up guidance, surveillance is organized under an ongoing risk-stratification framework, with neck ultrasonography and targeted laboratory testing forming the backbone of routine follow-up. In patients managed with thyroid lobectomy, periodic measurement of non-stimulated serum Tg may be considered (conditional recommendation). When serum Tg remains persistently elevated or rises over time—particularly in the setting of a negative neck ultrasound—additional imaging is advised, including contrast-enhanced neck computed tomography (CT) and, when appropriate, chest CT or a diagnostic radioiodine scan. Overall, the KTA emphasizes ultrasound-anchored surveillance, selective use of non-stimulated Tg after lobectomy, and avoidance of routine stimulated Tg in the absence of remnant ablation.

In conclusion, after thyroid lobectomy, serum Tg largely reflects physiologic secretion from the residual lobe; longitudinal cohorts consistently demonstrate modest year-to-year increases with limited discrimination for recurrence. Also, recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses likewise confirm the limited diagnostic performance of Tg after lobectomy; on this basis, contemporary guidelines endorse ultrasound-anchored surveillance rather than routine, serial Tg testing. However, in specific circumstances—such as a persistently rising Tg trajectory or an unexpectedly elevated Tg value—additional, time-sequenced Tg re-measurement should be considered. Taken together, definitive evaluation and clinical decision-making should remain grounded in imaging and cytologic or histologic confirmation, with Tg used as a selective adjunct in exceptional contexts to support follow-up of patients managed with lobectomy.
