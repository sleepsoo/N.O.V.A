# Reclassification of BRCA1/2 gene variants of uncertain significance according to American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics guidelines

## **Original Article**

J Genet Med 2025;22(2):56-62 https://doi.org/10.5734/JGM.2025.22.2.56 ISSN 1226-1769 (Print) 2383-8442 (Online)

# Reclassification of BRCA1/2 gene variants of uncertain significance according to American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics guidelines

Kyung Bae Lee1,† , In soo Rheem2,† , and Jae Kyung Kim3,* 1Department of Medical Laser, Dankook University Graduate School of Medicine, Cheonan, Korea

2Department of Laboratory Medicine, Dankook University College of Medicine, Cheonan, Korea 3Department of Biomedical Laboratory Science, College of Health Sciences, Dankook University, Cheonan, Korea

**Purpose:** Mutations in BRCA1 and BRCA2 genes are associated with increased risk of breast cancer. Variants identified in BRCA1/2 are classified as pathogenic, benign, or variants of uncertain significance (VUS). Regular reassessment of VUS is essential to ensure accurate patient risk identification.

**Materials and Methods:** In this study, we analyzed the reclassification of BRCA1/2 gene variants in 1,652 samples from 2020 to 2022, following the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics guidelines. Blood samples obtained from patients were sequenced and BRCA1/2 sequences were assessed to identify VUS.

**Results:** A total of 93 VUS were identified, of which 30 (32.25%) were reclassified. The reclassified variants included 10 benign, 3 benign/likely benign, 6 likely benign, 1 benign/uncertain significance, 7 likely benign/uncertain significance, 1 likely pathogenic/uncertain significance, 1 likely pathogenic, and 1 pathogenic. Reanalysis using public databases, such as ClinVar plays a critical role in variant interpretation. Additionally, regular data updates and institution-specific reanalysis protocols are essential for maintaining accuracy. The high reclassification rate observed in this study can be attributed to advancements in data analysis technology.

**Conclusion:** Regular reanalysis of BRCA1/2 variant is critical for personalized risk assessment and treatment. Institutions should implement systems for periodic reanalysis and incorporate the latest data for improved diagnostic accuracy. Future research should focus on larger sample sizes and foster collaboration to achieve global standardization of gene variant interpretation, contributing to the development of personalized treatment strategies and overall medical advancement.

**Key words:** BRCA1 protein, BRCA2 protein, Genetic variation, Breast neoplasms, Ovarian neoplasms, Mutation.

damaged DNA repair, thereby reducing cancer risk [2]. Women carrying mutations in BRCA1 face an increased 57% risk of developing breast cancer before the age of 70 years, whereas those with BRCA2 mutations have a 49% risk. Additionally, these mutations are also associated with increased risk for development of ovarian cancer, estimated at 40% for carriers of mutations in BRCA1 and 18% for carriers of mutations in BRCA2 [3]. In the South Korean population, the risk of developing breast cancer before the age of 70 years is higher than global average, reaching 72.1% for BRCA1 mutation carriers and 66.3% for BRCA2 mutation carriers, while the risks for ovarian cancer are 24.6% and 11.1%, respectively [4]. These numbers emphasize the urgent need for effective screening and management strategies within the South Korean population.

Based on the guidelines set by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG), BRCA1/2 mutations can be divided into five distinct categories: Pathogenic (P), Likely Pathogenic (LP), Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS), Likely Benign (LB), and Benign (B) [5]. VUS represent variants with undetermined pathogenicity at the time of testing [6]. Approximately 5-21% of individuals tested for BRCA1/2 mutations in the US are classified as carrying VUS, with higher rates in Asian and African-American populations [7]. Resources like the Breast Cancer Information Core (https://research.nhgri.nih.gov/bic/) and ClinVar (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/) provide risk assessments for VUS. However, classifying mutations remains difficult because of limitations in the available data [8]. Establishing internal guidelines for the analysis of VUS results is challenging owing to varying resources and workforce capacities of different institutions [9,10]. The College of American Pathologists has incorporated checklist requirements addressing variant interpretation and reporting for clinical next-generation sequencing testing; therefore, laboratories should establish and document internal procedures/guidelines for interpreting and managing VUS results [11]. In addition, periodic re-evaluation is recommended, and recent evidence suggests that VUS should be re-reviewed at intervals of no more than 3 years to support optimized patient care [12].

This study investigates the reclassification of BRCA1/2 mutations initially categorized as VUS, analyzing data from 1,652 individuals tested between 2020 and 2022 according to ACMG guidelines. The reclassification of BRCA1/2 mutations initially classified as VUS presents several challenges, including insufficient amount of relevant data, different interpretation methods, and the necessity for standardized guidelines across institutions. With our analysis, we aim to optimize the review cycle, facilitat-

ing faster clinical evaluation of mutations, improving diagnosis and treatment of patients harboring BRCA mutations.

### **Materials and Methods**

#### **1. Study participants**

We analyzed blood samples (collected in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid [EDTA] tubes) from 1,652 individuals tested for BRCA1/2 mutations according to the ACMG guidelines [13]. Samples were obtained from a single medical institution in Gyeonggi-do (South Korea) between January 2, 2020, and July 29, 2022. This retrospective study did not utilize any personally identifiable information and was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Dankook University (IRB No. 2020-10013).

#### **2. BRCA1/2 sequencing primers**

Comprehensive sequencing of the 47 exons of BRCA1 and BRCA2, along with ±30 bases of adjacent intronic regions, was conducted using 94 custom-designed primers specific to BRCA1 and 112 specific to BRCA2. Primer sequences have been excluded from the manuscript to ensure compliance with data protection and confidentiality requirements.

#### **3. DNA extraction**

Genomic DNA was extracted from EDTA-treated blood samples using the Tianlong Nucleic Acid Extraction Kit (Tianlong), specific for whole-blood processing [14]. The concentration of the extracted DNA was measured using a Nanodrop Spectrophotometer ND-1000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) [15].

#### **4. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and sequencing**

PCR amplification was conducted in 0.2-mL strip tubes on a ProFlex™ 96-well PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The reaction mixture was prepared to a final volume of 25 μL, comprising 17.4 μL of distilled water, 2.5 μL of 10X buffer, 2 μL of deoxynucleotide triphosphates (2 mM), 2 μL of primers at a concentration of 5 pmol/μL, 0.1 μL of Taq DNA polymerase (0.5 U/μL), and 1 μL of the DNA template. The thermal cycling protocol included an initial denaturation at 94°C for 5 min, followed by 34 cycles of denaturation at 94°C for 30 seconds, annealing at 58°C for 30 seconds, and extension at 72°C for 50 seconds, with a final extension step at 72°C for 10 minutes. After amplification, the PCR products were purified using the ExoSAP-IT™ PCR Product Cleanup Reagent (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific) in preparation for sequencing.

For sequencing, the purified DNA (1 μL of PCR product) was combined with 5 μL distilled water, 2 μL BigDye (Thermo Fisher Scientific) buffer, 1 μL sequencing primers (forward or reverse, 2 pmol/μL), and 1 μL BigDye, yielding a total reaction volume of 10 μL. Sequencing PCR was conducted with the following conditions: an initial denaturation at 96°C for 1 minute, followed by 25 cycles of 10 seconds at 96°C, 5 seconds at 50°C, and 4 minutes at 60°C. The reaction was then maintained at 4°C for 10 minutes to complete the process.

#### **5. Sequencing analysis**

Sequencing products were analyzed using the Applied Biosystems 3500 and 3500 Dx Series Genetic Analyzers (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and the resulting data were processed using SeqScape® Software Version 2.7 (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Significant sequence variants identified in exons and introns ±20 bp of BRCA1/2 were evaluated for pathogenicity using the Human Gene Mutation and ClinVar databases [16,17]. Genetic variants were categorized according to the ACMG guidelines into five types: P, indicating that the variant directly causes or significantly contributes to the development or progression of a disease; LP, suggesting the variant is probably disease-causing but may require further confirmation; VUS, wherein the association of the variant and the disease is unclear; LB, implying the variant is probably not disease-causing but may require additional evidence; and B, indicating that the variant has no impact on disease [18]. In addition to these standard ACMG classifications, certain variants were categorized into conflicting classification categories, such as LP/VUS or LB/VUS. These classifications were applied to variants demonstrating a probable shift toward a more definitive category based on available evidence but did not meet the full criteria for reclassification under ACMG guidelines. This approach facilitated a more nuanced representation of the evolving nature of variant interpretation and emphasized the importance of continuous reassessment using updated clinical and functional data. All genetic variants were documented using the Human Genome Variation Society standard nomenclature.

Pathogenic variants were classified using several criteria: PVS1 (loss-of-function mutation), PS1 (same amino acid alteration as a previously known pathogenic variant), PS2 (de novo mutation), PS3 (evidence from functional studies supporting pathogenicity), and PS4 (high frequency in affected individuals). Benign variants were categorized using criteria such as BA1 (high prevalence in the general population), BS1 (higher frequency than anticipated for the disorder), BS2 (presence in healthy indi-

viduals), and BS3 (functional studies demonstrating no harmful effect) [19].

### **Results**

Between 2020 and 2022, a total of 1,652 samples were analyzed for BRCA1/2 mutations and classified following the ACMG guidelines. The analysis revealed 186 P, 12 LP, 180 LB, and 1,449 B variants.

In 2020, we identified 89 VUS in 538 patients, corresponding to an incidence rate of 16.53%. In 2021, VUS were detected in 119 out of 649 samples, an incidence rate of 18.34%. In 2022, 54 out of 465 patients were found to harbor VUS, corresponding to an incidence rate of 11.61%. After excluding duplicate results, VUS classification included 1 nonsense mutation, 4 in-frame deletions, 11 intronic variants, and 82 missense mutations.

Using data from the Breast Cancer Information Core and ClinVar, 93 unique VUS were re-evaluated after removing duplicates. Out of the 93 VUS identified, 45 variants (48.38%) retained their classification as VUS, while 30 variants (32.25%) were reclassified. Additionally, ClinVar data was unavailable for the remaining 18 variants (19.35%; Fig. 1).

Among the reclassified variants (30 variants, 32.25%), 33.33% (10 variants) were reclassified as B, 10.00% (3 variants) as B/LB, 20.00% (6 variants) as LB, 3.33% (1 variant) as B/VUS, 23.33% (7 variants) as LB/VUS, 3.33% (1 variant) as LP/VUS, 3.33% (1 variant) as LP, and 3.33% (1 variant) as P (Table 1).

### **Discussion**

We investigated the reclassification of VUS identified in BRCA1/2 genetic tests conducted between 2020 and 2022. BRCA1/2 are critical tumor suppressor genes involved in the development of breast and ovarian cancers, making accurate classification of identified variants essential for diagnosis and treatment planning [20]. Our findings indicate that approximately 32.25% of the BRCA variants underwent reclassification, highlighting the importance of reassessing the clinical classification of existing variants. Reclassification based on evolving research and technological advances is essential for effective patient management [21].

Using public databases, such as ClinVar, is essential for the accurate interpretation of genetic variants [22]. ClinVar regularly updates its clinical classifications based on the latest research, offering valuable insights for refining clinical classification of variants [23]. The primary distinction between these conflicting classification categories and the ACMG five-tier system (P, LP, VUS, LB, B) is that ACMG guidelines do not formally recognize transitional classifications. However, in clinical practice and research settings, these categories provide valuable insights into the evolving understanding of variant pathogenicity, particularly when integrating new data from functional studies, segregation analyses, and in silico predictions. Through the incorporation of these transitional categories, our study aimed to highlight variants that may soon be reclassified based on emerging evidence, thereby improving the clinical utility of genetic testing.

Public databases integrate information from institutions and researchers worldwide, which is useful when assessing rare variants with limited available data. However, relying solely on these public databases may result in significant inaccuracies in clinical classification of variants if they are not frequently updated or lack patient-specific data [24]. Therefore, testing organizations should consistently consult these resources for the latest infor-

mation and align their internal protocols with regular reviews of ACMG standards and guidelines [25].

This study shows a notably higher reclassification rate for VUS compared with previous research studies. A study published in 2018 reported that only 7.7% VUS (2,048 of 26,670 variants) detected via cancer screening, were reclassified [26]. In contrast, the reclassification rate in this study was 32.25%, which can be attributed to the increased availability of updated information in databases and advances in reanalysis methods, particularly those using in silico methodologies [27,28]. These findings underscore the necessity of continuous monitoring of database updates, as well as in silico and analytical methods, to ensure accurate interpretation and reclassification of variants [29].

Considering our findings, institutions analyzing BRCA1/2 variants should establish regular reanalysis schedules to ensure continuous evaluation of variant pathogenicity. With the rapid advancements in understanding variant clinical significance, reclassification intervals should be shorter than the commonly suggested five years. Recent research indicates a one-year interval may be more appropriate [21,30].

This research is not without limitations. The sample size may not adequately represent the diversity of the population, potentially hindering the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, reliance on public databases might not reflect the latest research developments, potentially leading to inaccurate variant interpretations due to insufficient data availability for certain variants. Furthermore, expert judgment during the reclassification process can introduce inconsistencies, as differing interpreta-

tions may lead to varying conclusions [31]. Lastly, the retrospective nature of the study might not represent the most current data available from real-time analyses.

Despite these limitations, the study underscores the importance of advanced reanalysis techniques, using updated databases and algorithms, in refining patient diagnosis and treatment strategies. The findings emphasize the importance of periodic re-evaluation of genetic variants to improve risk assessment and personalize treatment plans. These practices will enhance our comprehension of genetic variation and contribute to a worldwide improvement in the standards of patient care.

Future research should include larger, more diverse sample populations to strengthen the generalizability of the results.

Furthermore, the standardization of global genetic variant interpretation is essential, achievable through the establishment of comprehensive and reliable databases and fostering increased collaboration and data sharing among institutions. These efforts will facilitate the development of tailored therapies and contribute to the advancement of the overall healthcare system.
