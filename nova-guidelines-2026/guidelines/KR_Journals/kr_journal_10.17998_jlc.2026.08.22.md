Stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) is a noninvasive, high-precision radiation technique that delivers a high dose to the tumor in a limited number of fractions, typically five or fewer, and is increasingly used as a curative-intent option for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), particularly when surgical resection, liver transplantation, or local ablative therapies are not feasible. Although domestic and international guidelines have incorporated SBRT into the treatment algorithm for HCC, the lack of comprehensive evidence and expert opinion regarding patient selection, pretreatment evaluation, treatment planning and delivery, complication management, and post-treatment follow-up has resulted in substantial variation in clinical practice. Accordingly, expert consensus-based practical recommendations for SBRT were developed by a group of radiation oncologists, hepatologists, and radiologists from the Research Committee of the Korean Liver Cancer Association, based on expert surveys, a comprehensive review of evidence-based literature, and a modified Delphi process. Encompassing the entire clinical workflow of liver SBRT, these recommendations are intended to provide practical guidance for the safe and effective implementation of SBRT in patients with HCC. ### INTRODUCTION

Stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) is an advanced radiation therapy (RT) technique characterized by the delivery of high-dose radiation to a tumor over a limited number of fractions, typically five or fewer. To achieve high-precision treatment, SBRT integrates cutting-edge technologies, including image-guided radiation therapy (IGRT), intensity-modulated radiation therapy, and respiratory motion management. These capabilities distinguish SBRT from conventional RT by enabling the delivery of curative doses to the target tumor while minimizing damage to the surrounding normal liver parenchyma and adjacent critical organs.1

The clinical significance of SBRT is increasingly recognized in both domestic and international clinical practice guidelines. The 2022 Korean Liver Cancer Association (KLCA)-National Cancer Center (NCC) Korea practice guidelines for management of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) recommend RT as an alternative treatment option when surgical resection, liver transplantation, or local ablative therapies are not feasible.2 Furthermore, the guidelines highlight SBRT as a potential subsequent treatment for recurrent HCC following primary interventions such as resection, local ablative therapies, or transarterial chemoembolization (TACE). Similarly, major guidelines from the United States, Europe, and Japan have incorporated SBRT into their treatment algorithms, establishing it as a key pillar of curative intent strategies.3-6

The KLCA has consistently provided practical clinical guidance by publishing expert consensus-based practical recommendations on major therapeutic modalities, including TACE in 2023, local ablation in 2024, and surgical treatment in 2025.7-9 As an extension of these systematic efforts, the current practical recommendations aim to provide specific and practical guidance for the safe and effective implementation of SBRT. This consensus was developed based on expert surveys, a comprehensive review of the available evidence, and multidisciplinary panel discussions. It encompasses the entire clinical workflow of SBRT, ranging from patient selection and treatment preparation to delivery techniques, complication management, and post-treatment follow-up.

### EXPERT CONSENSUS

The Research Committee of the KLCA formed an expert committee consisting of radiation oncologists (n=8), hepatologists (n=3), and radiologists (n=2). To provide practical guidance for the clinical implementation of SBRT in patients with HCC, the

committee established and developed eight core domains: patient selection, pretreatment imaging and liver function assessment, simulation, treatment planning, treatment delivery, post-treatment patient management and follow-up, and tumor response assessment. Through qualitative literature analysis across these eight domains, the expert committee derived 21 recommendations, which were validated by an external expert panel using a modified Delphi survey method. The expert panel consisted of 42 HCC specialists recommended by the KLCA and the Korean Society for Radiation Oncology, including specialists in radiation oncology, hepatology, surgery, and radiology.

The level of agreement among the expert panel for each recommendation was collected on a scale of 0 (strongly disagree) to 100 (strongly agree) and classified into five stages. Anonymity was maintained throughout the survey process so that all panelists participating in the Delphi survey were unaware of each other’s responses; the survey results were collected and analyzed by the Chair and the Secretary. Recommendations that received more than 75% agreement in the first Delphi round were considered to have reached a consensus. As a result of the first round, 20 of the 21 proposed recommendations reached a consensus, while one failed to do so. According to predefined criteria, the recommendation that did not reach consensus in the first round was partially revised and subjected to a second Delphi round. In this process, the individual responses of the panelists remained anonymous and were not identifiable by anyone except the Chair and the Secretary. In the second Delphi round, recommendations with more than 80% agreement were deemed to have reached a consensus. Following the second Delphi survey, one final recommendation failed to reach a consensus (Fig. 1).

### PATIENT SELECTION

According to the international clinical guidelines, including the 2022 revised HCC treatment guidelines by the KLCA and NCC Korea, RT is a treatment modality applied for various purposes across different stages of HCC.2-5,10 SBRT is an RT method that maximizes treatment efficacy by delivering high-dose radiation to a tumor over a limited number of fractions with high treatment accuracy. Although definitions vary slightly depending on the country and the primary tumor, in the Republic of Korea, SBRT is typically defined as high-dose therapy consisting of five or fewer treatment sessions. Compared to liver resection and local ablative therapies, SBRT is non-invasive, can be administered on an outpatient basis, and can be applied relatively safely even in patients who are elderly or have comorbid chronic conditions.

Due to these advantages, it is being utilized as an alternative curative treatment for HCC.1 This expert consensus focuses primarily on the role of SBRT as a curative treatment modality for HCC.

For liver SBRT, treatment is generally feasible provided that the prescribed dose can be delivered while respecting the dose constraints of the remaining liver and surrounding organs at risk, irrespective of tumor size or number. In particular, treatment can be performed relatively safely compared with other local therapies, even in cases where the tumor is located in the hepatic dome adjacent to the diaphragm or near intrahepatic vessels and bile ducts.11 However, the use of high-dose SBRT may be limited in cases where it is difficult to avoid radiation damage due to proximity to the gastrointestinal tract, where baseline liver function is insufficient to tolerate RT, or where the patient’s general condition is poor and cooperation with treatment is deemed difficult due to irregular breathing.1,12-14 In a survey regarding the optimal tumor size for SBRT, 89% and 42% of the expert panel selected ≤3 cm and ≤5 cm, respectively, as the optimal upper size limit.

Risk factors for major adverse events should also be considered when selecting HCC patients for SBRT. The most important adverse event after SBRT is deterioration of liver function. Since most HCCs develop in patients with liver cirrhosis caused by chronic hepatitis B, chronic hepatitis C, alcohol, and metabolic

dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, it is essential to consider both the natural decline in liver function associated with cirrhosis progression and SBRT-induced hepatic toxicity when assessing liver function after SBRT.15,16 In general, patients with Child-Pugh class A or B7 liver function represent the optimal indication for SBRT. For patients with Child-Pugh class B8 or higher, or those with uncontrolled ascites or hepatic encephalopathy, SBRT can be considered using more stringent dose-volume constraints.1-3,10 Gastrointestinal toxicity is another major adverse event following SBRT; therefore, the feasibility of SBRT should be thoroughly assessed when a tumor is adjacent to the gastrointestinal tract.3,4 Existing data suggest that combining SBRT with sorafenib or bevacizumab increases the risk of severe gastrointestinal toxicity, and greater caution is required when determining the dose-volume constraints in this clinical setting.17 In cases where an elevated risk of major adverse events is anticipated with SBRT, modification of the treatment strategy, including a reduction in dose per fraction and an increase in the number of fractions, should be considered.

The combination of SBRT and TACE should be considered for patients with HCC who have an incomplete response to TACE or in whom an inadequate response is anticipated. SBRT can be performed following the resolution of acute adverse events associated with TACE.18,19

In the real-world clinical setting, repeated liver SBRT is sometimes applied to patients with HCC who have previously undergone SBRT; however, evidence on the safety and efficacy of re-irradiation to guide clinical practice remains scarce. Few studies suggest that re-irradiation can be safely administered to patients with a long treatment interval since the previous SBRT, preserved liver function (Child-Pugh class A or B7), and sufficient remaining liver volume. However, the feasibility of re-irradiation is limited when high-dose volumes overlap with previously irradiated areas of the gastrointestinal organs or the central biliary tract.20,21 A survey was conducted on the optimal timing of re-irradiation following the initial SBRT in patients with a new intrahepatic recurrence that was sufficiently distant from the previous SBRT field. The results showed that 41.5% of the expert panel responded that repeated SBRT could be performed “immediately,” while 24.4% each suggested waiting 3 and 6 months, respectively. In a survey on whether re-irradiation with SBRT can be performed using the same dose-volume constraints as the initial SBRT in the absence of overlap with the previous SBRT field, 24.4% of the expert panel responded with “strongly agree” and 29.3% with “agree.” However, 46.3% of the panel opted for “depends on the situation,” suggesting that various clinical factors related to the patient and the tumor may influence the decision-making process for re-irradiation. Therefore, further research is warranted to define the optimal indication for re-irradiation of the liver with SBRT and to establish standardized recommendations for its clinical application.

###### **[Recommendations]**

1. The indications for SBRT should be based on the KLCA-NCC Korea practice guidelines for the management of hepatocellular carcinoma. Furthermore, both the patient’s performance status and the risk factors for major adverse events, including underlying liver function and tumor location, should be thoroughly evaluated.

### PRETREATmENT ImAgINg fOR STEREOTACTIC bODy RADIATION ThERAPy

SBRT is a highly precise, image-guided local treatment modality; therefore, pretreatment imaging is essential for accurate and safe treatment planning. Pretreatment imaging also serves as the baseline for post-treatment response assessment, extending its role beyond diagnostic evaluation alone. Appropriate and timely imaging contributes not only to improving treatment efficacy and safety but also to evaluating the anatomical relationship between

the tumor and adjacent structures, such as vessels, bile ducts, and the gastrointestinal tract. This information is important for determining the feasibility of SBRT and anticipating potential treatment-related complications.

For diagnostic evaluation prior to SBRT, dynamic contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are recommended as complementary imaging modalities. CT provides accurate anatomical assessment and can be readily registered with treatment planning CT because both are based on the same imaging modality. CT also offers high spatial resolution and wide scan coverage, making it useful for evaluating extrahepatic metastases, while offering advantages in cost-effectiveness.22 MRI complements CT by providing superior soft tissue contrast and multiparametric lesion characterization. MRI may be performed using either extracellular contrast agents (e.g., gadopentetate dimeglumine) or hepatocyte-specific contrast agents (e.g., gadolinium ethoxybenzyl diethylenetriamine pentaacetic acid), depending on clinical needs. In particular, hepatobiliary phase (HBP) imaging using a hepatocyte-specific contrast agent is useful for accurately delineating tumor margins by maximizing the signal intensity contrast between the tumor and the surrounding liver parenchyma. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) can also improve the detection of small intrahepatic lesions and may be helpful for evaluating the target lesion and detecting additional lesions before SBRT (Fig. 2).22-25 Meanwhile, treatment planning CT alone may not provide sufficient information regarding arterial phase hyperenhancement, portal venous washout, and tumor margins of HCC. Therefore, diagnostic dynamic contrast-enhanced CT or MRI should be obtained before treatment planning. These diagnostic images can be integrated with the treatment planning CT through image registration to improve the accuracy of target delineation and treatment planning.26,27

Although the optimal timing of pretreatment imaging for SBRT has not been established due to a lack of direct evidence, based on expert consensus informed by clinical experience, an interval within 1 month between imaging and treatment initiation may be appropriate, considering the potential for tumor progression. Imaging obtained within 2 months may still be acceptable for treatment planning; however, if the interval exceeds 2 months, changes in tumor size or margins may reduce the accuracy of target delineation and the appropriateness of baseline imaging for treatment response assessment. In such cases, additional imaging before SBRT may be considered. Further studies are needed to establish evidence-based guidance on the optimal timing of pretreatment imaging.

###### **[Recommendations]**

1. For pretreatment imaging prior to SBRT, dynamic contrast-enhanced CT and MRI are recommended as complementary modalities.

2. To support accurate treatment planning and reliable baseline imaging for response assessment, it is appropriate to keep the interval between imaging and SBRT within 1 month when feasible. If the interval exceeds 2 months, additional imaging may be considered.

#### **Liver function assessment prior to stereotactic body radiation therapy**

Most patients with HCC have underlying cirrhosis or hepatitis, which can increase the risk of deterioration of liver function after treatment. 28 Therefore, liver function assessment prior to SBRT is

essential, both to prevent post-treatment hepatic decompensation and to determine an appropriate radiation dose for each patient. Rather than relying on a single parameter, liver function should be evaluated by integrating multiple indicators, including clinical findings, laboratory tests, and imaging studies (Fig. 3).

Routine laboratory tests, including aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), total bilirubin, albumin, and platelet count, are useful for predicting the risk of post-treatment hepatic deterioration, and several composite models combining these parameters have been clinically applied. The ChildPugh score and class are the most widely used indices for assessing liver function and stratify patients into three classes: A (5-6), B (7-9), and C (10-15).29 Among Child-Pugh class B patients, those with a score of B7 are likely to have compensated cirrhosis, whereas those with B8-9 frequently exhibit features of decompensation such as ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, or jaundice, and are therefore at higher risk of hepatic deterioration following HCC-directed treatment.30 Multiple studies have demonstrated

It should also be noted that the Child-Pugh score relies on subjective parameters such as ascites and hepatic encephalopathy.

The albumin-bilirubin (ALBI) grade is a more objective and continuous index that complements the subjective limitations of the Child-Pugh score and is particularly useful for predicting prognosis and determining treatment indications in patients with borderline liver function (Child-Pugh A6-B7). Previous studies have shown that the ALBI score outperforms the Child-Pugh score in predicting overall survival and adverse events among Child-Pugh class A patients,32 and patients with ALBI grade 1 or 2 have demonstrated favorable efficacy and safety outcomes after SBRT.33 In patients with ALBI grade 3, SBRT may be considered on a limited basis, only for localized lesions when no other treatment is feasible, given the elevated risk of post-treatment hepatic failure and radiation-induced liver disease associated with reduced hepatic reserve.

Vibration-controlled transient elastography is a noninvasive imaging modality with high accuracy for the diagnosis of hepatic fibrosis and cirrhosis and has potential for liver function assessment; however, evidence specifically addressing its use prior to

SBRT remains limited.34 In addition, CT is useful for evaluating the presence of cirrhosis, ascites, and remaining liver volume.

In patients with a history of prior HCC-directed treatment, remaining liver volume may be reduced and liver function compromised; therefore, more meticulous evaluation of the risk of hepatic deterioration is required, and treatment decisions are best made through multidisciplinary discussion. In summary, prior to SBRT, liver function should be assessed by integrating multiple indicators rather than relying on a single tool, and treatment indications and radiation dose should be determined accordingly.

###### **[Recommendations]**

1. Liver function should be assessed prior to SBRT by integrating clinical findings, laboratory tests, and imaging studies.

2. The Child-Pugh score and the ALBI score may be used to assess liver function prior to SBRT.

#### **Medication management during stereotactic body radiation therapy**

During SBRT, medications should be used appropriately to prevent reactivation of viral hepatitis and to minimize adverse events, while drugs that may aggravate liver function should be avoided whenever possible.

In previous studies, the incidence of chronic hepatitis B reactivation during external-beam radiation therapy (EBRT) has been reported as 22.2-24.6%, classifying EBRT as a high-risk treatment, with the dose-volume relationship of the irradiated liver identified as a risk factor.35,36 Furthermore, the risk of hepatitis B virus (HBV) reactivation has been reported to be more than two-fold higher when EBRT is combined with TACE than with EBRT alone.37,38

In HCC patients undergoing EBRT, HBV reactivation rates were reported to be 21.8% in the control group and 0% in the lamivudine prophylaxis group, with corresponding ALT elevation rates of 12.5% vs. 2.3%, respectively, indicating a significantly higher rate in the control group.39 Another multicenter retrospective study of 133 patients receiving EBRT alone similarly reported HBV reactivation rates of 29.4% and 0% in patients without and with antiviral therapy, respectively.40 Accordingly, prophylactic antiviral therapy is recommended for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg)-positive patients undergoing SBRT. The KLCANCC HCC clinical practice guidelines also recommend prophylactic antiviral therapy for HBV-infected patients receiving highrisk anticancer treatments, including EBRT.2 Although prospective data specifically addressing the risk of HBV reactivation after SBRT remain limited, the consistent evidence from EBRT and combination therapy supports consideration of prophylactic antiviral therapy in HBsAg-positive patients undergoing SBRT.

##### Premedication and supportive medications prior to stereotactic body radiation therapy

Because adverse events during SBRT are infrequent, specific premedications are generally not required, in contrast to systemic chemotherapy. However, systemic symptoms such as fatigue, anorexia, and nausea may occur during treatment; in such cases, supportive measures, including adequate nutritional support and symptomatic therapy with antiemetics, may be considered. Such supportive care can help maintain quality of life and improve treatment continuity.

##### Management of hepatotoxic medications during stereotactic body radiation therapy

The use of potentially hepatotoxic agents during SBRT may increase the risk of hepatic deterioration and should therefore be discontinued or substituted whenever feasible (Table 1). When concomitant medications are used, regular evaluation of their efficacy and adverse effects is necessary, as radiation therapy may cause pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic alterations.

The risk of hepatic deterioration during SBRT may be increased by underlying liver disease and concomitant medications. Therefore, prophylactic antiviral therapy before and after SBRT, appropriate supportive care, and close monitoring of hepatotoxic medications are required. A multidisciplinary approach is essential to establish a treatment plan that maximizes the preservation of liver function and the safety of the procedure.

In hepatitis C virus (HCV)-related HCC, increases in HCV replication or hepatitis flares may occur after cancer treatment, although progression to hepatic failure or death is rare. A prospective observational study reported that elevations in HCV RNA may occur after cancer therapy, but, unlike HBV, the course is generally indolent and no association with hepatic failure or liver-related mortality has been established.41 Nevertheless, monitoring of HCV RNA titers and the development of hepatitis flares is warranted during the treatment of HCV-related HCC.

In addition, because HCC lesions are characterized on dynamic contrast-enhanced CT through the arterial, portal venous, and delayed phases, the use of contrast media during treatment planning CT is recommended; the contrast phase that provides the most informative depiction for target delineation should be selected according to the chosen respiratory motion management technique and the imaging characteristics of the tumor.48 Contrast use during treatment planning CT may be limited in patients with reduced renal function and should be avoided in patients with a previous immediate-type hypersensitivity reaction to iodinated contrast media or with concurrent acute thyrotoxic crisis; in such cases, alternative contrast agents or emergency-response preparations, including antihistamine premedication, may be required.46 In patients with severe renal impairment (estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] <30 mL/min/1.73 m2), acute kidney injury, or a history of moderate-to-severe contrast hypersensitivity, premedication such as intravenous hydration and antihistamine administration should be considered.46 For patients taking metformin who also have impaired renal function, metformin should be withheld for up to 48 hours after contrast administration.46 When contrast use is contraindicated and target delineation must be performed using non-contrast CT alone, it is first necessary to verify whether the low-density tumor is clearly visible. However, since the boundaries of the lesion are often difficult to distinguish, options such as image registration with diagnostic CT or MRI should be considered to assist in tumor volume delineation. In cases where such image registration is re-

quired, examination schedules must be adjusted to keep the interval between diagnostic imaging and treatment planning CT as short as possible to ensure accurate tumor volume definition.

In addition, four-dimensional (4D) CT may be performed during simulation so that respiratory-induced tumor motion is incorporated into the treatment plan. Because of its repeated image acquisition, 4D CT increases patient radiation exposure compared with conventional CT by approximately 60% based on the volumetric CT dose index, although the dose generally remains less than twice that of conventional CT. The scan range and number of repeat acquisitions should therefore be minimized to the extent clinically necessary.49

In the expert survey on treatment planning CT practice for SBRT (multiple responses allowed), 4D CT was used by 96.8% of respondents, whereas breath-hold CT and slow CT were used by 16.1% and 6.5%, respectively. None of the respondents reported acquiring CT without considering respiratory motion.

#### **Required equipment specifications**

SBRT utilizes ultra-hypofractionation with high doses per fraction; therefore, high-precision radiotherapy equipment is essential. While specific requirements may vary depending on tumor size, intensity modulation is generally recommended to ensure reliable, high-precision, and high-dose radiation delivery. To achieve this, high-resolution multileaf collimators are necessary to create a steep dose gradient between the target and the surrounding healthy tissues. Furthermore, image guidance systems, including kV fluoroscopy, CT, or MRI, are required to identify and compensate for changes in tumor position, patient movement, and respiratory-induced tumor motion before and during treatment. Additionally, a high dose rate of 1,000 monitor units/ min or more is preferred to shorten beam-on time. This helps maintain consistency in dose delivery by reducing uncertainties associated with patient discomfort, general condition, and variations in respiratory patterns.

Particle beam therapy, including proton and carbon-ion therapy, can reduce the volume of the remaining liver exposed to moderate or low radiation doses. Therefore, particle beam therapy may be used for SBRT in patients with HCC, who often have underlying liver disease.52,53 However, it should be noted that uncertainties in range, tissue heterogeneity, intestinal gas, and the use

of contrast agents can affect the range and position of the Bragg peak. If accurate positioning of the Bragg peak, a defining characteristic and advantage of particle beam therapies, cannot be ensured, there is a higher risk of both reduced tumor control and increased toxicity compared to photon radiotherapy.54,55

#### **Image registration and fusion for treatment planning**

Image registration for treatment planning CT should be performed using end-exhalation CT acquired during free breathing, breath-hold CT, or 4D CT scans acquired during simulation, according to the respiratory motion management strategy selected for each patient. If necessary, diagnostic CT, MRI, or positron emission tomography/CT images may be fused with the treatment planning CT to assist in target volume delineation. If the tumor identified on the treatment planning CT shows significant changes compared to pretreatment diagnostic images, additional diagnostic CT or MRI scans should be acquired. Then, these images should be registered to the treatment planning CT to ensure a more accurate assessment of the current tumor extent.

#### **Target volume definition**

The gross tumor volume (GTV) is defined based on findings from dynamic CT and MRI, particularly arterial phase hyperenhancement and portal venous or delayed phase washout. The clinical target volume is generally considered identical to the GTV because preserving the remaining liver is critical, and additional margins are already accounted for by respiratory motion.52,53,56,57 When treating a site after TACE, consideration should be given to including both the residual HCC and the iodized oil-retaining lesions within the GTV.58 The ITV is established by expanding the GTV to encompass the specific respiratory cycle range identified on 4D CT. Finally, the planning target volume (PTV) is typically defined by adding a 5-10 mm margin to the ITV, accounting for patient-specific setup variations and institution- or device-specific uncertainties.

#### **Definition of organs at risk**

The primary organs at risk (OARs) include the whole liver (or remaining liver) and the adjacent gastrointestinal tract (esophagus, stomach, duodenum, small intestine, and large intestine). Additional OARs include the spinal cord, kidneys, skin, chest wall, lungs, and heart. To define the liver volume accurately, contrast-enhanced CT should be used to clearly delineate the hepat-

ic vascular structures and tumor boundaries.

Recently, various artificial intelligence-based auto-segmentation technologies have been introduced, improving efficiency and reproducibility in the field of radiotherapy. Auto-segmentation results, however, must be verified by an experienced radiation oncologist before being applied to the actual treatment planning of individual patients.

Additionally, for appropriate image guidance and respiratory motion management, internal surrogates should be selected and defined. This typically involves contouring the diaphragm or liver dome, iodized oil deposits, surgical clips, or other implanted fiducial markers.57,59

#### **Treatment planning**

In SBRT for HCC, the evidence regarding the dose-response relationship for local control is not yet fully established. The target dose is generally a biologically effective dose (BED10) of 80-100 Gy or higher, assuming an α/β ratio of 10 for HCC, delivered in 3-5 fractions.1 Here, BED10 is a standardized metric that allows for the comparison of actual biological effects on tissues on a uniform scale, regardless of variations in fractionation schedules or physical doses. When establishing the plan, the pretreatment liver function must be prioritized. To preserve remaining liver function, it is recommended that at least 700 mL of the remaining liver receive ≤15 Gy, or that the mean liver dose (MLD) be limited to 10-15 Gy or less.47,56,57

Dose constraints for all other critical OARs, including the gastrointestinal tract, spinal cord, and kidneys, should be set based on international guidelines. #### **Dose prescription**

The primary objective of dose prescription in SBRT is to ensure that the PTV is covered by the 95% isodose line of the prescribed dose, following standard radiotherapy principles. However, “hot spots” within the target volume, even those exceeding 150% of the prescribed dose, may be acceptable, provided they do not extend into the surrounding normal tissues.63-65 Furthermore, the simultaneous integrated boost technique can be utilized to deliver a higher dose to the core of the GTV while tapering to a lower dose at the PTV periphery. Conversely, the simultaneous integrated protection technique can be applied to modulate and reduce the dose in areas immediately adjacent to critical OARs, thereby improving normal tissue sparing.66-68

#### **Post-treatment patient care**

##### Treatment-related complications

SBRT for HCC is generally associated with a relatively low incidence of severe treatment-related complications in patients with preserved liver function. In a study evaluating quality of life (QoL) in 222 patients enrolled in phase 1/2 clinical trials, SBRT did not significantly affect overall QoL. Fatigue and decreased appetite worsened transiently at 1 month after treatment but returned to baseline by 3 months.78

Potential acute complications (within 3 months of treatment completion) following SBRT for intrahepatic lesions include systemic symptoms (e.g., fatigue and anorexia), gastrointestinal symptoms (e.g., nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain), and laboratory abnormalities, including elevations in liver enzymes (AST and ALT), hyperbilirubinemia, and hematologic toxicities (e.g., leukopenia and thrombocytopenia).56,57 Reported late complications (beyond 3 months after treatment) include elevations in liver enzymes, deterioration of liver function (e.g., worsening of the Child-Pugh score or ALBI grade), gastrointestinal toxicities (e.g., gastric or duodenal ulcers), radiation pneumonitis (predominantly involving the right lower lobe), rib fractures, intercostal neuritis, and biliary strictures.56,57,79 However, these severe late complications are largely preventable through appropriate patient selection and meticulous treatment planning. Therefore, the risk of treatment-related complications should be assessed comprehensively, taking into account tumor location and size, the prescribed radiation dose, and other relevant treatment parameters.

##### Management of complications

Most complications following SBRT for HCC are mild, corresponding to common terminology criteria for adverse events grade 1 or 2, and typically resolve with conservative management and/or spontaneously over time.80,81 Rarely, gastrointestinal bleeding may require endoscopic hemostasis or blood transfusion. In cases where biliary stricture results in secondary complications such as jaundice, interventions including biliary drainage or biliary stent placement may be considered.

Prevention of complications is of paramount importance. Strict adherence to OAR dose constraints and careful patient selection based on a comprehensive pretreatment assessment of liver func-

tion are essential. In patients with compromised liver function, dose de-escalation and/or adjustments to the total prescribed dose or dose per fraction should be considered.44,82,83

#### **Follow-up management after stereotactic body radiation therapy**

##### Surveillance for recurrence and liver function

After SBRT for HCC, radiologic imaging (CT or MRI) and serum tumor markers, including alpha-fetoprotein and protein induced by vitamin K absence-II, are used to assess disease status and detect recurrence. Regular laboratory tests should also be performed to assess liver function and monitor treatment-related complications, including AST/ALT, bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, albumin, prothrombin time, and complete blood count. Additional evaluations may be performed as clinically indicated based on the patient’s symptoms.

##### **Hepatocellular carcinoma recurrence**

Recurrence after curative-intent treatment for HCC is most frequent during the first year after treatment and decreases thereafter. After 2 years, the risk of developing de novo HCC has been reported to exceed the risk of recurrence from the treated lesion.84 The 2022 KLCA-NCC practice guidelines for HCC recommend post-treatment evaluations at 6 to 8-week intervals. In patients who achieve a complete response, follow-up evaluations every 2-6 months are recommended until 2 years after treatment. Although studies specifically addressing tumor marker kinetics and post-SBRT surveillance strategies are limited, given the high complete response rates of treated lesions and the evidence that radiologic responses may take several months to fully manifest, it is reasonable to follow the KLCA-NCC surveillance schedule for monitoring intrahepatic recurrence after SBRT.

##### **Post-treatment liver function monitoring**

Elevations in liver enzymes and deterioration of liver function after SBRT have been reported in approximately 5%–35% of patients, depending on the study.44,56,85 When assessed using the Child–Pugh score, changes are most pronounced at approximately 3 months after treatment. While mild deterioration may persist up to 12 months, it is generally of limited clinical significance.86-88 However, in patients with impaired baseline liver function, the risk of post-treatment liver enzyme elevation or hepatic functional deterioration is higher, and recovery may be delayed.89 Therefore, close monitoring for acute hepatic complications is warranted during the first 3 months after treatment, and liver

function should be assessed serially using both the Child–Pugh score and ALBI grade.

##### Imaging surveillance

Imaging surveillance after SBRT should be guided by the post-treatment imaging findings of the lesion.90 When masslike enhancement that has increased in size compared with pretreatment imaging, or newly developed masslike enhancement, is observed, it should be considered suggestive of clinically significant tumor activity, and subsequent treatment strategies should be determined through multidisciplinary discussion. In such cases, the timing of repeat imaging should be individualized according to the planned treatment strategy. For masslike enhancing lesions that remain stable or have decreased in size, as well as those in which enhancement has completely disappeared, follow-up imaging at approximately 3-month intervals is recommended. The use of the same imaging modality is recommended whenever feasible to ensure accurate comparison. However, alternative imaging modalities or contrast agents may be used when a diagnostic benefit is anticipated. In addition, when other intrahepatic lesions are present outside the lesion treated with SBRT, the follow-up imaging interval may be adjusted accordingly.

In the expert survey, 95.2% of respondents agreed that the interval for post-treatment imaging follow-up should be individualized based on the imaging findings of the treated lesion and the development of new lesions. Accordingly, this consensus recommends that the imaging follow-up interval for tumor response assessment after SBRT be individualized according to the post-treatment imaging findings of the lesion.

#### **Considerations in tumor response assessment after stereotactic body radiation therapy**

SBRT is a treatment modality that induces tumor cell death through the delivery of high-dose radiation. The death of HCC cells after SBRT occurs gradually over several months through delayed cell death and radiation-induced cellular senescence rather than immediate necrosis.91-94 According to the literature, tumor enhancement generally diminishes over 6-12 months,57,95-99 and tumor size gradually decreases over 3-12 months (Fig. 6).97-100 Some studies have reported cases in which lesion enhancement persisted for up to 24 months after treatment but were ultimately confirmed as pathologic complete necrosis without additional treatment.98 Furthermore, considering the high local control rates of SBRT reported in the literature, masslike enhancement observed on imaging shortly after treatment does not necessarily indicate clinically significant tumor viability. These unique response characteristics should be taken into account when assessing tumor response after SBRT.

In addition, a focal liver reaction may be observed in the hepatic parenchyma surrounding the SBRT irradiation field, representing the imaging manifestation of radiation-induced hepatitis. The most common finding is geographic arterial phase hyperenhancement around the treated lesion, which appears 3-6 months after treatment and may persist for up to 12 months (Fig. 7).97,101103 This finding is thought to result from a compensatory increase in arterial blood flow due to small venous occlusion. Subsequently, progressive fibrosis may lead to chronic radiation-induced changes, including delayed enhancement, capsular retraction, and liver atrophy.103 Because these changes may mimic the imaging features of residual or recurrent HCC, it is essential to recognize them as part of the normal post-radiation course and to interpret them by comprehensively considering their morphologic characteristics and evolution over time.

#### **Selection of imaging modality**

Dynamic contrast-enhanced CT is widely used as the standard imaging modality for post-treatment surveillance of HCC and is useful for evaluating tumor size and enhancement patterns. However, after SBRT, accurate assessment of actual changes in lesion size may be challenging because of radiation-induced pa-

renchymal changes in the liver.102,104,105 Dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI generally provides higher sensitivity than CT for the diagnosis of HCC; however, evidence regarding its utility for response assessment after SBRT remains limited. Furthermore, contrast-enhanced MRI shares similar limitations with CT, as evaluation of the treated lesion may be hindered by radiation-induced changes in the surrounding hepatic parenchyma.95 In MRI using hepatocyte-specific contrast agents, the HBP, which provides the highest sensitivity for HCC detection, is also limited for response assessment after SBRT, as the signal intensity of the hepatic parenchyma surrounding the lesion is concomitantly reduced. In contrast, T2-weighted imaging and DWI, which are uniquely available with MRI, may aid in assessment, as new high signal intensity and a decrease in the apparent diffusion coefficient may suggest significant tumor viability.106 In addition, MRI

offers the advantage of detecting newly developed intrahepatic lesions with high sensitivity while also facilitating tumor response assessment.

To date, no clear evidence indicates which imaging modality is superior for response assessment after SBRT. In the expert survey, the majority of respondents (71.4% strongly agree and 26.2% agree) supported the complementary use of dynamic contrast-enhanced CT and MRI. Accordingly, the complementary use of both imaging modalities is recommended for tumor response assessment after SBRT.
