# Korean Clinical Practice Guidelines for Interstitial Lung Disease: Part 2. Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis

## Abstract

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a chronic, progressive, fibrosing interstitial pneumonia marked by declining lung function, severe dyspnea, and a poor prognosis. Antifibrotic agents have improved outcomes in patients with IPF, but diagnosing the disease, monitoring its course, and managing it over the long term remain challenging. Since the publication of the first Korean clinical practice guideline for interstitial lung disease in 2018, research has advanced substantially and has reshaped the clinical management of IPF. This updated guideline draws on evidence published over the past 5 years and covers the epidemiology, pathogenesis, risk factors, clinical manifestations, diagnosis, treatment, comorbidities, prognosis, and acute exacerbations of IPF. Expert consensus was also used to formulate key clinical questions, and systematic reviews and meta-analyses were then performed to reassess how antifibrotic agents affect lung function decline, mortality, and acute exacerbations in patients with IPF. This guideline aims to standardize the clinical management of IPF, improve diagnostic accuracy, and encourage the appropriate use of available treatments. It is intended for physicians, other healthcare professionals who care for patients with IPF, and healthcare policymakers in Korea.

## Introduction

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is the most common form of idiopathic interstitial pneumonia (IIP). Progressive pulmonary fibrosis gradually reduces lung function and causes exertional dyspnea, and the disease predominantly affects older men and carries a poor prognosis. IPF remains a major clinical challenge because of its progressive course, difficulty in early diagnosis, and limited therapeutic options.

In 2000, the American Thoracic Society (ATS) and the European Respiratory Society (ERS) issued the first consensus statement on the diagnosis and treatment of IPF, which laid the foundation for standardized care. The ATS and ERS have since worked with the Japanese Respiratory Society (JRS) and the Latin American Thoracic Society (ALAT) to develop successive clinical practice guidelines for diagnosing and managing IPF.

In Korea, the Korean Academy of Tuberculosis and Respiratory Diseases (KATRD) published the first clinical practice guideline for interstitial lung disease (ILD) in 2018 through its ILD Research Group. That guideline rested on a systematic literature review and meta-analysis of studies that appeared after the 2011 ATS/ERS/JRS/ALAT guidelines, and an English version followed in 2019 to reach an international readership. Meanwhile, the ATS, ERS, JRS, and ALAT revised the diagnostic criteria for IPF in 2018 and revised them again in 2022 to incorporate emerging evidence.

In Korea, evidence accumulated since the 2018 guidelines, together with evolving diagnostic approaches, made an update necessary. The first revised edition was therefore published in 2023 to provide accurate and current recommendations for diagnosing IPF and to support individualized treatment decisions that reflect patient values and preferences. It revised the diagnostic approach to IPF and added sections on nonpharmacologic management and IPF-associated comorbidities. The present English edition covers the updated content on epidemiology, diagnosis, treatment, comorbidities, acute exacerbations, and lung transplantation.

This guideline is intended primarily to help clinicians make evidence-based decisions when diagnosing, treating, and monitoring patients with confirmed or suspected IPF. It also offers recommendations for assessing long-term prognosis.

Building on the 2018 edition, the revised IPF section of the ILD clinical practice guideline pursues two goals:

(1) To incorporate the clinical research findings that have accumulated since the 2018 edition was published.

(2) To develop consensus-based recommendations through multidisciplinary collaboration, drawing on input from the Korean Society of Radiology and the Korean Society of Pathologists.

This clinical practice guideline was developed through a systematic process (Supplementary Materials and Methods, Supplementary Tables S1-5, and Supplementary Figures S1-9).

## Epidemiology

IPF is the most common subtype of the IIPs, a group of ILDs with no identifiable cause. In a Korean study of 2,186 patients given a diagnosis of IIP between 2003 and 2007, 77.1% had IPF, and a Western study similarly classified 68% of IIP cases as IPF.

In Korea, the annual incidence of IPF was 1.84 per 100,000 individuals among cases diagnosed between 1992 and 2010. Studies from Taiwan and Japan reported annual incidences of 1.6 and 2.23 per 100,000 individuals and prevalences of 6.4 and 10.0 per 100,000 individuals, respectively. After the international diagnostic guideline was revised in 2011, Korean studies reported a higher annual incidence of 12.9–13.1 per 100,000 individuals and a prevalence of 35.2–38.9 per 100,000 individuals.

Studies from the United States and Canada reported annual incidences of 17.4 and 25.8 per 100,000 individuals and prevalences of 63 and 78.4 per 100,000 individuals, respectively. Both the incidence and the prevalence of IPF rise with age and are higher in men. In a nationwide Korean cohort, the 1,345 patients given a diagnosis of IPF in 2018 had a mean age of 68.8 years, and 76% were men. The Australian IPF Registry likewise reported a mean age of 71.9 years, with men accounting for 69% of participants. Participants in international multicenter clinical trials have also been 65 years of age or older on average.

Recent studies have linked IPF to other chronic diseases. In one retrospective study, 88% of patients with IPF had at least one comorbidity, and cardiovascular disease and lung cancer influenced mortality most strongly. Meta-analyses have identified IPF as a significant risk factor for lung cancer, particularly peripheral tumors, of which squamous cell carcinoma is the most common histologic type.

Reported prevalences of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) among patients with IPF vary widely, from 6% to 67%. Approximately 8% of cases present as combined pulmonary fibrosis and emphysema (CPFE), which differs from typical IPF in that lung volumes are preserved while diffusion capacity is reduced and pulmonary hypertension (PH), severe dyspnea, and hypoxemia are present.

Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) has also been associated with IPF, although reported prevalences vary across studies. Obstructive sleep apnea is another notable comorbidity; one Korean study reported a prevalence of 64.9%.

## Diagnosis

### 1. Clinical features

IPF should be considered in adults who present with unexplained chronic exertional dyspnea, cough, inspiratory crackles, and clubbing. The disease usually develops after the age of 50, occurs more often in men, and is strongly associated with a history of smoking. Most cases follow a chronic, progressive course, and spontaneous improvement is rare. Complications such as right heart failure and peripheral edema may develop at advanced stages.

### 2. High-resolution computed tomography findings

High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) characteristically shows a usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) pattern in IPF. Imaging patterns on HRCT fall into four categories: UIP, probable UIP, indeterminate for UIP, and alternative diagnosis (Table 1).

A UIP pattern is defined by honeycombing that predominates in the basal and peripheral lung zones and is often accompanied by traction bronchiectasis or bronchiolectasis (Figure 1). A UIP pattern on HRCT corresponds closely to histopathologic UIP findings.

A probable UIP pattern shows reticular abnormalities with traction bronchiectasis or bronchiolectasis, again predominantly in the basal and subpleural regions, and no features that suggest an alternative diagnosis. Such imaging findings frequently correspond to a histopathologic UIP pattern (Figure 2).

The indeterminate for UIP category applies when computed tomography (CT) shows fibrosis that lacks features distinctive enough to support a specific diagnosis. The alternative diagnosis pattern, by contrast, shows features such as cysts, mosaic attenuation, and ground-glass opacities that point away from UIP and toward another diagnosis (Figures 3, 4).

### 3. Histopathologic findings

On low-power examination, a UIP pattern characteristically combines normal areas, interstitial inflammation, fibrosis, and honeycombing within the same specimen (Table 2 and Figure 5). As with HRCT findings, histopathologic findings fall into four categories: UIP, probable UIP, indeterminate for UIP, and alternative diagnosis.

A histopathologic UIP pattern comprises fibrotic lesions distributed mainly in subpleural or septal locations, advanced fibrotic changes such as architectural distortion and honeycombing, and early fibrotic findings such as fibroblast foci, with no features that suggest an alternative diagnosis. Honeycomb areas consist of cystic fibrotic lesions that are filled with mucus and inflammatory cells and lined by bronchiolar epithelium, and smooth muscle hyperplasia of the interstitium commonly accompanies areas of fibrosis and honeycombing. Because other diseases can also produce a UIP pattern, differential diagnosis is essential, particularly against ILD associated with connective tissue disease, chronic hypersensitivity pneumonitis (extrinsic allergic alveolitis), and pneumoconiosis (particularly asbestosis).

### 4. Diagnostic approach

The diagnostic criteria and the algorithm (overview) for IPF are shown in Table 3 and Figure 6. Accurate diagnosis requires a multidisciplinary discussion among experienced pulmonologists, radiologists, and pathologists, in which other causes are excluded.

In patients with suspected ILD, clinicians should take a careful medical history and perform a physical examination to identify known triggers of ILD, such as medications, connective tissue diseases, or environmental exposures. Once chest HRCT has been obtained, a multidisciplinary team decides whether additional tests, such as bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) or surgical lung biopsy, are needed.

When no cause can be identified, IPF can be diagnosed without biopsy if HRCT shows characteristic findings, namely a UIP or probable UIP pattern. Otherwise, biopsy is required, and the diagnosis rests on the combination of HRCT and histopathologic findings (Figure 6).

#### 1) History taking and basic evaluation

IPF should be suspected in older patients (particularly those aged ≥60 years) who present with chronic dyspnea or dry cough together with physical findings such as clubbing or inspiratory crackles on auscultation. Increased opacities in the lower lung zones on chest radiography further support this suspicion. Detailed history taking is also essential in younger patients aged 40 to 60 years, in whom a family history of pulmonary fibrosis raises the possibility of IPF.

A thorough occupational history should be taken to rule out smoking-related and occupational lung diseases, and the environmental exposure history should be reviewed to assess the possibility of hypersensitivity pneumonitis. Medication use and any history of radiation therapy should also be investigated. Because connective tissue diseases may be involved, symptoms affecting the joints and skin warrant careful evaluation. ILD can precede extrapulmonary manifestations such as arthritis or skin changes, so even asymptomatic patients should undergo autoantibody testing that includes antinuclear antibody and rheumatoid factor. When other diseases are suspected, additional autoantibody tests, including anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP), anti-Sjögren’s syndrome-related antigen A/B (anti-SSA/SSB), anti-topoisomerase I (anti-Scl-70), and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), should be performed as appropriate.

#### 2) HRCT

Chest HRCT is essential to the diagnostic process for IPF and is a sensitive technique for detecting changes in the lung parenchyma. It is performed without contrast, using thin-section volumetric HRCT images (≤1.5 mm slice thickness). Additional imaging in the expiratory phase and in the prone position is recommended to assess associated conditions such as air trapping or atelectasis; these techniques help distinguish ILDs involving the small airways or pseudolesions. When IPF of unknown cause is suspected after thorough history taking and autoantibody testing, HRCT should be the first diagnostic step, and a multidisciplinary approach then determines whether additional tests, such as surgical lung biopsy, are needed.

#### 3) Bronchoalveolar lavage

In patients with IPF, BAL often shows a higher proportion of neutrophils than in healthy individuals or in patients with other lung diseases, although similar findings occur in connective tissue disease-related lung disease and other conditions. When chest CT does not show a UIP pattern, analyzing the cellular fraction and the CD4/CD8 ratio in BAL fluid can help differentiate IPF from other ILDs.

#### 4) Surgical lung biopsy

The need for surgical lung biopsy should be determined after HRCT and multidisciplinary discussion. Because histopathologic patterns may differ between lobes in the same patient, tissue samples should be taken from at least two separate lobes. Any decision to proceed must weigh the diagnostic benefit against the procedural risk, taking patient age, physiologic impairment, and comorbidities into account.

#### 5) Transbronchial lung cryobiopsy

Transbronchial lung cryobiopsy (TBLC) performed by bronchoscopy has recently been introduced as a tissue-sampling method for diagnosing ILD. One study that compared TBLC with conventional surgical lung biopsy found no significant difference in diagnostic yield or complications between the two methods. Because institutional expertise varies and the procedure has not been standardized, TBLC should be considered an alternative to surgical lung biopsy only in centers with substantial experience in the technique.

## Treatment

Pirfenidone is an oral antifibrotic agent that attenuates the decline in forced vital capacity (FVC) in clinical studies. This protective effect on lung function has been observed both in patients with an FVC <80% and in those with an FVC ≥80%, and it persists regardless of the degree of FVC impairment. In the CAPACITY trials and the RECAP54 extension study, FVC declined more slowly in the pirfenidone groups than in the control group, and the rate of decline slowed further in control patients after they crossed over to pirfenidone. The benefit extended across subgroups defined by age (≤65, >65, and >75 years), sex, race, diffusing capacity, and 6-minute walk test results.

In the Czech IPF cohort, the 5-year survival rate among patients with IPF who received pirfenidone was 55.9%, significantly higher than the 31.5% observed in those who did not receive treatment. Overall mortality and IPF-related mortality were also significantly lower in the pirfenidone group than in controls.

Nintedanib similarly protects lung function in patients with an FVC ≥90% and in those with an FVC <90%. A meta-analysis of the TOMORROW trial (2011) and the INPULSIS-1 and INPULSIS-2 trials (2014) found that nintedanib significantly reduced on-treatment mortality.

A pooled analysis of the CAPACITY 1, CAPACITY 2, ASCEND, INPULSIS-1, and INPULSIS-2 clinical trials showed that antifibrotic agents significantly slowed lung function decline relative to control treatment.

The 2018 KATRD clinical practice guideline for ILD recommended antifibrotic agents such as pirfenidone and nintedanib for preventing FVC decline, with a high level of evidence. The 2023 revision framed the efficacy of these agents as key clinical questions (KQs) addressing lung function and mortality; summaries of the evidence for each KQ appear in the Supplementary Materials and Methods.

- KQ 1-1. In patients with IPF, does pirfenidone protect against FVC decline compared with the control group?

## Nonpharmacologic Therapy

IPF is a chronic, progressive fibrotic lung disease that currently has no curative treatment other than lung transplantation, so healthcare providers must manage it along several dimensions to preserve lung function and quality of life. Such care combines antifibrotic drug therapy to inhibit disease progression, pulmonary rehabilitation, oxygen therapy, symptomatic treatment of cough and dyspnea, management of comorbidities and complications, and education and counseling about treatment goals and disease progression (Figure 7).

## Comorbidities

Patients with IPF may have a wide range of respiratory and nonrespiratory comorbidities, including pulmonary, gastrointestinal, cardiovascular and cerebrovascular, metabolic, and psychiatric conditions, which clinicians often overlook. In a recent prospective cohort study of 3,580 patients with IPF, 91.3% had at least one comorbidity and 37.8% had four or more. Shared risk factors such as smoking, advanced age, and male sex probably explain this clustering. Comorbidities aggravate symptoms and shorten survival, and they account for approximately 30% of deaths in this population; PH, lung cancer, and cardiovascular disease in particular are associated with shorter survival. The 2023 revised edition highlights the comorbidities of greatest clinical importance in IPF (Table 4).

### 1. Respiratory diseases

#### 1) Lung cancer

IPF is an independent risk factor for lung cancer even after adjustment for key factors such as smoking. Lung cancer occurs more than eight times as often in patients with IPF as in the general population, and its estimated prevalence in this group ranges from 3% to 48%. Data from the Korean National Health Insurance Service showed a lung cancer prevalence of 6.4% and a 5-year cumulative incidence of 7% in patients with IPF. Mortality is more than five times higher in patients with IPF and lung cancer than in those without lung cancer, partly because surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy can all exacerbate IPF. Interestingly, pirfenidone and nintedanib have shown antitumor effects, which warrants further investigation.

#### 2) Pulmonary hypertension

PH occurs in approximately 14% of patients with IPF and mild restrictive lung disease and in 30%–50% of those with severe IPF. PH is associated with more than a threefold increase in mortality and reduces quality of life and exercise capacity. Patients with IPF should be evaluated for PH when they have dyspnea or hypoxemia disproportionate to their lung function, signs of right heart failure, a marked reduction in diffusing capacity, or radiologic findings such as pulmonary artery enlargement or right ventricular hypertrophy.

#### 3) Emphysema or COPD

The prevalence of emphysema in IPF ranges from 8% to 51%. CPFE, characterized by upper-lobe emphysema and lower-lobe fibrosis, is present in approximately 8% to 30% of patients with IPF. It carries a higher incidence of lung cancer and PH than IPF alone, although mortality is comparable to that of IPF without emphysema. No specific treatment exists for CPFE, but general measures such as oxygen therapy, pulmonary rehabilitation, and inhaled bronchodilators for coexisting COPD can be applied, and nintedanib has shown some efficacy in patients with CPFE enrolled in IPF clinical trials.

#### 4) Sleep apnea

Sleep apnea is notably common in patients with IPF, with prevalences of 59% to 88% in international studies and 64.9% at a single Korean institution. Reduced lung volume increases upper airway instability and resistance and thereby promotes sleep apnea, and the resulting intermittent hypoxemia may in turn aggravate pulmonary fibrosis. In one study, patients with IPF and moderate-to-severe sleep apnea who adhered to continuous positive airway pressure therapy for 1 year had significantly better quality of life, sleep quality, and survival than nonadherent patients.

### 2. Nonrespiratory diseases

#### 1) GERD

GERD is common in patients with IPF and often occurs without typical reflux symptoms; one study found asymptomatic GERD in approximately 50% of patients with IPF. GERD may contribute to the development and progression of IPF and frequently causes chronic cough. Recent large-scale studies and meta-analyses, however, have shown that neither medical nor surgical treatment of GERD significantly affects IPF progression or mortality. On this basis, the 2022 updated guidelines conditionally recommend against treating GERD solely to improve respiratory symptoms in patients with IPF.

#### 2) Cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases

Cardiovascular disease is present in approximately 78% of patients with IPF, and ischemic heart disease shows the strongest association with mortality. A Finnish study found that acute exacerbation was the leading cause of death in patients with IPF (67.5%), followed by ischemic heart disease (14.8%), and the incidence of ischemic heart disease in IPF exceeds that in both the general population and patients with COPD. Chronic hypoxemia and the severe tissue injury and fibrosis associated with IPF may induce a prothrombotic state and thereby account for this excess risk.

#### 3) Anxiety and depression

The chronic, refractory nature of IPF, together with dyspnea and activity limitations, produces a high prevalence of anxiety (30%–50%) and depression (20%–30%) among patients with IPF. Psychological distress in turn worsens dyspnea. A Korean study of 112 patients with IPF reported anxiety in 21.4% and depression in 25.9%. Despite this high prevalence, only about 25% of affected patients receive treatment.

## Acute Exacerbation

This section covers only the updated epidemiology and treatment of acute exacerbations. Among Asian patients, the 3-year incidence of acute exacerbation exceeds 20%; among Korean patients, the 1-year incidence is 6.72% and the 3-year incidence is 16.6%. International guidelines recommend high-dose corticosteroid therapy as a supportive measure for acute exacerbations. No standard dose has been established, but a commonly used regimen is methylprednisolone 500 to 1,000 mg daily for 3 days, followed by gradual tapering.

Some studies have suggested that early use of immunosuppressive agents such as cyclophosphamide and tacrolimus, as well as polymyxin B-immobilized fiber column hemoperfusion, may also be beneficial; however, further study is needed.

Treating GERD has not been shown to prevent acute exacerbations and is therefore not recommended. Supportive measures such as oxygen therapy for dyspnea or hypoxemia are commonly provided. Intubation and mechanical ventilation are generally avoided because of the poor prognosis, except when respiratory failure has a treatable cause or lung transplantation is being considered.

In one meta-analysis, antifibrotic therapy reduced the rate of acute exacerbations in patients with IPF by 37% and delayed their onset compared with no antifibrotic treatment. The present revision therefore framed the efficacy of antifibrotic agents in preventing acute exacerbations as a KQ and addressed it through a systematic literature review and meta-analysis.

- KQ 3. In patients with clinically or histologically diagnosed IPF, does antifibrotic therapy (pirfenidone or nintedanib) reduce the incidence of acute exacerbations compared with no antifibrotic therapy?
- A. We conditionally recommend the use of antifibrotic agents (pirfenidone or nintedanib) in patients with clinically or histologically diagnosed IPF to prevent acute exacerbations. (Evidence level: Moderate, Recommendation grade: Conditional recommendation)

## Lung Transplantation

On the basis of a systematic literature review and meta-analysis conducted during the development of the 2018 Korean guidelines, we suggested that patients with IPF undergo lung transplantation at an appropriate time to improve survival (strong recommendation, moderate confidence in estimates of effect)5. The most common indications for lung transplantation are ILD, COPD, cystic fibrosis, alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, and primary PH, which together account for approximately 85% of lung transplant recipients worldwide; the remaining 15% have various other end-stage pulmonary diseases. In South Korea, IPF accounts for 50% of lung transplantations, followed by post-transplant bronchiolitis obliterans (6%) and bronchiectasis (5%). Unlike in other countries, COPD accounts for only 1% of indications for lung transplantation in Korea.

### 1. Patients requiring lung transplantation

The indications for lung transplantation are as follows: (1) advanced lung disease for which medical treatment is ineffective or unavailable; (2) a greater than 50% risk of death from the lung disease within the next 2 years without transplantation; and (3) an estimated post-transplant survival rate of at least 80% at 5 years.

### 2. Evaluation of potential lung transplant candidates

Evaluating a potential lung transplant candidate is a comprehensive process. It covers the patient’s medical history, physical examination, laboratory tests (e.g., complete blood count and screening for multiple viral infections), and imaging and functional studies (e.g., chest CT, ventilation/perfusion scans, cardiac evaluations, left and right heart catheterization, osteoporosis and cancer screening, and assessments of gastroesophageal reflux and gastrointestinal motility), as well as vaccination status and psychosocial assessment.

A multidisciplinary team at a lung transplant center typically conducts this evaluation, including pulmonologists who specialize in lung transplantation, transplant surgeons, nurses, social workers, and other specialists. The specific tests and evaluations may vary with the patient’s age, medical history, and the protocols of the transplant center.

### 3. Survival rates of lung transplantation

Lung transplantation confers substantial survival benefits: the ISHLT reports global survival rates of 79% at 1 year, 63% at 5 years, and 44% at 10 years. Outcomes differ by underlying condition; IPF is associated with a 5-year survival rate of approximately 50%, whereas patients with cystic fibrosis reach a higher rate of around 70%.

Lung transplantation began in South Korea in 1996, and recent single-center studies have shown promising results, with 1- and 5-year survival rates of approximately 75.5% and 61.8%, respectively, reflecting outcomes that are comparable to or better than global standards.

## Conclusion

This updated Korean guideline for IPF incorporates recent evidence to improve diagnostic accuracy and standardize management. It supports the appropriate use of antifibrotic therapy, which slows FVC decline, reduces mortality, and may lower the risk of acute exacerbations.
