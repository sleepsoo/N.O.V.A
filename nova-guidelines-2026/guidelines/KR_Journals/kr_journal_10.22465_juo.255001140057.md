# Clinical Guideline for the Use of Biodegradable Rectal Spacers During Radiotherapy for Prostate Cancer

## Abstract

### Purpose

Radiotherapy (RT) remains a cornerstone of curative treatment for localized and locally advanced prostate cancer. However, dose escalation to improve tumor control is often constrained by the proximity of the rectum, which increases the risk of gastrointestinal (GI) and genitourinary toxicities. Biodegradable rectal spacers inserted between the prostate and rectum have emerged as an effective approach to reduce rectal radiation exposure. This guideline provides evidence-based recommendations on indications, contraindications, procedural standards, and clinical management for biodegradable rectal spacer insertion during prostate cancer RT.

### Materials and Methods

This guideline was developed by a multidisciplinary expert panel through a systematic review of the literature, analysis of international guidelines (National Comprehensive Cancer Network, European Association of Urology, American Society for Radiation Oncology), and expert consensus among radiation oncologists, radiologists, and urologists with clinical experience in spacer insertion. The strength of each recommendation and the level of evidence were classified according to the modified GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) system.

### Results

Spacer insertion is conditionally recommended (Grade C, Level I) for patients receiving definitive external-beam RT without rectal invasion. It reduces the high-dose rectal irradiation volume (V70–75) by >50%, decreases acute GI toxicity, and helps maintain bowel-related quality of life. However, the benefit for late severe toxicity (grade 2 or higher) remains debated in recent meta-analyses. Contraindications include rectal invasion, anatomical inaccessibility, infection, and material hypersensitivity. Procedures should be performed under local anesthesia in a sterile environment by trained physicians. Short-course antibiotics and simulator-based training, including completion of multiple supervised cases, are advised.

### Conclusion

Biodegradable rectal spacer insertion is clinically validated and effective in reducing acute rectal toxicity. Although pivotal trials demonstrated a favorable procedural safety profile, real-world postmarket data include reports of rare but severe procedural complications. This guideline provides standardized recommendations tailored to Korean clinical practice while remaining consistent with international standards, emphasizing the importance of operator training and careful patient selection.

## INTRODUCTION

Radiotherapy (RT) is one of the most widely adopted curative treatment modalities for patients with localized and locally advanced prostate cancer. Dose escalation improves local tumor control and biochemical recurrencefree survival; however, it simultaneously increases the risk of radiation-induced toxicities to adjacent normal tissues, particularly the rectum and lower urinary tract. The rectum lies immediately posterior to the prostate with only a few millimeters of separation (often <5 mm on imaging), placing it within the high-dose radiation field during RT. Despite modern techniques, gastrointestinal (GI) toxicities (e.g., proctitis/bleeding/fibrosis) still occur—most commonly low-grade—and can affect patient quality of life (QoL) and limit treatment continuity.

To overcome these challenges, the use of biodegradable rectal spacers has been introduced as an innovative technique to physically separate the prostate and rectum. These spacers, composed of biocompatible hydrogels (e.g., SpaceOAR, Barrigel), create a temporary anatomical space that significantly reduces rectal radiation dose without compromising tumor coverage. The first product received U.S. Food and Drug Administration approval in 2015, and since then, this procedure has been widely adopted across many countries. In Korea, it was approved as a new medical technology in 2021 and has been reimbursed under the national health insurance system since 2023.

Multiple randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and metaanalyses have confirmed the clinical benefits of spacer insertion. According to recent meta-analyses, spacer placement reduces high-dose rectal irradiation volume (V70–75), the rectal volume (in cm3) receiving a radiation dose of at least 70 Gy to 75 Gy, by over 50% and significantly lowers acute and late GI toxicities of any grade. Although grade ≥2 toxicity differences were not always statistically significant, all studies demonstrated a trend toward improved bowel-related QoL. Longterm follow-up from Hamstra et al. showed no grade ≥2 rectal toxicity in the spacer group versus 5.7% in controls, with no grade ≥3 events, confirming durable safety.

Although acute genitourinary (GU) toxicity shows minimal difference between groups, some studies have reported improved long-term urinary and sexual function among patients receiving spacers. In addition, the physical separation of the rectum and prostate facilitates radiation treatment planning, enhances target localization accuracy, and contributes to the precision of modern RT techniques such as intensity-modulated RT (IMRT), proton therapy, carbon ion therapy (CIRT), and stereotactic body RT (SBRT). Serious complications are uncommon in contemporary series (typically low single-digit percentages; serious events rare), though isolated infections or rectal wall infiltration have been reported.

Given this evidence, biodegradable rectal spacer insertion is considered a clinically effective technique that requires a careful risk-benefit analysis and rigorous operator training. The present guideline has been developed to provide evidence-based recommendations tailored to Korean clinical settings, including indications, contraindications, procedural standards, and periprocedural management. By standardizing these practices, this guideline aims to improve the quality and safety of prostate cancer RT nationwide.

## METHODS

### 1. Eligibility Criteria (PICOS)

• Population: Patients with diagnosed localized or locally advanced prostate cancer undergoing curative-intent RT (external-beam RT [EBRT] or brachytherapy).

• Intervention: Insertion of a biodegradable rectal spacer (e.g., hydrogel) prior to RT.

• Comparator: RT without rectal spacer insertion (control group).

• Outcomes:

- Dosimetric: rectal dose-volume histograms

- Clinical: Acute and late GI and GU toxicities (any grade and grade≤2), QoL, and procedural complications.

• Study Design: RCTs, prospective cohort studies, and retrospective comparative studies published up to June 2024. Case reports were excluded from the efficacy analysis but reviewed for rare adverse event documentation.

• Databases: PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science.

### 2. Literature Search and Evidence Synthesis

To establish the evidence base for these recommendations, the panel utilized data from a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis previously conducted and published by the authors. A systematic literature search was performed using PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and Web of Science databases for articles published up to June 2024. The review focused on RCTs and prospective/retrospective studies evaluating rectal dosimetry (Dmax, Dmean, V50, V70, etc.) and GI/GU toxicities (acute/late, any grade/grade≥2) in patients receiving prostate cancer RT with or without rectal spacers.

The quality of the included studies was assessed using the RoB 2 (Risk of Bias 2) tool for RCTs and the ROBINS-I (Risk of Bias In Non-randomized Studies - of Interventions) tool for nonrandomized studies. The certainty of evidence was evaluated according to the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach.

### 3. Panel Composition and Consensus Mechanism

The guideline development involved a 18-member multidisciplinary panel comprising urologists (n=9), radiation oncologists (n=7), and radiologists (n=2) from major academic medical centers. The consensus process followed a 3-stage modified Delphi approach:

#### 1) Evidence review & initial symposium

The full panel (N=18) reviewed the systematic review data to establish the preliminary scope of the guideline.

#### 2) Focus group interviews & structured voting

To address clinical questions where high-level evidence was lacking (e.g., specific contraindications, training requirements), a core advisory group of 13 specialists (subset of the panel: 5 urologists, 6 radiation oncologists, 2 radiologists) participated in detailed focus group interviews (FGIs).

- Consensus measurement: Participants responded to structured questionnaires regarding clinical strategies and safety. Consensus was defined based on agreement rates (e.g., 87.5% agreement on the difficulty of independent performance after training) and weighted scoring systems (e.g., defining “rectal-prostate separation” as the #1 success criterion with a weighted score of 46).

#### 3) Final ratification

The draft recommendations, incorporating both the literature evidence and FGI consensus, were circulated to the full panel (N=18). Disagreements were resolved through an iterative revision process where feedback was incorporated until final approval was obtained from all members.

### 4. Grading of Recommendations

Recommendations were classified based on the strength of evidence and the degree of expert consensus using a modified GRADE system (Table 1).

• Grade A (strong recommendation): Supported by highquality evidence (Level I) or strong expert consensus where benefits clearly outweigh risks.

• Grade B (moderate recommendation): Supported by moderate-quality evidence (Level II–III) or expert consensus.

### 5. Summary of Recommendations

Table 2 summarizes key clinical recommendations for biodegradable rectal spacer insertion during prostate cancer RT. Recommendation grades (A–C) and levels of evidence (I– IV) follow the modified GRADE system.

#### 1) Indications

Biodegradable rectal spacers are primarily indicated for patients with localized or locally advanced prostate cancer receiving curative-intent EBRT or brachytherapy, particularly when dose escalation or hypofractionation is planned. By creating a temporary physical separation between the prostate and rectum, spacer insertion reduces rectal dose exposure and thereby mitigates radiation-induced toxicity without compromising tumor control.

Spacer insertion may also be considered in selected highrisk or oligometastatic cases undergoing RT for local control, although data in these settings remain limited. Even in cases with posterior capsular invasion, the procedure can be performed after neoadjuvant systemic therapy (e.g., androgen deprivation therapy) if imaging confirms regression of the tumor and adequate perirectal space has been restored.

Clinical evidence supports a strong recommendation for this indication. A meta-analysis by Kwon et al. demonstrated that the mean rectal volume receiving high-dose radiation (≥70 Gy, V70) was reduced by >50% following spacer insertion, accompanied by significant reductions in both acute and late GI toxicity. Furthermore, the long-term phase III randomized trial by Hamstra et al. reported 0% grade ≥2 rectal toxicity in the spacer group after 3 years, compared with 5.7% in the control arm. These findings strongly support the clinical efficacy and durability of rectal protection.

From a technical standpoint, patients undergoing highdose or hypofractionated RT—such as IMRT, proton therapy, CIRT, or SBRT—derive particular benefit from spacer insertion, especially where steep dose gradients are required near the rectum. The procedure also facilitates precise target localization by minimizing prostate motion, thereby improving overall treatment accuracy and dosimetric outcomes.

Taken together, spacer insertion is a conditionally recommended procedure (Grade C, Level I) for patients undergoing curative-intent EBRT for prostate cancer. This conditional recommendation reflects the panel’s judgment that the benefits (dosimetric advantage, reduced acute toxicity, and improved QoL) should be carefully weighed against the rare but severe real-world procedural risks and the conflicting evidence on late severe toxicity. This is especially true for those with high rectal dose constraints, high-risk anatomy, or clinical scenarios requiring dose escalation.

#### 2) Contraindications and patient selection

While generally safe and well tolerated, spacer placement still requires careful patient selection to optimize benefit and minimize risks. The procedure should not be performed in patients with conditions that significantly increase the risk of complications or diminish the expected therapeutic gain (expert consensus and limited clinical data).

Absolute (consensus-based) contraindications include:

• Rectal wall invasion confirmed by imaging or clinical examination

• Lack of a safely expandable perirectal plane on transrectal ultrasound (TRUS), including cases where hydrodissection fails to create adequate separation

• Active infection or acute inflammatory conditions of the rectum or perineum (e.g., acute bacterial proctitis, perineal abscess, fistula)

• Known hypersensitivity to biodegradable spacer materials such as polyethylene glycol Relative contraindications include:

• History of extensive rectal or perineal surgery leading to anatomical distortion or fibrosis that limits transperineal access

• Inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn disease or ulcerative colitis) in the absence of active perirectal inflammation, with consideration that rectal spacers may provide additional protection in selected patients.

In addition, the procedure should be avoided in patients with a permanent colostomy or severe perineal adhesions that preclude safe transperineal needle insertion (feasibility/ access limitation). The decision to proceed should be made after comprehensive evaluation of the patient’s anatomical feasibility, comorbid conditions, and treatment goals, including preprocedure TRUS/magnetic resonance imaging review and, when in doubt, a trial hydrodissection under ultrasound.

These contraindications are primarily based on expert consensus and recent clinical guidelines, which advise against spacer use in patients with clear rectal wall involvement or extensive posterior capsular invasion. The same safety principles are supported by local expert focus group discussions, where “rectal invasion, limited anatomical space, impaired access, and infection risk” were consistently identified as major exclusion criteria (details of panel composition and consensus methods provided in Methods).

While these recommendations are derived from low-level evidence (Level III), they are considered strong recommendations (Grade A) due to their direct relevance to procedural safety and consistent expert agreement. Careful patient selection in accordance with these criteria is crucial to maintaining high procedural success rates and preventing avoidable complications.

#### 3) Procedure and environment

Biodegradable rectal spacer insertion should be performed under local anesthesia in a sterile procedural environment such as an operating room or an outpatient procedure suite with equivalent aseptic conditions. The patient is positioned in the lithotomy position (or modified lithotomy in dorsal position depending on table design) to facilitate transperineal access under real-time TRUS guidance.

The procedure requires at least 2 trained personnel— a primary operator and an assistant—with experience in TRUS-guided prostate interventions. High-frequency (≥7 MHz) biplanar TRUS is recommended to delineate the Denonvilliers’ space and confirm symmetric gel spread.

Local anesthesia, typically via perineal infiltration with lidocaine, provides adequate pain control in most patients. For those with high anxiety or low pain tolerance, conscious sedation or epidural anesthesia conditionally is recommended as per institutional policy. Strict adherence to aseptic technique is mandatory to minimize infection risk. The hydrogel (e.g., SpaceOAR, Barrigel) is injected into the perirectal (Denonvilliers’) space using a prefilled applicator, creating a separation of approximately 1.0–1.5 cm between the prostate and rectum. The gel remains in place during RT and is gradually absorbed within 6 months.

Concurrent fiducial placement is efficient and recommended when image-guided tracking is required. Both procedures share the same transperineal approach and ultrasound guidance, allowing efficient simultaneous placement. Fiducial markers enable precise target alignment, minimizing intra- and interfractional prostate motion during image-guided RT.

A successful procedure is characterized by symmetrical gel distribution and adequate rectoprostatic separation without intramural or extrarectal injection. The reported overall procedural success rate in the pivotal trial exceeded 99%, with late rectal toxicity rates below 1%.. But this contrasts with real-world postmarket data, which includes reports of severe complications such as rectal wall infiltration, abscess, and fistula. Adherence to standardized protocols, including thorough preprocedural planning and intra-procedural verification of the needle trajectory and hydrodissection test prior to gel injection is essential for consistent and safe outcomes.

This recommendation corresponds to a moderate recommendation (Grade B) based on Level III evidence and expert consensus. Proper procedural standardization and maintenance of aseptic technique are critical for ensuring reproducible safety and efficacy across institutions.

#### 4) Pre- and postprocedural care

Although the risk of infection after biodegradable rectal spacer insertion is very low, appropriate periprocedural management is necessary to further minimize complications. The procedure involves transperineal access and TRUS probe insertion, both of which can introduce rectal flora into the perineal tract.

Prophylactic antibiotics are therefore recommended selectively, particularly for patients with identifiable infection risk factors such as diabetes mellitus, immunosuppression, or rectal mucosal injury. A single preprocedure dose (e.g., cefazolin 1 g intravenous or fluoroquinolone per local resistance data) is adequate; extend ≤3 days only in high-risk patients. This approach aligns with prophylaxis protocols for other transperineal urologic procedures, including prostate biopsy.

Routine antibiotic administration for all patients is not mandatory, as infection rates remain below 1% in published clinical series. However, in institutions with higher background rates of postbiopsy infection or in immunocompromised populations, short-course prophylaxis remains prudent.

After spacer insertion, patients should be observed for acute perineal discomfort, hematuria, or urinary retention, although these symptoms are typically mild and self-limited. Most patients can be discharged the same day. Followup imaging is not routinely required unless malposition, asymmetrical distribution, or unexpected rectal symptoms are suspected. Initial computed tomography simulation for RT planning serves as implicit verification of spacer position.

This recommendation is based on low-level evidence (Level III) and expert consensus, corresponding to a Grade C (conditional recommendation). Selective antibiotic prophylaxis and short-term observation are considered adequate to ensure procedural safety while avoiding unnecessary antimicrobial exposure.

#### 5) Operator training and competency

The safety and success of biodegradable rectal spacer insertion are closely dependent on the operator’s technical proficiency and understanding of periprostatic anatomy. To ensure consistent procedural outcomes across institutions, structured training and credentialing systems should be established.

Physicians performing this procedure should complete a formal training program that includes theoretical education, hands-on simulator practice, and supervised clinical cases. Initial procedures should be conducted under the direct supervision of an experienced mentor, preferably one with extensive expertise in TRUS-guided transperineal interventions. Based on expert consensus, this panel recommends a target of approximately 10–20 supervised cases before performing the procedure independently. This aligns with the principle of manufacturer training programs, which require physicians to complete “multiple cases” under supervision to demonstrate competency. Simulation-based education has been shown to shorten the learning curve, reduce complications, and improve accuracy in identifying the correct injection plane. Institutions introducing this technology should consider implementing certified applier training programs or equivalent competency assessments to standardize technique and ensure quality assurance.

While the pivotal trial reported a <1% rate of severe late rectal toxicity, real-world data demonstrates that severe procedural adverse events, while rare, do occur. Common determinants of procedural difficulty include patient anatomy (e.g., prostate volume, rectal spacing, or prior surgery), ultrasound resolution, and operator experience. Therefore, ongoing performance monitoring and participation in continuing medical education programs are strongly recommended.

This recommendation is based on expert consensus (Level IV) and is categorized as a Grade C (consensus recommendation). Consistent operator education and institutional quality assurance are critical components in maintaining procedural safety, accuracy, and reproducibility in clinical practice.

#### 6) Comparison with international guidelines

Among international clinical practice guidelines, the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) is currently the only major guideline that explicitly mentions the use of biodegradable rectal spacers in prostate cancer RT. The NCCN Guidelines for Prostate Cancer (Version 2.2026) state that “…a biocompatible and biodegradable spacer may be inserted between the prostate and rectum to displace the rectum out of the high-dose region,” and particularly emphasize its role in hypofractionated or SBRT settings. The NCCN also advises against spacer use in cases with rectal wall invasion or significant posterior capsular infiltration, aligning with the contraindications described in this Korean guideline.

In contrast, the European Association of Urology (EAU) and the American Urological Association/American Society for Radiation Oncology guidelines do not yet include explicit recommendations regarding biodegradable spacers. The 2025 EAU guideline discusses the spacer, noting the pivotal trial benefits but also explicitly warning of “uncommon, but severe and life-changing, complications, including prostatic abscess, fistulae, and sepsis.” Nonetheless, both sets of guidelines emphasize the overarching principle that RT should aim to maximize target accuracy while minimizing radiation exposure to surrounding organs at risk, a goal directly supported by the clinical application of rectal spacers. As clinical adoption continues to increase across Europe and North America—particularly for high-dose RT regimens such as IMRT, VMAT(volumetric modulated arc therapy), and SBRT—it is expected that future revisions of these guidelines will incorporate specific recommendations for spacer use.

The present Korean guideline is largely concordant with the NCCN recommendations while expanding upon several practical domains that remain underrepresented in international guidance. Specifically, it introduces: (1) detailed procedural and environmental standards, including aseptic technique and staffing requirements; (2) periprocedural antibiotic protocols, tailored to local clinical practice; and (3) operator training and certification recommendations to enhance procedural reproducibility and patient safety.

By addressing these operational details, this guideline provides a comprehensive framework optimized for Korean clinical settings, while maintaining consistency with international evidence-based principles. As additional local and global data accumulate, future updates of both domestic and international guidelines are expected to further refine these recommendations.
