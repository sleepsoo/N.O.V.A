# Recent Recommendations for Sclerotherapy in Varicose Veins: American and European Guidelines

## Abstract

Varicose veins are among the most common venous disorders affecting the lower extremities, and many individuals suffer from their symptoms. A variety of treatment methods, including sclerotherapy, are currently being employed for varicose veins. Sclerotherapy, a minimally invasive technique, has recently gained increased attention, and various approaches are being developed and introduced. Although long-term clinical outcomes have yet to be fully established, recent major clinical guidelines are referencing sclerotherapy with increasing frequency, and both the quality of evidence and the strength of recommendations are steadily improving. In the United States, major guidelines from professional societies addressing sclerotherapy were published in two parts, in 2022 and 2023. Similarly, updated European guidelines, which include recommendations on sclerotherapy, were published in 2022. By analyzing the key statements on sclerotherapy from these recent American and European guidelines, we aim to contribute to a broader understanding of clinical practices and outcomes in the field.

## Introduction

Varicose vein sclerotherapy is also referred to as chemical endovenous ablation. In this procedure, a sclerosing agent is injected into the target vein, causing endothelial damage and subsequent fibrosis of the vessel. This technique can be applied not only to superficial truncal veins but also to tributary varicose veins and smaller veins of the lower extremities, such as reticular veins and telangiectasia.

Sclerotherapy can be performed using various injection methods, including fine needles, butterfly needles, flexible cannulas, or long catheters. The choice of technique often depends on the preference and experience of the practitioner.

The most commonly used sclerosing agents are sodium tetradecyl sulfate and polidocanol, which are administered in either foam or liquid form. Foam sclerotherapy is reported to be approximately two to three times more effective than the liquid form, and due to its higher efficacy, the required dose is typically four to five times lower.

As the preference for less invasive treatments continues to grow, numerous trials and studies on this treatment approach are actively being conducted. Given that sufficient evidence has not yet been established, we reviewed the current research and clinical outcomes, with a focus on the guidelines recently issued by major academic societies in the United States and Europe (Table 1).

## American Guidelines

These guidelines from three American academic societies (Society for Vascular Surgery, American Venous Forum, and American Vein and Lymphatic Society) are an update to the 2011 clinical practice guidelines.

In the 2022 (Part I) and 2023 (Part II) Clinical Practice Guideline of American academic societies, all recommendations follow the GRADE system (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation). The first component of the GRADE system is to assess the quality of evidence (A: high; B: moderate; C: low to very low), and the second is to determine the strength of the recommendation (1: strong; 2: weak) (Table 2).

Part I focuses on how to perform sclerotherapy on tributary varicose veins in the treatment of superficial truncal reflux, while Part II addresses the treatment of varicose tributaries and recurrent varicosities themselves.

### 1. The 2022 Clinical Practice Guideline (Part I)

The 2002 American Guidelines (Part I) adopted five key questions regarding the treatment of varicose veins, the last of which concerns sclerotherapy. These are addressed in Guideline 2 and 5.

In Guideline 2, sclerotherapy is not recommended as a first-line therapy for patients with symptomatic varicose veins and axial reflux in the great saphenous vein (GSV) or small saphenous vein (SSV). Instead, endovenous thermal ablation or high ligation and stripping is recommended over physician-compounded ultrasound guided foam sclerotherapy (UGFS) when long-term outcomes are important. However, the strength of this recommendation is weak (Grade 2), and the quality of evidence is moderate (B) or low to very low (C).

Guideline 5 provides recommendations for the concomitant or staged treatment of varicose tributaries in patients undergoing ablation of incompetent superficial truncal veins such as the GSV or SSV, using phlebectomy or liquid or foam sclerotherapy. In this context, foam sclerotherapy includes either physician-compounded foam or commercially prepared polidocanol endovenous microfoam.

According to Guidelines 2 and 5, sclerotherapy alone is not recommended for the treatment of incompetent GSV or SSV. However, when treating superficial truncal veins, sclerotherapy is recommended in a limited manner—either concomitantly or in a staged fashion—for treating tributary veins. Notably, concomitant sclerotherapy carries a strong recommendation (Grade 1) despite the low quality of evidence (C).

On the other hand, staged sclerotherapy is given a weak recommendation (Grade 2) with low-quality evidence (C), and is only advised when there are anatomical or medical reasons to do so. If ablation is initially performed alone, a follow-up period of more than 3 months is required to assess whether additional staged phlebectomy or ultrasound guided sclerotherapy is needed for persistent or recurrent symptoms. However, the recommendation for this clinical practice is ungraded.

### 2. The 2023 Clinical Practice Guideline (Part II)

As mentioned above, Part II addresses the treatment of varicose tributaries and recurrent varicosities.

When symptoms are present, sclerotherapy is recommended for the treatment of varicose tributaries, reticular veins, and telangiectasias. Although it is not recommended as the initial treatment for incompetent GSV or SSV, sclerotherapy is also recommended for these superficial truncal veins if recurrence occurs after prior treatment.

The guideline notes that there are no clinical studies comparing the effectiveness of different foam sclerotherapy techniques—such as room air, a CO2 gas mixture, physician-compounded foam (prepared using the Tessari method), or commercially prepared polidocanol endovenous microfoam. This indicates a need for further research in this area.

Additionally, sclerotherapy is described as a useful intervention prior to surgical treatment in cases involving bleeding, particularly when combined with leg elevation and direct compression.

## European Guideline

New recommendations were added to the previous 2015 guideline, and the 2022 version includes a total of 94 recommendations.

In the 2022 European guideline, the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) system was used to grade the evidence and recommendations. Levels A, B, and C represent the quality of evidence, while Classes I, IIa, IIb, and III indicate the strength of recommendations (Table 2).

The 2022 European guideline provides more detailed information on sclerotherapy than the American guidelines. In addition, sclerotherapy is recommended more aggressively for superficial truncal veins compared to the American guidelines.

In the newly added Recommendation 31, it is stated that ultrasound-guided foam sclerotherapy may be used to treat saphenous trunks with a diameter of 6 mm or less. Although this recommendation is classified as Class IIb, Level B, it clearly places greater emphasis on sclerotherapy than the American guidelines. Differences in the healthcare systems and clinical practice environments between the United States and Europe may also contribute to this discrepancy.

Taking it a step further, foam sclerotherapy should be performed under ultrasound guidance (Recommendation 32; Class I, Level C).

The use of UGFS is emphasized for both safety and efficacy, and it is considered essential to avoid inadvertent intra-arterial injection of the sclerosing agent. Most complications associated with UGFS are known to be mild and typically resolve without serious sequelae.

Of note, catheter-directed foam sclerotherapy (CDFS) may be considered for the treatment of an incompetent GSV (Recommendation 33). CDFS was developed as an alternative to traditional UGFS, utilizing short catheters or direct puncture techniques. When performing CDFS, a tumescent solution is sometimes infiltrated around the saphenous vein to reduce its diameter.

For varicose tributaries, ambulatory phlebectomy and/or UGFS is recommended with relatively strong support (Recommendation 36; Class I, Level B). In cases of endovenous ablation of an incompetent saphenous trunk, concomitant treatment of tributaries is also recommended (Recommendation 48).

Similar to the American guidelines, sclerotherapy is recommended for reticular veins and/or telangiectasias (Recommendations 40 and 41). The key difference from the American guidelines is that, in the European guideline, significantly incompetent associated veins should be treated first—indicating a staged rather than concomitant approach.

A new recommendation has been added indicating that sclerotherapy may also be applied to varicose veins of the foot and ankle (Recommendation 54).

The use of sclerotherapy for recurrent varicose veins is consistent with the American guidelines. Additional new recommendations include the use of sclerotherapy in cases of active venous leg ulceration (Recommendation 78) and acute bleeding (Recommendation 90).

## Conclusion

In recent years, the use of sclerotherapy has increased significantly, accompanied by a growing number of clinical research findings. In response to this trend, major authoritative academic societies have released updated guidelines. While there are no substantial differences between the American and European guidelines, the latest revisions recommend a more proactive application of sclerotherapy, with increasing strength and clarity in the level of recommendations.

In clinical practice, sclerotherapy is often applied more broadly than the scope suggested by current guidelines. However, such indiscriminate use should be approached with caution and gradually discouraged. Moving forward, it is hoped that further high-quality research—particularly studies with long-term clinical outcomes—will be conducted, leading to the development of more advanced and evidence-based guidelines.
