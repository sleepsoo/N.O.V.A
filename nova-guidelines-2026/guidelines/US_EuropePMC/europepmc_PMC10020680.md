# Expanding the scope of health disparities research in Alzheimer's disease and related dementias

## Abstract

Topics discussed at the “Leveraging Existing Data and Analytic Methods for Health Disparities Research Related to Aging and Alzheimer's Disease and Related Dementias” workshop, held by Duke University and the Alzheimer's Association with support from the National Institute on Aging, are summarized. Ways in which existing data resources paired with innovative applications of both novel and well‐known methodologies can be used to identify the effects of multi‐level societal, community, and individual determinants of race/ethnicity, sex, and geography‐related health disparities in Alzheimer's disease and related dementia are proposed. Current literature on the population analyses of these health disparities is summarized with a focus on identifying existing gaps in knowledge, and ways to mitigate these gaps using data/method combinations are discussed at the workshop. Substantive and methodological directions of future research capable of advancing health disparities research related to aging are formulated.

## 1 INTRODUCTION

With more than 152.8 million people projected to be living with the Alzheimer's disease (AD) and related dementias (ADRD) worldwide by 2050, including more than 10 million people in the United States, the health‐related burden of ADRD is expected to continue to present a significant public health challenge well into the future. In the United States, this burden will not be shared equally across the population as significant disparities in AD/ADRD onset, progression, and survival exist by race/ethnicity, sex, and geography‐related substrata of the general population. These disparities have been growing over time and are caused by a complex interaction of demographic, socioeconomic, health‐related, and historical factors.,,,

By 2015, the growing impact of health disparities led the National Institute on Aging (NIA) to develop a theoretical framework to address the problem. This framework highlighted populations identified by race, ethnicity, sex, socioeconomic status, disability status, and gender identity as priority populations for health disparities research related to aging. Furthermore, it provided an organizational structure for health disparity–related factors arrayed across four categories: (1) environmental (geographic, political, socioeconomic and health care‐related factors); (2) sociocultural (cultural, social, and psychological factors); (3) behavioral (coping and health behaviors, psychosocial risk and/or resilience); and (4) biological (morbidity and other physiological indicators, genetic stability, cellular function, and communication). Analysis of the effects of these interacting factors and the identification of mechanisms by which they generate health disparities in AD/ADRD are expected to lead to better understanding of the causes of health disparities over the life course. Although many risk‐related (e.g., apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4, older age, macrovascular comorbidities) and protective (e.g., cognitive reserve, physical activities, diet) factors, have been identified, AD/ADRD disparity research would benefit from improved comparability and generalizability between the range of studies conducted, improved statistical power in the available data, and increased representation of select population groups such as the 85+ age group.

Ensuring sufficient statistical power for increasingly complex analyses incorporating detailed environmental, sociocultural, behavioral, and biological information is a major challenge. However, such details are essential to uncovering where and how disparities in AD/ADRD risk begin and grow. Recent years have seen unprecedented growth in the availability of data that can be used for analyses of health disparities both in the United States and worldwide. From a global perspective, the main focus was directed at sex and/or gender disparities. Identifying the drivers of such disparities in ADRD across multiple countries can act to inform interventions unique to each individual geographical and sociocultural area as well as provide insight into any commonalities constant across national and cultural divides. The Health and Retirement Sister Studies, which currently represent 18 studies around the world (table 2 of ref.) can be the first building block of such activities. In the United States, large‐scale administrative claims and electronic medical records databases are becoming increasingly widespread both as independent resources and through linkages to independent survey‐based studies. These changes in the epidemiologic and research environment prompted experts from across the country to conduct a workshop, held jointly by Duke University and the Alzheimer's Association. The workshop is structured around an iterative conceptual framework (Figure 1) and is focused on exploration of how existing analytic methods and data can be leveraged to accelerate progress in understanding the causes and mechanisms of health‐related disparities in AD/ADRD and other prominent age‐related diseases.

### RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

1. 2. Interpretation: The status quo of health disparities research was discussed, with an emphasis on common methodological and data‐related challenges. Promising new methodologies as well as innovative applications of traditional techniques aimed at improving the quality of health disparities research were showcased.

This article presents a synopsis of the ensuing discussion. To do so, we summarize the current literature on population analyses of health disparities, with a focus on identifying existing gaps in knowledge and ways to mitigate these gaps using data/method combinations discussed at the workshop. Substantive and methodological directions of future research capable of advancing health disparities research related to aging are then formulated. When referring to existing literature and/or data set‐specific results, we utilize the same race/ethnicity/sex/gender designation used by the source article and/or available in the data set being discussed. Otherwise, we use sex to represent phenotypic differences based on biology; gender to represent social differences based on roles, relationships, and behaviors; and race/ethnicity to represent differences in socio‐cultural background.

## 2 RACE/ETHNICITY‐RELATED DISPARITIES

Research found higher prevalence and incidence of AD in Black and Hispanic populations compared to their White or Asian counterparts,,, with the Black‐to‐White ratio of age‐adjusted incidence equaling 1.37 for ADRD and 1.34 for AD. These estimates were obtained based on a systematic review of literature and meta‐analysis, Kaiser Permanente Northern California data, and Medicare administrative claims data., Race/ethnicity‐specific patterns of AD/ADRD mortality differ from those described for incidence and prevalence. The age‐adjusted cause‐specific AD/ADRD mortality ratios among non‐Hispanics were 0.92 for Black/White and 0.65 for Hispanic/non‐Hispanic. Lower mortality and higher incidence of AD/ADRD are consistent with better survival in disadvantaged populations observed in some studies., The death hazard ratios for individuals diagnosed with dementia obtained in meta‐analyses were 0.86 and 0.65, respectively, for Black and Hispanic populations. Additional support for these findings were presented during the workshop, extending existing work based on Medicare data;, Black and Hispanic subpopulations demonstrated higher risk for both AD and ADRD when compared to non‐Hispanic Whites (AD hazard ratios of 1.33 for Black and 1.21 for Hispanic) but lower survival post AD/ADRD onset (AD hazard ratios of 0.86 and 0.69).

A major group of limitations present in the existing studies on the epidemiological estimates of AD/ADRD is centered on measure and data quality. Measures based on self‐identification of race and ethnicity are commonly used to study race/ethnicity‐related disparities. Although preferable to other approaches, such as the ascribing of race/ethnicity (e.g., genetic ancestry) or imputation methods, such measures do not account for variations in the way individuals define their ethnic and racial identity, the mixed heritage of a large percentage of the US population, and the historical and cultural context of the concept of race. Furthermore, the heterogeneity that exists within standardized and commonly used racial and ethnic categories (e.g., Hispanic) obscures important detail such as differences by country of origin (e.g., Mexican‐American vs Puerto Rican) and nativity (e.g., US‐born vs foreign‐born).

In clinical trials there is poor recruitment of patients with AD/ADRD coming from minoritized and/or socially and economically disadvantaged populations. A recent meta‐analysis showed that only 59.2% of recent clinical trials reported information on participant race and that non‐White populations accounted for a small proportion of the individuals enrolled. Most clinical trials did not report efficacy and safety/tolerability statistics for race/ethnicity‐specific groups or perform race/ethnicity‐specific sensitivity analyses. This exclusion exacerbates other problems with representability present in the data, such as poor inclusion of the 85+ age group, which represents about 38% of all individuals with AD but only 8% of clinical trial participants.

Although well written conceptually, existing diagnostic criteria are not unified, with multiple different operationalizations existing simultaneously. Neurocognitive tests that define the cognitive phenotype and severity level often lack detail about the etiology underlying the impairment. This is important because onset and progression of AD/ADRD can be affected by a variety of specific biomarkers (including genetics) as well as disease/disease and disease/treatment interactions. A separate issue is the design of the cognitive instruments themselves. Although subgroup‐specific tests of cognition have been developed, many commonly used instruments are calibrated for White older adults and do not take into account differences in education, culture, and cognitive reserve common in disadvantaged minorities. These limitations may contribute to the significant difficulty in consistent and timely diagnosis, and lead to AD/ADRD cases being observed at different age/level of severity combinations, thereby complicating causal analysis.

Another group of limitations is related to differences in assumptions made for study sampling, which leads to differences in derived estimates of disparities and risk‐factor effect size. For example, studies have suggested that some of the race/ethnicity‐related disparities in AD/ADRD could be caused by healthier individuals within a group who survive past the initial ages of higher mortality risk to older age, thus surpassing the average life expectancy of the group., This could lead to the effects such as the Hispanic paradox, which refers to the comparable‐or‐better survival in Hispanic American groups despite their lower‐than‐average socioeconomic status and education level., The healthy migrant (successful migrants have better initial levels of health) and salmon (aging/sick migrants moving back to their home country) effects operate through a similar mechanism. Differences in health, especially age‐related co‐morbidities and AD/ADRD risk factors, play an important role in survival and attrition, and when these are ignored can lead to misleading findings.

Mismatched linkages and overall quality of mortality data can also present a problem. For example, AD and especially ADRD is often under‐reported on death certificates,; this problem affects the Black and Hispanic population more than non‐Hispanic White population. The impact of this problem increases with age and is exacerbated by higher misreports of an individual's exact age in these higher age brackets.,, Finally, many studies on racial differences in AD/ADRD risk use oversimplified models that cannot account for the complex interactions between risk and protective factors determining what is observed in the data.

Although significant progress has been made in measuring the size of health disparities in AD/ADRD, a large gap remains in understanding the drivers of the disparities in AD/ADRD prevalence, incidence, and mortality in diverse US populations. The critical research question in disparity research consists of identification and quantification of the causes/sources of observed disparities. To date, racial disparities have been found to be generated through differences in socioeconomic characteristics, and to a lesser extent, by genetic markers and/or other health‐related conditions., Specifically, it has been shown that differences in disease risk and outcomes are not necessarily associated with biology, but are correlated with the built environment, lifestyle, physical activity, access to and utilization of health care, delays in care, misdiagnosis or underdiagnosis, insurance status, and education., Furthermore, cultural beliefs of patients and their families, lack of trust in the health care system, unequal allocation of and access to health care resources, and differences in the ability to make informed health decisions influence AD/ADRD risk. Historically, many minoritized racial groups reside in communities that have a greater concentration of poverty and are lacking in equitable access to health care resources, and thus increasing dementia risk., Studies focused on identifying biological mechanisms that generate racial disparities have revealed that Black and White patients with dementia demonstrate different neuropathological changes, suggesting differences in the manifestations of the underlying pathophysiological processes., Observational studies of diverse cohorts demonstrate important differences in vascular and metabolic risk factors for AD/ADRD and their treatment that may explain excess risk in both mortality and ADRD among Black and Hispanic older adults.,,, However, although biological and genetic factors are associated with higher AD/ADRD risk in certain racial groups, social determinants of health account for more than half of the variance in health outcomes.

Future studies of race/ethnicity‐related disparities in AD/ADRD should focus on remedying the limitations described above. Specifically, when planning new trials, sufficient enrollment of socially and economically disadvantaged populations should be a major focus: oversampling of such groups should become the gold standard. This is especially true for AD/ADRD‐specific drug‐ development protocols, which already face problems with age inclusion. When using existing data, analytic methods that allow researchers to attenuate the impact of small sample size among socially and economically disadvantaged populations should be developed and implemented, and potential biases introduced by the low generalizability of the minority‐specific results should be recognized and corrected. Any potential etiologic causes for race/ethnicity‐related disparities in AD/ADRD are unlikely to be elucidated without the incorporation of biofluid and neuroimaging biomarkers of AD, ADRD, and vascular contributions to dementia. Finally, it is important to consider that the potential causes of race/ethnicity‐related disparities are multifactorial in nature and are subject to the effects of structural discrimination and concentrations of disadvantage; therefore, data that permit a big picture intersectional approach that allows for interactions between different sources of health disparities (e.g., race/ethnicity, sex/gender, geographic location, and so on) should be prioritized. Specific attention should be paid to the role of structural racism, its causes, and the proportion of the total racial disparity in AD/ADRD risk accounted for by associated factors. It is also important to better understand and illustrate the sources of variability within groups so both within‐ and between‐group analyses are needed to further clarify how to make effective comparisons.

## 3 SEX‐RELATED DISPARITIES

AD is more prevalent in women,, who also show greater AD‐related cognitive impairment across a wide range of cognitive domains. In the United States, the female/male ADRD death ratio was 1.29 in 2017. However, estimates of AD incidence are conflicting both in the United States and globally.,, An example of this discrepancy was found in a recent meta‐analyses, where 22 studies demonstrated insignificantly higher incidence for females., Furthermore, observed male–female differences can disappear after controlling for other determinants. The reasons for such discrepancies range from pure methodological (e.g., variability in study designs, statistical power, and methods of classifying and confirming AD diagnoses) to substantive (e.g., risk‐related co‐morbidities)., In fact, the longer female life expectancy and selective survival of males with better cardiovascular health into old age has been documented, and research findings consistent with this hypothesis exist. In contrast, several viable pathways through which sex‐related differences in AD/ADRD incidence could manifest have also been suggested. These include physiological differences during development and sex‐specific changes during the aging process and over the course of AD development/progression; sex‐specific susceptibility to the effects of the APOE*ε4 allele and cardiovascular risk factors; sex‐specific roles of sex hormones in brain development, aging, and AD development/progression,; female‐specific immune signatures in the blood and brain; as well as other female‐specific pathways associated with the development of amyloid and tau pathology. Thus the fundamental question is why so many studies have detected the presence of female‐specific, biological risk factors and why so few studies have uncovered a difference in incidence between sexes. Estimates based on a 5% nationally representative sample of Medicare beneficiaries, and a univariable Cox model show that females had higher risks of AD and ADRD (hazard ratios of 1.23 and 1.15) but better survival after disease onset (hazard ratios of 0.71 and 0.71).

Surprisingly, sex‐specific analyses in AD clinical trials are not commonplace, and the importance of sex as a critical modulator of response to treatment is vastly underappreciated.,, However, because sex is an important factor that impacts precision pharmacology for drug research and development,, sex‐specific strategies for prevention, diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment of AD are needed. Identifying whether there are (biological) sex‐specific causal differences in AD/ADRD incidence, or if the higher AD/ADRD prevalence observed in women is the result of differences in other environmental, sociocultural, behavioral, and biological factors, should be a priority. Part of the solution may be through increased attention to the publication of quality studies with negative results (e.g., failed to find a statistically significant difference). Finally, as mentioned previously, sex encompasses biological differences due to sex chromosomes, reproductive organs, and hormones; the self‐reported measure of sex available in most data sets omits information on sex‐related variation (e.g., individuals engaged in gender affirmation therapy) as well individuals from Lesbian, Gay, Bisexual, Trans, Queer, Intersex, Asexual and Other (LGBTQIA+) communities., These groups face distinct risk factors related to cognitive impairment and dementia,, and their health outcomes need to be studied in specific population strata rather than lumped with the larger group.

## 4 GEOGRAPHIC DISPARITIES

The size of both race/ethnicity and sex‐related disparities can be strongly influenced by the current and historical characteristics of a discrete geographic region. Analysis of fee‐for‐service records from the Medicare system showed that the highest incidence rates of AD were observed in the Midwest and the South of the United States. County‐level variation within these regions has also been observed, thus underscoring the need for interventions and public health efforts tailored to local communities. Furthermore, it has been shown that Black and White residents born in the Stroke Belt region within the United State are at higher risk of dementia‐related mortality than those born in other states, even for people who moved out of this region. Disparities in dementia‐related mortality are not strictly concentrated around the Stroke Belt. For example, they were also identified as the highest contributor to differences in total mortality observed between Washington and New York states. The higher rate of mortality from diseases of the nervous system was almost completely compensated for by opposing contributions of circulatory diseases resulting in comparable total mortality rates in both states. The study concludes by showing that these mortality patterns could not be explained by the differences in cause‐of‐death coding practices and emphasizing the critical importance of comorbidity‐related factors.

Regional differences in prevailing morbidity profiles are a strong source of health disparities, both directly and through exacerbating existing race/ethnicity and sex‐related disparities. However, there is a significant paucity of knowledge on the effects of multimorbidity patterns on the onset and progression of AD/ADRD over the life course, and the methodology for carrying out such analyses is still nascent., Much of the existing knowledge on the effect of co‐existing morbidities is hampered by piecemeal examination of a small number of individual conditions without attempting to assess the potential effects of multiple disease combinations. Application of novel statistical approaches, such as the longitudinal extension to the average attributable fraction, to meta‐analyses of previously conducted studies and re‐analysis of existing data sources could help mitigate this problem. Finally, the effect of treatment of other chronic diseases on the onset and progression of AD/ADRD should not be overlooked., For example, the estimated risk associated with some medications such as antidepressants has varied depending on the reason for the prescription: higher for depression and lower for migraine.

Social factors are a powerful source of geographic disparities directly, and indirectly through their effects on comorbidities and predictors of AD/ADRD as highlighted by the NIA Health Disparities Research Framework. AD/ADRD incidence derives from an interaction between biological pathophysiology, and social determinants of AD/ADRD including environmental exposure.,, A later‐life onset of AD/ADRD derives, in part, from the sum of adverse and beneficial health‐related exposures accumulated over an individual's life course.,,,, These exposures incur risk of AD/ADRD through several distinct but interrelated pathways such as social deprivation from economic, legal/judicial, and environmental‐injustice stressors, and health care inequities driven by inadequate access to health insurance and health care facilities and substandard treatment of individual health conditions. Socially and economically disadvantaged groups are often subject to multiple adverse social and geography‐related factors, which can turn a disparity into a race/ethnicity or income‐related disparity as well.,, The effects of geographic differences can be direct (e.g., through adverse environmental exposure) or indirect (e.g., through increased probability of one or more adverse health conditions), and both types of effects can be identified and targeted for intervention. However, few models of the phenomenon focusing on detailed measurement to guide intervention exist, and more work in this area is required.

Geography is also closely related to differences in environmental exposures. For example, a number of studies showed that ambient particulate matter (PM) may contribute to a variety of neurodegeneration‐related diseases, such as cognitive impairment, AD, Parkinson's disease, and dementia., Analysis of current air pollution‐dementia revealed that exposure to PM2.5 was positively associated with higher risk for dementia and AD, and that long‐term exposures to ambient PM2.5 levels exceeding the World Health Organization standards significantly increased risk of death and hospital admissions for AD. Furthermore, exposure to higher levels of arsenic in certain geographic areas has also been shown to be associated with increased risk of AD and other neurodegenerative diseases.,, Such environmental exposures are an important part of the social determinants of health experienced by a disadvantaged group, since many such groups have been historically marginalized into neighborhoods exposed to adverse environmental conditions. Therefore, it would be beneficial to account for the potential impact of local differences in the type and concentrations of non‐metal and metal PM (e.g., lead, mercury) in the air, as well as arsenic and other contaminants in the water, when analyzing differences in health outcomes.

## 5 LEVERAGING EXISTING DATA AND ANALYTIC METHODS FOR HEALTH DISPARITIES

A variety of methods have been used to estimate health disparities and then to explain their formation in terms of predictors. Quantification of the size of the disparity usually involved the calculation and comparison of age‐adjusted rates for each subpopulation., Cox proportional hazards models were then used to estimate the association between sex/race/ethnicity/geographic region and dementia risk in univariable and multivariable analysis. Because age is the most important non‐genetic risk factor for AD/ADRD, age should be used as a time scale variable in these Cox models. In this case the estimates of disparities from age‐adjusted rates and Cox models are comparable. Both approaches allow for the evaluation of confidence intervals providing a good basis for statistical comparisons.

Methods of identifying causes of health disparities were usually based on the Cox model.,, A predictor explains some portion of a health disparity if the effect associated with the disparity‐affected group (e.g., race/ethnicity; sex) is significantly reduced when an explanatory factor is added to the multivariable Cox model. Although some ideas underlying this approach are available, such methods remained ad hoc approaches that are not mathematically proven, especially in the case of use with the Cox model. Because estimates are made in several subpopulations of interest, an analysis that allows for simultaneous estimates of effects of predictors and statistical comparisons of their impact on the disparity of interest cannot be performed using conventional regression models and requires further methodological development. One promising approach involves extensions of Oaxaca‐Blinder methods for binary,, and censored, outcomes. The generalized Oaxaca‐Blinder–based model identifies two effects for each predictor: exposure (a higher prevalence of the risk factor in a population group) and vulnerability (a higher risk of AD in individuals with the risk factor in a population group)., Recent applications have demonstrated that arterial hypertension was responsible for exceptionally large proportions of the total Black/White disparity in AD/ADRD risk, with vulnerability accounting for about 80% of the total disease‐specific effect. Other approaches used for quantitative explanation of disparities are related to marginal structural or structural equation modeling,; however, they require specific assumptions on how these equations are constructed.

Causal estimates of the effects of specific factors on AD/ADRD disparities can be obtained through the use of propensity score and rank‐and‐replace methods suggested by the Institute of Medicine in their conceptualization of disparities, as the differences between groups that are not attributable to differences in clinical need and/or treatment preferences. Such methods are based on the implementation of group variable effects and allow for the identification of the proportion of the total effect accounted for by environmental, sociocultural, behavioral, and biological factors. Both propensity score and rank‐and‐replace methods involve the creation of two counterfactual (pseudo) populations in such a way that they effectively differ only due to the factors being analyzed. Propensity score approaches model the probability of being part of a disadvantaged group and estimate a set of inverse probability weights, the use of which equalizes (methodologically) the advantaged/disadvantaged groups in terms of the variables included in the propensity score model. In rank‐and‐replace methods the variables of interests are ranked (by the value of a linear predictor representing the effects of all such factors of interest) within two disparity‐affected subpopulations, and then the values of the linear predictors in the disadvantaged subpopulation are replaced with those of the advantaged group in order of the created rank. These two methods can be combined.

The informativeness of many of the methods that are used for estimation and explanation of health disparities is enhanced when they are applied to individual‐level large health data sets such as Medicare Administrative claims data. The advantages of such data include: (1) national representativeness of the United States with zip‐code level geographic data for beneficiaries and providers; (2) longitudinal design covering over 25 years of comparable data on US older adults; (3) sufficient statistical power even for rare medical conditions and/or small population subgroups, such as socioeconomically disadvantaged individuals through links to Medicaid claims; (4) a wide range of research targets including health conditions, treatment, screening/preventive care, drug use, cost of care, characteristics and habits of providers; and (5) expandability through linkage to other data sources. The use of Medicare data has well‐known limitations such as the lack of information on disease severity. However, the volume and comprehensiveness of Medicare data allow the successful application of a spectrum of methods for analysis of disparities and time trends, starting from pure empiric methods of stratification and rate evaluation, usual regression models, and finishing with advanced population approaches developed to clarify specific properties of disparities and their time trends. The scope of analyses using Medicare data can be extended through linkages to other data sets from disease registries or surveys.

### Box 1. Disparities in AD/ADRD and related health outcomes: What is known

Black and Hispanic populations demonstrate higher risk but improved survival of Alzheimer's disease (AD) and related dementias (ADRD) compared to White populations.

Although women have higher AD prevalence and greater symptoms, there is no consensus on whether this difference extends to incidence and mortality once the effects of other predictors have been controlled for.

The highest incidence rates of AD were observed in the Midwest and the South of the United States.

There is a strong gradient in AD‐related mortality with high rates in the West/North West, and low rates in the North East.

For race/ethnicity‐related disparities, sociocultural determinants account for more than half of the variance in health outcomes, whereas the effects of genetic markers are minor.

Sociocultural factors are a powerful source of AD/ADRD, and related health disparities acting both directly and indirectly through their effects on other predictors.

Environmental exposure presents an important aspect of the problem, more so for race/ethnicity disparities, since many minorities have been historically marginalized into neighborhoods exposed to adverse environmental conditions.

A time trend can be considered as a disparity among subpopulations across different time‐periods; therefore, methods for analyses of time trends can be generalized and combined with methods of decomposition for analyses of health disparities. For example, the partitioning approach designed to explain the epidemiologic causes of the time trends in disease prevalence and incidence‐based mortality (i.e., mortality of individuals with disease onset during lifetime) showed that AD/ADRD incidence is the primary determinant of the trends in AD/ADRD prevalence and mortality. Next, trend partitioning of the racial disparities in AD/ADRD prevalence and mortality (demonstrated at the workshop) showed that, over time, the racial disparities in AD prevalence were overwhelmingly the result of racial differences in disease incidence. Analysis of racial differences in AD/ADRD incidence through Oaxaca‐Blinder decomposition showed that arterial hypertension played a critical role in the generation of racial disparities in AD/ADRD incidence. Applying trend partitioning again (this time to hypertension) it was demonstrated that over time the racial disparities in hypertension prevalence are overwhelmingly the result of racial differences in pre‐existing hypertension prior to Medicare eligibility at age 65. This suggests that sources of racial disparities may be tied to the onset of circulatory diseases in earlier life. Such a combination of partitioning and decomposition methods applied iteratively provides a promising approach to identifying the causal mechanisms behind health‐related disparities.

### Box 2. Disparities in AD/ADRD and related health outcomes: Existing challenges

Problems with data quality and skewed representation of heterogeneity in study design hamper future progress.

The under‐representation of race/ethnicity, sex, older age, disability, and gender identity–specific populations in the available data and clinical studies must be corrected.

Use of self‐identification of sex/race/ethnicity does not account for variations in the way individuals define their background and identity.

Cognitive measures are strongly influenced by linguistic, educational, or cultural factors and may not capture the cognitive status of minority populations with equal accuracy.

A large gap remains in understanding the drivers of disparities in AD/ADRD prevalence and incidence in diverse US populations.

Racial/ethnic differences in survival following AD/ADRD diagnosis are not widely studied; observed patterns remain unexplained.

It is unclear if a sex‐related difference in AD/ADRD incidence exists after other factors have been controlled for.

Although information on geographic variations of AD/dementia incidence/mortality exists, no underlying mechanisms explaining the patterns of the observed spatial and spatial‐temporal distributions have been provided.

A critical gap remains in understanding the relevance and temporal occurrence of multimorbidity patterns and treatment patterns for generating disparities in the onset and progression of AD/dementia.

Several types of forecasting (or projection) models have been used to predict the future prevalence of AD/ADRD and/or the number of individuals living with the disease. Such projections are important because they provide specific targets of future health interventions and ultimately ensure that the health system is prepared for future challenges. The simplest approach,, includes: estimation of disease prevalence in population subgroups, calculation of the future values of disease prevalence using information about current trends, and computing the number of affected individuals by multiplying the estimated future prevalence values by the projected population given by the Census. In some approaches predictions are based on information (or assumptions) about the future dynamics of incidence, total mortality, and the death hazard ratio of being in an unhealthy state. More advanced approaches use multistate models involving healthy and unhealthy states.,, The partitioning approach also provides forecasting models in which mutual interdependence between the measures of interest (i.e., incidence, prevalence, comorbidity, recovery/long‐term remission, and patient survival) is an inherent property.

### Box 3. Disparities in AD/ADRD and related health outcomes: Recommendations

Identification of the causes and magnitude of race/ethnicity, sex, and geography‐related disparities in AD/ADRD onset and associated adverse health outcomes, as well as their time trends remain a current challenge and should be pursued.

To meet this challenge, estimates need to be obtained and compared in the general population, but also across an appropriate representation of homogeneous population groups.

Data quality and study design limitations need to be recognized and addressed, perhaps through analyses of the variation in the results of previously conducted studies.

Because routine mortality statistics may misestimate AD/ADRD mortality, results should be cross‐checked with other sources of data.

Use of high‐power nationally representative big health intersectional data may help overcome some of the existing data‐related problems.

Medicare data have sufficient statistical power and potential for studying disparities in AD/ADRD in three interacting directions: multi‐ethnic structure of population, place of residence, and time period.

Emphasis should be placed on analysis of interrelations and interactions in influential risk and protective factors associated with disparities in AD/ADRD.

Simultaneous estimates of the effects of predictors and statistical comparisons of their impact on the disparity of interest cannot be performed using conventional regression models and require further methodological development.

Future studies should consider the unique risk and protective factors of this population when analyzing disparities in cognitive impairment and AD/ADRD. Longitudinal studies could provide information on the progression of cognitive decline in LGBTQIA+ and other subgroups of older adults, accessing supportive services, and how their needs change over time.

Broader use of the complex methodological approaches such as decomposition and partitioning analyses, Bayesian modeling, latent structure analyses, instrumental variables, and other approaches to causal inference, as well as decision analysis/simulation, is recommended, as such analyses will help progress in the integration of demographic, genetic, epigenetic, biomarker, and clinical data from large studies into predictive models.

One challenge facing health disparities research are the structural issues with the data being collected and utilized. Information on critical demographic characteristics (e.g., sex, race, ethnicity, and so on) is based on self‐report, which is restricted to a set of pre‐determined responses, for example, only five categories (White, Black or African American, Asian American, American Indian/Alaska Native, and Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander) are officially recognized in the US Census and six (White, Black, Other, Asian, Hispanic, and North American Native) in the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS). The respondent is then faced with a problem of identification (from a data perspective: the study participant filling out a questionnaire) with one of the presented options which, for many people, can present overlapping cultural and biologic characteristics. The reverse side of this question is labeling (from a data perspective: a third party filling out the forms for the study participant; this is not uncommon e.g., proxy interviews). In addition to issues with identification, such aggregations are inherently problematic, for example, the category of “Asian,” which spans a wide range of cultural and biological characteristics representing over 60% of the world population. By excluding these differences from health disparities models we obtain results valid for the “average Asian,” and the more relevant subgroup‐specific effects (especially for subgroups that are relatively small with respect to total population size) are lost. In this, the Surveilance Epidemiology and End Results (SEER) and SEER‐Medicare data are ahead of the curve. SEER currently provides data on 25 Asian subgroups including Chinese, Japanese, Filipino, Korean, Vietnamese, and Polynesian among others. Note that this problem is not tied to population size. “North American Native,” represents a very small group within the total US population, but this category still overgeneralizes a diverse group of peoples characterized by clustering around separate tribe/tribe‐group‐specific territories. Similarly, until recently, the common practice was to use sex and gender interchangeably to indicate the biological sex at birth. This combined the (potentially different) effects of biological sex and gender and overlooks populations with unique needs and health characteristics such as those undergoing gender‐affirmation therapy. Unfortunately, the solution to the problem may not be as simple as including more complex sociocultural instruments, since as populational subgroups become better defined, ensuring that the representation for that subgroup is sufficient for statistical analysis becomes a serious challenge. This is especially relevant for disparities in AD/ADRD, which already suffers from low representation among the 85+ age group, one of the primary age strata of interest. The uncertainties coming from these limitations should be tested for in sensitivity analyses, and the respective estimates can then be adjusted to provide a more accurate interpretation of the results. These sensitivity studies could include empiric and mixture modeling, comparing estimates across data sets, and attracting genetic information for evaluating the biological components of these variables.

Several other advanced approaches to clarify causes and mechanisms of disparities in AD/ADRD were discussed more briefly at the workshop: (1) longitudinal Grade of Membership (L‐GoM) modeling provides new insights into predicting the natural history of AD based on the dynamics of multiple predictors, (2) racial/ethnic estimates of dementia prevalence and spatiotemporal clustering of dementia mortality in the United States,, (3) analyses and explanation of contradictions between increased AD/ADRD prevalence and declined cognitive impairment prevalence, (4) the effect of chemotherapy, and anesthesia on the risk of AD, (5) adherence to disease management guidelines of AD‐risk‐related diseases and associated impact on AD, (6) genetics of AD/ADRD and related disparities,,,,, (7) studying disparities in brain health using the Health and Retirement Study, (8) longitudinal interrelationships between multimorbidity, function, and cognition: disparities by race and ethnicity, (9) racial differences in AD biomarkers: design and methods of future studies, (10) advantages of within group analysis of race/ethnicity, (11) how to evolve therapeutics in AD, and how to envision use of biomarkers for detection and screening, (12) leveraging volunteers to support ADRD family caregiving and to help address health disparities, (13) racial/ethnic disparities in later stages of dementia among homebound older adults, (14) longitudinal interrelationships between multimorbidity, function, and cognition: disparities by race and ethnicity, and (15) using a multiple stage duration model to account for population heterogeneity and partial observability.

## 6 SUMMARY

The identification of the causes and magnitude of race/ethnicity, sex/gender, and geography‐related disparities in AD/ADRD onset and associated adverse health outcomes, as well as their time trends, remains a current challenge. To meet this challenge, estimates have to be obtained in the general population but also across a diversity of homogeneous population groups. Data quality and study design limitations need to be recognized and addressed, perhaps through the analyses of the variation in the results of previously conducted studies. Because routine mortality statistics may mis‐estimate AD/ADRD mortality, results should be cross‐checked with other sources of data. Emphasis should be placed on the analysis of interrelations and interactions in influential risk and protective factors associated with disparities in AD/ADRD. The use of statistical methods capable of evaluating and comparing the effects of multiple predictors in the same study design, and a shift away from research designs that emphasize single‐factor explanations for disparities in cognitive impairment and AD/ADRD in favor of methods that capture multi‐level underlying differences are desirable. Medicare data are a data set with sufficient statistical power and potential for studying disparities in AD/ADRD in interacting directions: sex/gender, multi‐ethnic structure of population, place of residence, socioeconomic status, and time period. Broader use of complex methodological approaches such as decomposition and partitioning analyses, Bayesian modeling, latent structure analyses, instrumental variables and other approaches to causal inference, as well as decision analysis/simulation are recommended. These analyses will help progress in the integration of demographic, genetic, epigenetic, biomarker, and clinical data from large studies into predictive models. Mitigation of the above challenges can lead to a better understanding of the causes of aging‐related health disparities over the life course.
