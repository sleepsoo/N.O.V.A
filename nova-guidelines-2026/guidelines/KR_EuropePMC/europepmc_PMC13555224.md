# Korean Thyroid Association Guidelines on the Management of Differentiated Thyroid Cancers, Part II: Follow-up Surveillance after Initial Treatment 2026

## Abstract

In patients with differentiated thyroid cancer, initial recurrence-risk stratification based on clinical, histopathological, and perioperative findings remains central to management during the first 1–2 years after initial treatment. Ongoing risk stratification (ORS) complements this initial assessment by continuously integrating longitudinal clinical data and treatment response, thereby refining long-term prognostic assessment and supporting individualized risk-adapted management. Building on the 2024 Korean Thyroid Association (KTA) guidelines, the 2026 KTA guideline incorporates new evidence from large Korean multicenter cohorts and comprehensive systematic reviews. The updated recommendations reinforce the continued application of ORS and provide more specific guidance on risk-adapted thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) targets, surveillance strategies for recurrence detection, and long-term follow-up tailored to individual recurrence risk and treatment response. Consistent with the evolving paradigm toward risk-adapted and individualized management, this revision updates recommendations regarding target serum TSH levels, serum thyroglobulin thresholds for response assessment and long-term surveillance, and follow-up intervals based on both initial-risk category and the ORS response category. These evidence-based recommendations aim to minimize unnecessary treatment and excessive surveillance in the majority of patients who have an excellent prognosis after initial therapy while ensuring individualized long-term management for patients who require closer surveillance or additional therapeutic intervention.

## INTRODUCTION

This guideline is the 2026 revision of Part II, “Follow-up surveillance after initial treatment,” of the Korean Thyroid Association (KTA) clinical practice guidelines for differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC). Building on the 2024 edition, the Guideline Committee for Thyroid Nodule and Cancer, comprising specialists in internal medicine, surgery, and otolaryngology nominated by the relevant medical specialty societies, identified 11 new key questions addressing target serum thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels and the clinical role of serum thyroglobulin (Tg) during follow-up after initial treatment for DTC.

For each key question, panel members and methodology experts developed the search strategies and systematically searched MEDLINE, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library, supplemented by manual searches. Eligibility criteria were predefined according to the Population, Intervention, Comparator, and Outcome (PICO) framework. Evidence was synthesized using both quantitative methods, including meta-analysis, and qualitative systematic review. Draft recommendations were formulated from the synthesized evidence and further informed by clinical data from Korean multicenter retrospective cohort studies.

Following review by the full committee, the draft guideline was presented at the 2025 KTA Spring and Fall Scientific Meetings. Consensus was subsequently achieved through a Delphi process involving 24 experts nominated by the relevant societies, with all key questions reaching agreement in the first Delphi round. The revised guideline was presented at the 2026 KTA Spring Scientific Meeting for further refinement before undergoing external review and endorsement by the Korean Endocrine Society, Korean Association of Endocrine Surgeons, Korean Society of Otolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery, Korean Society of Nuclear Medicine, and Korean Society of Thyroid Radiology. Before final publication, the draft was posted on the KTA website for public comment from KTA members.

Key clinical points in each section are presented as specific recommendations, and their corresponding recommendation strengths are summarized in Table 1. The full guideline and executive summary are available in Korean in the official journal of the KTA, the International Journal of Thyroidology, and on the KTA website (www.thyroid.kr).

## FOLLOW-UP SURVEILLANCE OF DIFFERENTIATED THYROID CANCER

The primary goal of long-term surveillance in patients with DTC who achieve clinical cure after initial treatment is the accurate detection of recurrent disease. In patients with persistent or recurrent disease, treatment aims to achieve cure or reduce morbidity and mortality; when cure is not feasible, additional treatment may be used to reduce tumor burden or delay disease progression. With the development of more sensitive and specific diagnostic methods, more conservative, cost-effective, and safer management strategies are now recommended for low-risk patients, whereas aggressive intensive management remains appropriate for patients at high risk of recurrence. Because many patients with DTC achieve long-term survival even in the presence of residual disease, recent management approaches have increasingly emphasized individualized care that incorporates patient preferences, quality of life, and socioeconomic considerations, in addition to disease control.

In this context, Chapter 5 of Part I of the KTA guideline addresses initial-risk stratification, which assesses postoperative disease status and predicts the risk of recurrence using the clinical characteristics and prognosis of Korean patients with DTC. During follow-up, however, oncologic outcomes are influenced not only by initial-risk stratification but also by the treatment received and the response to therapy. Therefore, after initial treatment has been completed according to initial-risk stratification, treatment response and disease status should be reassessed both immediately after treatment and throughout follow-up. Additional diagnostic testing, subsequent treatment strategies, and follow-up plans should be individualized dynamically according to these reassessments.

Ongoing risk stratification (ORS), formerly referred to as dynamic risk stratification (DRS), was introduced in the 2015 American Thyroid Association (ATA) guideline, adopted in the 2016 KTA guideline, and retained in the current revision. The 2025 ATA guideline introduced the DATA framework (Diagnosis, Assessment of risk/benefit, Treatment decisions, response Assessment), a decision-making model spanning the entire continuum of thyroid cancer care from diagnosis through long-term follow-up. The DATA framework is grounded in ORS, which is performed at each follow-up visit after initial thyroid cancer treatment to reassess disease status and guide subsequent management and surveillance. Therefore, ORS has become central to long-term management of DTC.

This revision updates and expands the ORS response-assessment criteria and corresponding target serum TSH levels presented in the 2024 guideline. These updates are based on systematic reviews and recent evidence from a KTA-supported multicenter cohort study. Some recommendations in this guideline differ from those presented in the 2025 ATA Clinical Practice Guidelines for DTC. These differences primarily reflect the incorporation of newly available evidence, including comprehensive systematic reviews and Korean multicenter studies, as well as differences in healthcare systems and clinical practice across countries. This guideline provides an evidence-based framework for response assessment after initial treatment and individualized long-term management of patients with DTC.

This guideline defines the ORS criteria used to assess disease status according to treatment response in patients with DTC. It also provides updated recommendations for “Part II. Follow-up surveillance after initial treatment,” including diagnostic evaluation, treatment strategies, and surveillance according to postoperative initial-risk stratification and ORS.

### II.1. Reassessment of disease status according to treatment response; ongoing risk stratification (ORS)

Recommendation II.1.A. After completion of initial treatment, ORS is recommended to continuously reassess disease status by integrating clinical, biochemical, and imaging findings. Recommendation level 3

[Clinical considerations] For the initial postoperative assessment of disease status:

- 1) Serum Tg and anti-thyroglobulin antibody (TgAb) levels are measured 6–12 weeks after total thyroidectomy under either TSH-unstimulated conditions or TSH-stimulated conditions (TSH >30 mIU/L).
- 2) Serum Tg and TgAb levels are measured 6–12 weeks after lobectomy while serum TSH level remains within the reference interval.

Recommendation II.1.B. Decisions regarding target serum TSH levels, follow-up surveillance modalities and intervals, and additional treatment strategies are determined by the integrated assessment of initial-risk stratification and ORS. Recommendation level 3

#### (1) Definition and purpose of ongoing risk stratification

After completion of initial treatment, including surgery and risk-adapted radioactive iodine (RAI) therapy, recurrence risk should be reassessed according to treatment response. This reassessment provides the clinical basis for early detection of recurrent disease and for selecting appropriate management strategies. Because disease status may change over time, reassessment should be performed regularly throughout follow-up after initial treatment and after any subsequent treatments.

Clinical, biochemical, structural and functional imaging, and cytopathological findings obtained during follow-up are integrated to reassess disease status and evaluate treatment response. The process of reassessing disease status, evaluating treatment response, and updating recurrence risk based on these findings was originally described as DRS, a concept first introduced in the 2009 ATA guidelines.

When DRS was first proposed, it primarily referred to the best objective response observed during the first 2 years after initial treatment. However, recurrence risk is not fixed by initial-risk stratification or by a single post-treatment assessment. Rather, it may evolve throughout long-term follow-up as new clinical information becomes available. Accordingly, the 2015 ATA guideline emphasized ORS, which continuously refines recurrence risk estimates using updated clinical, biochemical, and imaging data obtained during follow-up. Consistent with this concept, the 2025 ATA guideline incorporates ORS into the DATA framework as a longitudinal approach to disease assessment during long-term follow-up.

This guideline adopts the same approach by applying ORS at each follow-up assessment and using response-to-therapy criteria to guide the intensity of surveillance and subsequent management. The primary goal is to avoid unnecessary diagnostic procedures and therapeutic interventions in patients with a favorable prognosis while directing clinical attention and healthcare resources toward patients at high risk of persistent or recurrent disease.

Because DRS and ORS reflect the same principle of iterative risk reassessment during follow-up, the term ORS is used throughout this guideline. Table II.1.A summarizes the revised ORS response categories according to the extent of surgery and RAI remnant ablation status, and Table II.2.A presents the corresponding target serum TSH levels and follow-up surveillance strategies according to initial-risk stratification and ORS.

Based on ORS, patients are classified into one of four response categories according to clinical, biochemical, and structural findings obtained during follow-up: excellent, indeterminate, biochemical incomplete, or structural incomplete response.

Excellent response: No clinical, biochemical, or structural evidence of disease (indicating complete remission with no evidence of a residual lesion).

Indeterminate response: Nonspecific biochemical or structural findings that do not allow a definitive distinction between the presence and absence of disease. This category includes patients with stable or decreasing serum TgAb levels in the absence of structural evidence of disease.

Biochemical incomplete response: No structural evidence of disease, but serum Tg levels remain above the established threshold or show a rising trend over time, or serum TgAb levels increase during follow-up.

Structural incomplete response: Locoregional or distant disease detected by clinical evaluation or structural and/or functional imaging, including RAI whole-body scan (WBS), neck ultrasound, CT, 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron-emission tomography/computed tomography (18F-FDG PET/CT), or other diagnostic imaging modalities.

This risk stratification system helps to predict recurrence risk and should be applied throughout follow-up to guide individualized surveillance and management (Table II.1.A). It can be applied to patients who undergo total thyroidectomy with or without RAI therapy as well as to those treated with lobectomy. Disease assessment should incorporate findings from all available studies, including RAI WBS, neck ultrasound, CT, and 18F-FDG PET/CT.

#### (2) Ongoing risk stratification: criteria and application

ORS is based on post-therapy RAI findings, neck ultrasound, and serum Tg and TgAb measurement. For the initial postoperative disease-status assessment, serum Tg and TgAb are measured 6–12 weeks after surgery. Because the interpretation of serum Tg and TgAb levels depends on the TSH level, serum TSH should be measured concurrently.

In patients who have undergone lobectomy, if serum Tg is measured, the results should be interpreted when the serum TSH concentration is within the reference interval. In patients who have undergone total thyroidectomy, serum Tg and TgAb may be measured under either TSH-unstimulated or TSH-stimulated conditions. A TSH-stimulated state is defined as TSH >30 mIU/L. If RAI therapy was performed within this state, the TSH-stimulated serum Tg and TgAb levels obtained at the time of treatment can be used for disease state assessment.

Serum Tg and TgAb results obtained 6–12 weeks after surgery may help guide decisions regarding additional diagnostic evaluation or treatment. Additional imaging, including neck ultrasound, may also be useful in initial high-risk patients or when further clinical evaluation is warranted.

After completion of all initial treatments, including RAI therapy, treatment response is assessed at 6–12 months as shown in Table II.1.A. Thereafter, ORS is performed throughout followup to evaluate treatment response and disease status. However, whether ORS-reclassified response categories can be applied uniformly across all initial-risk groups remains unclear. Specifically, it is uncertain whether an excellent response in patients initially classified as high- or intermediate-risk confers the same recurrence risk as an excellent response in patients initially classified as low risk.

Several studies reported that even initially high-risk patients had a low risk of structural recurrence if they showed an excellent response at the first ORS assessment. However, one of these studies included only five high-risk patients, and other studies reported that, among initially high-risk patients with an excellent response at first ORS, 11% subsequently developed biochemical evidence of disease and 11% developed structural recurrence during follow-up. Therefore, initially high-risk patients require careful surveillance for recurrence even when they are classified as having an excellent response.

In initially intermediate-risk patients who show an excellent response at first ORS, the recurrence rate is approximately 2%. Reported predictors of recurrence in this group include lateral neck lymph node (LN) metastasis, TSH-stimulated serum Tg ≥ 1 ng/mL before RAI remnant ablation, and RAI uptake in lateral neck LNs. Additional useful predictors include FDG uptake on 18F-FDG PET and serum Tg doubling time. Therefore, patients within the same ORS category should still be managed by considering both their initial-risk group and other predictors of recurrence.

The extent of surgery and RAI remnant ablation status affect serum Tg and TgAb levels. A recent systematic review reported limited usefulness of serum Tg for identifying recurrence or metastatic lesions in patients with DTC who underwent lobectomy. Serum Tg values also differ between TSH-stimulated and TSH-unstimulated states, and further evidence is needed to clarify when stimulated Tg assessment is necessary.

#### (3) Serum Tg threshold in ongoing risk stratification

The 2024 KTA guideline incorporated serum Tg thresholds from the 2019 European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) guideline. Because evidence supporting specific Tg thresholds for patients who underwent lobectomy or total thyroidectomy without RAI remnant ablation was limited, the Guideline Committee conducted a systematic literature review and a multicenter retrospective cohort study as part of a KTA-supported clinical research project. The results showed no significant association between serum Tg levels and recurrence among patients who underwent lobectomy, and no clinically meaningful threshold for predicting recurrence could be identified. Therefore, serum Tg concentration thresholds were removed from the classification criteria for patients treated with lobectomy, and the indeterminate response and biochemical incomplete response categories no longer apply in this setting. This approach is consistent with the 2025 ATA guideline.

In contrast, for patients who underwent total thyroidectomy without RAI therapy, the thresholds for excellent response and biochemical incomplete response were maintained at 0.2 ng/mL and 5.0 ng/mL, respectively, rather than adopting the 2.5 ng/mL threshold for excellent response proposed in the 2025 ATA guideline. Because serum Tg levels are influenced by both tumor burden and the amount of remnant thyroid tissue after surgery, the Guideline Committee considered serum Tg thresholds supported by Korean clinical evidence to be more appropriate for clinical practice in Korea.

### II.1.1. Excellent response: no clinical, biochemical, or structural evidence of disease (complete remission)

Excellent response is defined as the absence of clinical, biochemical, or structural evidence of disease during follow-up (Table II.1.A). Patients with an excellent response are reclassified by ORS as having a low risk of recurrence, regardless of their initial-risk stratification. This reclassification may occur within weeks to months after completion of initial treatment. Accordingly, patients initially classified as intermediate- or high-risk may also be reclassified as having a low risk of recurrence if they achieve an excellent response. However, caution is warranted when evaluating excellent response in patients initially classified as high-risk.

This reclassification helps guide subsequent diagnostic evaluation and decisions regarding additional treatment. The biochemical criteria for excellent response according to the extent of surgery and RAI treatment status are as follows.

#### (1) Excellent response after total thyroidectomy with RAI therapy

In patients who underwent total thyroidectomy and RAI therapy, excellent response is defined as the absence of structural or functional evidence of disease, together with a TSH-unstimulated serum Tg <0.2 ng/mL or TSH-stimulated serum Tg <1.0 ng/mL, and negative serum TgAb. At initial postoperative risk stratification, 86%–91% of low-risk patients, 57%–63% of intermediate-risk patients, and 14%–16% of high-risk patients showed an excellent response, with a reported 10-year recurrence risk of 1%–4% in retrospective studies.

Notably, approximately two-thirds of patients initially classified as intermediate-risk were reclassified as having an excellent response at first ORS, with recurrence or persistence rates of 1%–2%, markedly lower than the 36%–43% predicted by initial-risk stratification. In the 5-year follow-up analysis of the large multicenter prospective randomized Étude STIMulation ABLation (ESTIMABL-1) trial, only one of 631 patients (0.16%) with initially low-risk and excellent response at 6–10 months developed structural recurrence in cervical LNs.

Even among patients initially classified as high-risk, the recurrence rate was approximately 1%–2% if they showed an excellent response at first ORS, although more intensive surveillance remains warranted. Because dedifferentiated thyroid cancer tissue may produce a reduced amount of Tg, additional structural and functional imaging should be performed to detect lesions that cannot be identified by neck ultrasound or Tg measurement when dedifferentiation is suspected. If no structural lesion is found, patients may be classified as having an excellent response.

#### (2) Excellent response after total thyroidectomy without RAI therapy

In patients who undergo total thyroidectomy without RAI therapy, or lobectomy with remnant thyroid tissue, the serum Tg criteria described above cannot be directly applied. Alternative serum Tg thresholds have therefore been proposed to define treatment response in these patients. However, clinical evidence establishing reliable TSH-stimulated serum Tg thresholds in these settings remains scarce. The 2019 ESMO guideline did not provide reference values for stimulated serum Tg in either clinical setting. Given these limitations, the present guideline also refrains from defining a stimulated serum Tg threshold and advises against routine stimulated serum Tg measurement in these patient groups.

TSH-unstimulated serum Tg levels are influenced by the amount of remnant thyroid tissue and therefore differ between patients who have undergone lobectomy and those who have undergone total thyroidectomy without RAI therapy. In this guideline, excellent response after total thyroidectomy without RAI therapy is defined as unstimulated serum Tg <0.2 ng/mL and negative serum TgAb (Table II.1.A), a profile associated with reported recurrence rates of 0%–3.6%. A recent study proposed defining excellent response in patients who underwent total thyroidectomy without RAI therapy as the absence of structural disease on ultrasound, negative serum TgAb, and stable or decreasing unstimulated serum Tg trends. In addition, a systematic review of five studies in this population suggested that serum unstimulated Tg levels of 1.0–2.5 ng/mL indicate a low risk of persistent or metastatic disease.

To evaluate the proposed criteria for excellent response, the Guideline Committee performed a systematic literature review and meta-analysis of seven studies. Although the result was not statistically significant, unstimulated serum Tg levels of 0.2–1.0 ng/mL or higher showed a trend toward a 3.01-fold higher pooled recurrence risk (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.80 to 11.30) compared with values below this range. Notably, rising serum Tg levels were associated with a markedly increased recurrence risk (pooled odds ratio [OR], 566) compared with stable or decreasing trends, whereas no recurrences were confirmed in patients with stable or decreasing serum Tg levels. Because the analyzed cohort included relatively few recurrence events, these risk estimates should be interpreted with caution.

A KTA-supported multicenter retrospective study of 9,753 Korean patients showed that, among individuals without recurrence, the median TSH-unstimulated serum Tg concentration was 0.1 ng/mL (interquartile range [IQR], 0.1 to 0.3) at 6 months after surgery and 0.1 ng/mL (IQR, 0.1 to 0.2) at 24 months. In TgAb-negative patients, the 10-year recurrence rates stratified by 6-month postoperative unstimulated Tg levels (<0.2, 0.2–0.3, 0.3–1.0, 1.0–2.5, 2.5–5.0, 5.0–10.0, and ≥10.0 ng/mL) were 0.2%, 0.3%, 2.6%, 3.2%, 7.1%, 10.5%, and 8.3%, respectively, at a TSH threshold of ≤2.0 mIU/L. When the TSH threshold was raised to <4.0 mIU/L, the corresponding recurrence rates remained similar (0.2%, 0.2%, 2.5%, 3.2%, 6.7%, 11.5%, and 7.1%, respectively), supporting an association between higher unstimulated serum Tg levels and higher recurrence risk when serum TSH is maintained below 4.0 mIU/L.

Although 10-year recurrence rates were comparable (0.2%–0.3%) between patients with unstimulated Tg values <0.2 ng/mL and 0.2–0.3 ng/mL, this guideline set the excellent-response threshold at <0.2 ng/mL. This cutoff reflects the median Tg value of 0.1 ng/mL in nonrecurrent patients, the detection limit of the Tg assay kits (0.1 ng/mL), and the functional sensitivity (0.2 ng/mL). Because patients who recur may show rising unstimulated serum Tg levels over time, continued surveillance is warranted for patients with an initial unstimulated serum Tg <0.2 ng/mL who later demonstrate an upward trend during follow-up.

Taken together, these findings support retaining an unstimulated serum Tg threshold of <0.2 ng/mL for defining an excellent response after total thyroidectomy without RAI therapy.

#### (3) Excellent response after lobectomy

In this guideline, the response-assessment criteria for patients who underwent lobectomy have been revised to exclude serum Tg concentration as a response criterion, consistent with the 2025 ATA guideline. Consequently, the TSH-unstimulated serum Tg and TgAb criteria from the 2024 KTA guideline have been omitted, and excellent response is now defined solely by the absence of structural disease, such as LN metastasis or other recurrent disease, on imaging studies. Benign nodules in the remnant thyroid are not considered structural abnormalities.

This change is supported by a KTA-supported multicenter retrospective study of 2,415 Korean patients with DTC who underwent lobectomy. The study found no significant association between specific serum Tg thresholds and recurrence risk and no statistically significant difference between patients with rising Tg trends and those with stable or decreasing trends.

Similarly, the Guideline Committee’s systematic review of seven studies showed that, although rising Tg levels after lobectomy were associated with a 4.04-fold higher recurrence risk compared with decreasing or stable levels, the finding was not statistically significant (95% CI, 0.91 to 17.93). Subgroup analysis showed no significant differences between Asian and non-Asian populations. Even when significant Tg increases were defined as ≥50% or ≥100%, the observed increases in recurrence risk (4.56- and 5.09-fold, respectively) were not statistically significant. Accordingly, biochemical criteria have been removed from the definition of excellent response, and the indeterminate response and biochemical incomplete response categories no longer apply to patients treated with lobectomy.

Nonetheless, three studies have reported that a persistent increase or a rapid rise in TSH-unstimulated serum Tg was associated with a higher risk of recurrence. Therefore, caution is warranted when serial serum Tg measurements demonstrate a persistent or marked increase.

### II.1.2. Biochemical incomplete response: abnormal Tg levels without structural evidence of disease

Biochemical incomplete response applies only to patients who underwent total thyroidectomy, regardless of whether RAI therapy was performed. It is defined as the absence of structural disease on structural or functional imaging together with abnormal TSH-unstimulated or stimulated serum Tg levels or persistently rising serum TgAb levels. Serum Tg thresholds differ according to whether RAI therapy was performed. Approximately 15%–20% of patients with DTC are classified as having a biochemical incomplete response after initial treatment. Most of these patients are eventually reclassified as having no evidence of disease by the end of follow-up, even without additional RAI therapy or surgery. However, rising serum Tg or TgAb levels are associated with an increased risk of locoregional recurrence or distant metastases and therefore warrant careful evaluation.

#### (1) Biochemical incomplete response after total thyroidectomy with RAI therapy

In patients who underwent total thyroidectomy with RAI therapy, the criteria for biochemical incomplete response remain unchanged from the 2024 KTA guideline. Biochemical incomplete response is defined as a TSH-unstimulated serum Tg ≥1.0 ng/mL or a TSH-stimulated serum Tg ≥10.0 ng/mL.

#### (2) Biochemical incomplete response after total thyroidectomy without RAI therapy

In patients who underwent total thyroidectomy without RAI therapy, the criteria for biochemical incomplete response remain uncertain. Less aggressive initial management strategies for DTC, including active surveillance, lobectomy, and more selective use of postoperative RAI therapy, have become increasingly used. Consequently, further evidence is needed to define the prognostic significance of serum Tg levels and appropriate response criteria in these patients. Given the limited clinical evidence, this guideline maintains the TSH-unstimulated serum Tg criterion of ≥5.0 ng/mL for biochemical incomplete response in patients who did not receive RAI therapy after total thyroidectomy, consistent with the 2024 KTA guideline.

The 2024 KTA guideline adopted the serum Tg criteria proposed in the 2019 ESMO guideline. To obtain additional clinical evidence, the Guideline Committee subsequently performed a systematic literature review. Among the studies identified, only one evaluated a TSH-unstimulated serum Tg threshold of 5.0 ng/mL during follow-up, and recurrence rates were higher among patients with Tg levels >5.0 ng/mL (1.36%, 3/221) than among those with stable or decreasing levels. In the aforementioned multicenter study of 9,753 Korean patients, 10-year recurrence rates exceeded 10% when TSH-unstimulated serum Tg was 5.0–10.0 ng/mL; 10.5% and 11.5% at serum TSH thresholds of <2.0 and <4.0 mIU/L, respectively. These rates differed significantly from the 3.4% recurrence rate observed at 1.0–5.0 ng/mL, supporting 5.0 ng/mL as an appropriate serum Tg threshold.

Taken together, these findings support retaining a TSH-unstimulated serum Tg threshold of ≥5.0 ng/mL for defining biochemical incomplete response after total thyroidectomy without RAI therapy.

#### (3) Biochemical incomplete response after lobectomy

The 2024 KTA guideline defined biochemical incomplete response after lobectomy as an increase in TSH-unstimulated serum Tg at comparable TSH levels. However, as described in the excellent-response section, the present guideline excludes serum Tg and TgAb from the response-assessment criteria in patients who underwent lobectomy. Therefore, biochemical incomplete response and indeterminate-response classifications no longer apply to these patients.

Nevertheless, as previously noted, a persistent or marked increase in unstimulated Tg level is associated with an increased risk of recurrence. Among patients previously classified as having a biochemical incomplete response after lobectomy or total thyroidectomy without RAI therapy, reported recurrence rates were 0%–9% in Korean studies and as high as 32% in non-Korean studies.

Accordingly, when serum Tg or TgAb is measured, serial changes should be evaluated. Caution is warranted when they show a persistent rising trend or a marked increase, similar to the approach used for biochemical incomplete response after total thyroidectomy. The clinical utility of serum Tg and TgAb during follow-up after lobectomy may be further clarified by an ongoing Korean multicenter prospective study.

### II.1.3. Structural incomplete response: persistent or newly detected locoregional or distant metastases

Structural incomplete response is defined by structural or functional evidence of locoregional or distant metastasis on imaging studies, including RAI WBS, neck ultrasound, CT, and 18F-FDG PET/CT. Structural lesions include biopsy-proven lesions and lesions considered clinically highly suspicious for metastasis. Structural incomplete response occurs in 2%–6% of low-risk, 19%–28% of intermediate-risk, and 67%–75% of high-risk patients.

Among all response categories, structural incomplete response is associated with the highest disease-specific mortality because additional treatment often fails to achieve cure. A Korean retrospective study of 715 patients with DTC who underwent total thyroidectomy and RAI therapy reported a structural incomplete response rate of 12% and a disease-free survival rate of 6.8% after a median follow-up of 8 years.

Prognosis varies according to the location of structural lesions. Over 15 years of follow-up, death occurred in 11% of patients with locoregional structural incomplete response and in 57% of patients with distant metastasis. Persistent or recurrent locoregional lesions showed high remission rates (29%–51%) after additional treatment, particularly surgery.

When remission was not achieved after additional treatment, most surviving patients remained in the structural or biochemical incomplete response group at the end of follow-up. Even among patients with structural incomplete response, prognosis varies according to the size and location of structural disease, the rate of disease progression, and RAI avidity. Therefore, treatment should be individualized according to each patient’s clinical circumstances.

### II.1.4. Indeterminate response: nonspecific biochemical or structural findings

Indeterminate response also applies only to patients who underwent total thyroidectomy regardless of whether RAI therapy was performed. It refers to cases in which biochemical, structural, or functional findings do not permit definitive classification as either complete response or persistent disease. These patients should be classified separately and monitored carefully as a distinct response category rather than being classified as having either an excellent or an incomplete response. Examples include nonspecific nodules <1 cm in the thyroid surgical bed on ultrasound, faint RAI uptake in the thyroid bed on RAI WBS with undetectable TSH-stimulated serum Tg, and detectable but persistently low and stable serum Tg levels. This category includes 12%–29% of low-risk, 8%–23% of intermediate-risk, and 0%–4% of high-risk patients.

Most patients classified as having an indeterminate response remain free of disease during long-term follow-up. However, 13%–20% eventually show biochemical, functional, or structural progression and require additional treatment. In a recent Korean study of low- to intermediate-risk patients who underwent total thyroidectomy without RAI therapy, 18%–30% showed an indeterminate response, but only 3.6% progressed to structural incomplete response during a median follow-up of 8.6 years. Therefore, in patients who underwent total thyroidectomy without RAI therapy, an indeterminate response appears to have limited prognostic impact.

Consistent with the 2024 KTA guideline, indeterminate response in patients with negative serum TgAb is defined as a TSH-unstimulated serum Tg of 0.2–1.0 ng/mL or a stimulated serum Tg of 1.0–10.0 ng/mL after total thyroidectomy with RAI therapy, or an unstimulated serum Tg of 0.2–5.0 ng/mL after total thyroidectomy without RAI therapy. In patients with positive serum TgAb, indeterminate response is defined as stable or decreasing serum TgAb levels without evidence of structural disease.

### II.1.5. Role of molecular markers in disease-status assessment

Telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) promoter mutations are identified in approximately 9% of patients with DTC in Korea. Among patients who achieve an excellent or indeterminate response after initial treatment, those with TERT promoter mutations have a significantly higher risk of structural recurrence than those without such mutations. In addition, the presence of multiple (coexisting) mutations, including BRAFV600E, RAS, and TERT promoter mutations, is associated with poor prognosis. Therefore, patients with molecular markers associated with poor prognosis may require closer follow-up than would otherwise be recommended based on their ORS category.

### II.2. Target serum TSH levels during follow-up according to disease status in patients with DTC

Key question 1. Does initial TSH suppression improve long-term prognosis in differentiated thyroid cancer?

II.2.A. Target serum TSH levels during follow-up in patients with DTC should be determined according to ORS. Recommendation level 1

II.2.B. Target serum TSH levels during follow-up in patients with DTC should be individualized according to each patient’s risk of recurrence, taking into account the potential benefits and harms associated with the target serum TSH level. Recommendation level 2

Follicular cell-derived thyroid cancer cells express TSH receptors on their cell membranes, thereby allowing TSH to stimulate tumor growth and progression. Serum TSH concentrations are regulated by endogenous thyroid hormone levels or by exogenous levothyroxine (LT4) therapy. Suppression of TSH to levels below 0.1 mIU/L in patients with advanced or high-risk thyroid cancer has been associated with significant reductions in recurrence and mortality rates. Based on these findings, TSH suppression therapy—the administration of high-dose LT4 beyond the physiological dose required to maintain normal thyroid function—was introduced to improve the long-term prognosis of thyroid cancer.

However, long-term target TSH levels should be individualized according to risk of recurrence. Target serum TSH levels span a broad spectrum, ranging from values just below the lower reference limit (LRL) to completely suppressed concentrations. The benefits of TSH suppression depend not only on the degree of suppression and structural recurrence risk but also on each patient’s risk of treatment-related complications. Therefore, uniformly suppressing TSH below 0.1 mIU/L in all patients with DTC is not appropriate. Excessive TSH suppression can adversely affect the cardiovascular system, musculoskeletal system, and overall quality of life.

From a cardiovascular perspective, TSH suppression therapy can increase heart rate and left ventricular mass, impair diastolic function, and reduce arterial compliance, thereby increasing the risk of atrial fibrillation, heart failure, and coronary artery disease. The cardiovascular risk associated with TSH suppression is particularly pronounced with advanced age, prolonged suppression, and preexisting comorbidities. A recent meta-analysis reported significantly higher risks of atrial fibrillation, cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, and all-cause mortality in patients with thyroid cancer.

Thyroid hormones have important roles in bone growth, development, and remodeling. Endogenous overt hyperthyroidism accelerates bone loss by increasing bone turnover, thereby increasing the risk of osteoporosis and fragility fractures. In patients with DTC, TSH suppression therapy has a more pronounced effect on bone mineral density (BMD) in postmenopausal women than in men or premenopausal women. For example, when serum TSH was suppressed below 0.4 mIU/L in low- to intermediate-risk patients, the incidence of osteoporosis was significantly higher than in patients maintained without suppression (hazard ratio [HR], 2.1; P=0.05). A recent meta-analysis also reported significant BMD loss in postmenopausal women receiving TSH suppression therapy, particularly when serum TSH was suppressed below 0.1 mIU/L.

TSH suppression therapy also affects quality of life in patients with DTC. In a Korean multicenter prospective randomized study of low- and intermediate-risk patients who underwent lobectomy for DTC, health-related quality of life scores assessed 3 months postoperatively did not differ significantly between the low-TSH group (target range, 0.30 to 1.99 mIU/L) and the high-TSH group (target range, 2.00 to 7.99 mIU/L). However, the score in the physical domain was higher in the high-TSH group. Therefore, to avoid complications related to unnecessary thyroid hormone excess, the target serum TSH level should be individualized through continuous disease assessment, balancing individual recurrence risk against patient-specific risks of treatment-related complications.

In summary, this guideline recommends that target serum TSH levels be individualized by balancing the oncologic benefits associated with different target serum TSH levels against each patient’s risk of treatment-related complications. The target serum TSH levels recommended in this guideline span a broad range, from maintenance within the reference interval to varying degrees of TSH suppression, depending on recurrence risk and clinical disease status. At the initial evaluation immediately after surgery, target serum TSH levels are determined according to postoperative initial-risk stratification. After completion of primary treatment, including RAI therapy, and throughout follow-up, target serum TSH levels should be reset according to continuous disease-status assessment using ORS (Table II.2.A). The target serum TSH thresholds presented in this guideline were adjusted on the basis of systematic literature reviews and recent KTA-supported multicenter studies. Overall, the recommended target serum TSH levels were individualized to reduce unnecessary LT4 use and minimize the systemic harms associated with chronic subclinical hyperthyroidism. Accordingly, the current guideline focuses on individualized target serum TSH levels rather than uniform TSH suppression.

### II.2.1. Target serum TSH levels immediately after surgery according to initial-risk stratification

Initial postoperative target serum TSH levels are determined according to the extent of surgery and postoperative initial-risk stratification (Table I.5.1.A). The 2024 KTA, 2015 ATA, and 2019 ESMO guidelines recommended target serum TSH levels of 0.5–2.0 mIU/L for low-risk, 0.1–0.5 mIU/L for intermediate-risk, and <0.1 mIU/L for high-risk groups, based on initial-risk stratification. However, evidence regarding the optimal initial target TSH level according to initial-risk stratification and extent of surgery remains limited.

Even among patients classified as low-risk on initial-risk stratification, the effect of target serum TSH levels on recurrence may vary according to the extent of surgery. In low-risk patients with National Thyroid Cancer Treatment Cooperative Study Group (NTCTCS) stage I–II disease who underwent total or near-total thyroidectomy, overall survival and disease-free survival did not differ significantly between patients with undetectable TSH suppression and those with serum TSH levels of 0.1–0.4 mIU/L.

In two randomized controlled studies that applied AMES (age, metastasis, extension, size) classification, recurrence rates did not differ among low-risk patients with serum TSH levels <0.01 mIU/L, <0.1 mIU/L, or within the reference interval. Therefore, initial TSH suppression is generally not recommended in low-risk patients with thyroid cancer who underwent total or near-total thyroidectomy.

Similarly, one prospective study reported no reduction in recurrence even when serum TSH levels were maintained <2.0 mIU/L in low-risk patients. Accordingly, the 2024 KTA guideline recommended maintaining initial target serum TSH levels at LRL–2.0 mIU/L without suppression below the LRL in low-risk patients, 0.1–2.0 mIU/L in intermediate-risk patients, and <0.1 mIU/L in high-risk patients, with further readjustment of the recommended target serum TSH levels.

In studies evaluating target serum TSH levels below 0.1 mIU/L, several reports found no benefit in reducing recurrence but reported increased risks of cardiovascular and osteoporotic complications. In analyses restricted to low-risk patients, recurrence risk was significantly higher at serum TSH ≥4.0 mIU/L than at serum TSH 0.5–2.0 mIU/L in both the total thyroidectomy group (HR, 1.08) and the lobectomy group (HR, 1.09), whereas no difference in recurrence rate was observed when serum TSH levels were maintained at 2.0–4.0 mIU/L. A recent meta-analysis of intermediate- and high-risk patients also showed an unclear benefit of maintaining serum TSH <0.5 mIU/L. Therefore, a risk-adapted TSH management strategy that accounts for prognosis and comorbidities is needed, rather than uniform TSH suppression.

To establish criteria appropriate for the Korean clinical setting, the committee conducted systematic literature reviews and multicenter studies. Based on these data, the guideline further de-escalates target serum TSH levels and recommends maintaining serum TSH within the reference interval for intermediate-risk patients, as for low-risk patients.

For patients with an indeterminate response on ORS, the recommended target serum TSH level was de-escalated from the biochemical incomplete response target to the excellent-response target, whereas the surveillance interval remained the same as that recommended for biochemical incomplete response. In high-risk patients, the initial target was changed from <0.1 mIU/L to below LRL, with subsequent readjustment according to ORS (Table II.2.A).

#### (1) Considerations for the reference range and target serum TSH levels

Previous guidelines defined a serum TSH reference range using a lower limit of 0.3–0.5 mIU/L, an upper limit of 4.0–4.5 mIU/L, and a median value of 2.0 mIU/L as reference points for target serum TSH levels. Because the serum TSH reference range varies according to population characteristics and assay methods, this guideline recommends using the upper reference limit (URL) and LRL established by each testing institution, when available. The reference range for the Korean population were well established as 0.62–6.86 mIU/L using data from the 2013–2015 Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES; measured with Roche TSH immunoassay). Based on these data, the KTA developed assay-specific estimated TSH values for different assay kits derived from correlation analyses (Table II.2.B). If an institution’s assay kit is included in Table II.2.B, the corresponding values may be used. Otherwise, a reference range of 0.3–4.0 mIU/L, derived from commonly used assay kits in Korea, may be applied.

The fixed TSH maintenance threshold of 2.0 mIU/L used for certain populations in this guideline does not account for interassay differences and corresponds to the midpoint of the previously used reference range with an upper limit of 4.0 mIU/L. To align this criterion with current assay kits, conversion to the assay-specific midpoint of the reference range may be appropriate. However, although many studies have used kit-specific upper reference limits, no studies have evaluated kit-specific adjustment for the 2.0 mIU/L criterion. Therefore, this guideline presents the 2.0 mIU/L threshold without interassay adjustment.

For the Roche TSH immunoassay, a median value of 3.33 mIU/L corresponds to the previous value of 2.0 mIU/L, and estimated values for other kits are also presented in Table II.2.B. Additional research is needed to clarify the clinical meaning of the 2.0 mIU/L criterion and the corresponding assay-specific median values.

[Target serum TSH levels used in this guideline]

- Reference interval: The reference interval for the Korean population, from the LRL to the URL.

- Lower reference limit (LRL): The medical decision point (MDP) of each assay kit corresponding to 0.62 mIU/L on the reference assay kit, or a fixed value of 0.3 mIU/L.

- Upper reference limit (URL): The MDP of each assay kit corresponding to 6.86 mIU/L on the reference assay kita, or a fixed value of 4.0 mIU/L.

- Limit of detection (LOD): The assay-specific LOD, typically 0.1 mIU/L.

- 2.0 mIU/L: A fixed target level that does not account for interassay variability.

*The midpoint of the reference interval for currently used assay kits corresponds to the assay-specific MDP equivalent to 3.33 mIU/L using the reference kita.

aThe Roche TSH immunoassay was used as the reference assay in the Korean population dataset (2013–2015 KNHANES).

When initiating or adjusting LT4 therapy to achieve the target serum TSH level, the following considerations apply. In patients who are not receiving LT4 after lobectomy, isolated serum TSH elevation with normal serum thyroid hormone levels (subclinical hypothyroidism) should generally be confirmed on at least one follow-up test before treatment is initiated. In patients with autoimmune thyroiditis, repeated testing after restriction of excessive iodine intake is recommended before treatment decisions are made.

In patients receiving LT4, adherence to dosing instructions should be verified before dose modification. Dose changes should be based on adherence, the degree of TSH change, thyroid hormone levels, and the target serum TSH level. Significant changes in body weight or concomitant medications that affect thyroid hormone metabolism should prompt immediate reassessment. When nonadherence is identified, reevaluation after patient education and repeat testing may be preferable before adjusting the dose.

#### (2) Target serum TSH levels immediately after lobectomy

Key question 2. Should target serum TSH levels be maintained after initial treatment for differentiated thyroid cancer when residual thyroid tissue remains?

II.2.1.A. In patients with DTC classified as low- or intermediate-risk at initial evaluation after lobectomy, serum TSH levels are initially maintained within the reference interval. Serum TSH levels may be individualized to <2.0 mIU/L according to the patient’s clinical circumstances and preference. Recommendation level 2

II.2.1.B. In patients with DTC classified as high risk at initial evaluation after lobectomy, serum TSH levels are initially maintained below the LRL. Recommendation level 2

[Clinical considerations]

1) Serum TSH targets may be adjusted within the reference interval after balancing potential benefits and harms, considering the patient’s age, comorbidities, and other clinical factors.

2) In patients not receiving LT4, if serum TSH levels become elevated while serum thyroid hormone levels remain within the reference interval, the need for LT4 therapy is determined after restricting excessive iodine intake and repeating thyroid function testing at least once.

3) The reference TSH range is based on the reference interval of the Korean population (2013–2015 KNHANES, 0.62–6.86 mIU/L using the Roche TSH immunoassay). When an institution-specific reference interval is unavailable, a lower limit of 0.3 mIU/L and an upper limit of 4.0 mIU/L may be used, based on the assay-specific reference intervals most commonly used in Korea.

4) In patients classified as high risk after lobectomy, completion thyroidectomy followed by RAI therapy should be considered. If residual thyroid cancer is confirmed, serum TSH levels are maintained below 0.1 mIU/L.

A meta-analysis evaluating the impact of TSH suppression after lobectomy (3,974 patients) showed no significant difference in recurrence rates between patients maintained at serum TSH <2.0 mIU/L and those maintained at serum TSH ≥2.0 mIU/L (OR, 1.45; 95% CI, 0.58 to 3.61). Another retrospective study reported that serum TSH levels above the reference interval were not significantly associated with recurrence; however, in intermediate- to high-risk groups, 10-year disease-free survival was significantly higher in the TSH 0.6–2.0 mIU/L group than in the ≥4.0 mIU/L group (84.4% vs. 69.4%). Another report found that LT4 supplementation itself was associated with a lower recurrence risk regardless of post-lobectomy thyroid function (HR, 0.236). Therefore, target serum TSH levels should be individualized according to the patient’s risk.

In a Korean multicenter study of 2,415 patients with DTC who underwent lobectomy, maintaining serum TSH within the reference interval after lobectomy was not associated with recurrence in low- to intermediate-risk patients; however, at serum TSH ≥4.0 mIU/L, 10-year disease-free survival was significantly lower than that in patients who maintained serum TSH levels within the reference interval (HR, 2.04).

Taken together, in patients with DTC who underwent lobectomy, rates of locoregional or distant recurrence did not differ between those managed with a predefined target serum TSH level and those managed without a predefined serum TSH target while maintaining serum TSH within the reference interval. Therefore, considering the greater need for LT4 therapy, more frequent laboratory monitoring, and the potential risks of treatment-related adverse events, target serum TSH levels below the reference interval are not recommended for patients initially classified as low- or intermediate-risk after lobectomy. However, depending on the patient’s clinical circumstances and preferences, the target serum TSH level may be individualized to <2.0 mIU/L while remaining within the reference interval.

Patients classified as high risk after lobectomy usually proceed to completion thyroidectomy; therefore, the 2024 guideline did not provide separate target serum TSH levels for this group. However, because completion thyroidectomy may be difficult in the short term after lobectomy in some cases, this guideline provides initial postoperative serum TSH targets for these patients. Although the distinction among initial-risk groups was unclear in the analyzed studies, the anticipated recurrence risk in this group supports maintaining serum TSH below the LRL in patients classified as high risk after lobectomy, as in patients who underwent total thyroidectomy. When residual thyroid cancer is confirmed after surgery, serum TSH should be maintained at <0.1 mIU/L. In patients with DTC classified as high risk at initial evaluation after lobectomy, completion thyroidectomy followed by RAI therapy is recommended, after which ORS should be performed and target serum TSH levels should be reassessed accordingly.

#### (3) Target serum TSH levels immediately after total thyroidectomy

Key question 3. Should serum TSH be suppressed in patients with differentiated thyroid cancer after total thyroidectomy?

II.2.1.C. Routine suppression of serum TSH below 0.1 mIU/L is not recommended in patients with DTC who have undergone total thyroidectomy. Recommendation level 1

II.2.1.D. In patients with DTC classified as low- or intermediate- risk at initial evaluation after total thyroidectomy, initial serum TSH levels are maintained above the LRL and below 2.0 mIU/L. Recommendation level 2

II.2.1.E. In patients with DTC classified as high risk at initial evaluation after total thyroidectomy, initial serum TSH levels are maintained below the LRL. Recommendation level 2

II.2.1.F. In patients with DTC who have residual disease, such as distant metastases, initial serum TSH levels are maintained below 0.1 mIU/L. Recommendation level 2

[Clinical considerations]

1) Serum TSH targets may be adjusted to within the reference interval taking into account potential benefits and harms, depending on the patient’s age, comorbidities, and other clinical factors.

2) The TSH reference range is based on the reference interval of the general population (2013–2015 KNHANES, 0.62–6.86 mIU/L using the Roche TSH immunoassay). When the institution-specific reference interval is unavailable, a lower limit of 0.3 mIU/L and an upper limit of 4.0 mIU/L may be used, based on assay-specific reference intervals derived from the assays most commonly used in Korea.

In a meta-analysis of studies of patients who underwent total thyroidectomy, serum TSH thresholds of 0.1, 0.5, and 2.0 mIU/L were not associated with statistically significant differences in recurrence risk in the overall population. This finding may reflect heterogeneity in clinical risk factors and treatment intensity among the study populations. In studies that included many low-risk and lobectomy patients, strong TSH suppression showed no recurrence benefit, and maintaining serum TSH ≥2.0 mIU/L was also not associated with a difference in recurrence risk in the overall analysis. However, after exclusion of one highly heterogeneous study in a sensitivity analysis, the estimated recurrence risk increased by 36%. Therefore, even in patients initially classified as low- or intermediate-risk after total thyroidectomy, this guideline recommends maintaining the initial serum TSH level within the reference range and below 2.0 mIU/L when LT4 therapy is required.

In sensitivity analyses of studies including many intermediate- and high-risk patients who underwent total thyroidectomy and RAI therapy, recurrence risk was significantly higher when TSH was maintained ≥0.1 mIU/L (2.27-fold; 95% CI, 1.29 to 3.99). In patients with DTC who had residual disease, such as distant metastases, recurrence risk increased 3.3-fold when serum TSH was maintained ≥0.1 mIU/L compared with <0.1 mIU/L. Accordingly, among patients classified as high risk at initial evaluation immediately after surgery, those with confirmed residual disease or distant metastases are maintained target serum TSH below 0.1 mIU/L from immediately after surgery, as recommended in the 2024 guideline. In other high-risk patients, the recommended target serum TSH level is de-escalated to below the LRL to reduce the risk of treatment-related complications. Therefore, target serum TSH levels should be reassessed according to ORS, which should be performed within 6–12 months after each treatment, especially when RAI therapy was performed.

[Excellent or indeterminate response]

II.2.2.A. In patients with DTC classified as low- or intermediate-risk who show an excellent or indeterminate response on follow-up evaluation, serum TSH levels are maintained within the reference interval. Recommendation level 2

II.2.2.B. In patients with DTC classified as high-risk who show an excellent or indeterminate response on follow-up evaluation, serum TSH levels are maintained above the LRL and below 2.0 mIU/L. Recommendation level 2

[Biochemical incomplete response]

II.2.2.C. In patients with DTC classified as low- or intermediate-risk who show a biochemical incomplete response on follow-up evaluation, serum TSH levels are maintained above the LRL and below 2.0 mIU/L. Recommendation level 2

II.2.2.D. In patients with DTC classified as high-risk who show a biochemical incomplete response on follow-up evaluation, serum TSH levels are maintained below the LRL. Recommendation level 2

[Structural incomplete response]

II.2.2.E. In patients with DTC who have residual disease, such as distant metastases, serum TSH levels are maintained below 0.1 mIU/L. Recommendation level 2

[Clinical considerations]

1) Serum TSH targets may be adjusted within the reference interval after balancing potential benefits and harms and considering the duration of follow-up, findings suspicious for recurrence during follow-up (particularly in patients with an excellent response on ORS), patient age, comorbidities, and other clinical factors.

2) In patients not receiving LT4, if serum TSH levels become elevated while thyroid hormone levels remain within the reference interval, the need for LT4 therapy should be determined after restricting excessive iodine intake and repeating thyroid function testing at least once.

3) In patients receiving LT4, adherence to dosing instructions should be verified when serum TSH levels change during follow-up.

4) The TSH reference range is based on the general-population reference interval (2013–2015 KNHANES, 0.62–6.86 mIU/L using the Roche TSH immunoassay). When the reference range is uncertain, a lower limit of 0.3 mIU/L and an upper limit of 4.0 mIU/L may be used, based on the assay-specific reference interval derived from assays commonly used in Korea.

No prospective studies or long-term follow-up data have yet established the optimal target serum TSH levels or duration of maintaining those targets for each ORS response category. However, in a retrospective analysis of 501 patients followed for a median of 105 months after RAI therapy, initial-risk stratification and the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) (8th edition) staging system showed a close correlation with ORS. Most patients with indeterminate or biochemical incomplete responses were subsequently reclassified as having an excellent response without additional treatment, whereas most disease progression and disease- related deaths occurred in patients initially classified as high-risk or in those with a structural incomplete response. Therefore, regardless of initial-risk group, patients with a structural incomplete response should maintain serum TSH below 0.1 mIU/L unless contraindicated. However, the target serum TSH level may need to be adjusted in patients with serious comorbidities or a high risk of treatment-related complications.

#### (1) Target serum TSH levels in patients with an excellent response on ORS

Recurrence risk is low in patients classified as having an excellent response on ORS. The 2019 ESMO guideline recommended maintaining serum TSH levels at 0.5–2.0 mIU/L in patients with an excellent response. The 2022 UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guideline recommended lifelong maintenance of serum TSH at 0.3–2.0 mIU/L in patients with an excellent response after RAI therapy, a population that largely includes high-risk patients. Considering these recommendations, this guideline does not recommend suppressing TSH to undetectable levels in any patient, including high-risk patients, who show an excellent response on ORS (Table II.2.A, Fig. II.2.A).

In the meta-analysis discussed in the section on target serum TSH levels immediately after surgery, strong TSH suppression showed no benefit in studies that included many low-risk patients and patients who underwent lobectomy. Although a sensitivity analysis excluding one highly heterogeneous study showed increased recurrence risk in patients maintained at serum TSH >2.0 mIU/L, and an additional analysis of the multicenter NTCTCS Registry showed a 2.6–2.7-fold increase in recurrence risk among patients with serum TSH >2.0 mIU/L even in low- to intermediate-risk groups, these results were derived from heterogeneous populations and cannot be directly applied to patients with an excellent or indeterminate response.

In a Korean multicenter study, the 10-year recurrence rates were 0.2%, 3.2%, and 11.8% for excellent, indeterminate, and biochemical incomplete responses, respectively, on the first ORS performed 6 months after total thyroidectomy. Given the low recurrence rate among patients with an excellent response, this guideline recommends maintaining serum TSH levels for low- and intermediate-risk patients within the reference interval. Even in initially high-risk patients, this guideline recommends maintaining serum TSH below 2.0 mIU/L within the reference interval, representing a less intensive target than that recommended immediately after surgery.

Among patients who underwent lobectomy, the previously described meta-analysis showed no reduction in recurrence when serum TSH was maintained <2.0 mIU/L. However, studies in intermediate- to high-risk groups showed reduced 10-year disease-free survival at serum TSH ≥4.0 mIU/L, and LT4 supplementation itself after lobectomy has been reported to be associated with reduced recurrence risk. In a Korean multicenter study, 10-year disease-free survival was significantly lower in low- to intermediate-risk patients with serum TSH ≥4.0 mIU/L after lobectomy than in those maintained within the reference range. Therefore, this guideline recommends maintaining TSH within the reference interval to reduce recurrence risk, even in low- to intermediate-risk patients with an excellent response after lobectomy.

Because this guideline does not apply Tg- or TgAb-based ORS categories after lobectomy, patients previously classified as having an indeterminate response or biochemical incomplete response after lobectomy are now included in the excellent-response group. In a Korean retrospective study of 619 patients with papillary thyroid carcinoma treated with lobectomy, structural recurrence during a median follow-up of 103 months occurred in 1.6% of patients in the excellent-response group, 3.8% in the indeterminate-response group, and 2.9% in the biochemical incomplete response group. A significantly higher recurrence rate was observed in patients with higher serum TSH levels at 1 year postoperatively (TSH 9.67 vs. 3.41 mIU/L), whereas serum TSH levels at 2 years were not associated with recurrence.

A recent meta-analysis of seven studies involving 2,455 low-risk patients maintained at serum TSH ≤2.0 mIU/L for an average follow-up of more than 5 years (mean, 60 months; median, 100 months) showed a low recurrence rate of 2.3%, with no significant difference compared with patients maintained above this level. Thus, even among patients previously classified as having an indeterminate response or biochemical incomplete response, evidence is insufficient to support maintaining serum TSH below 2.0 mIU/L.

Maintaining a serum TSH target ≤2.0 mIU/L after lobectomy requires LT4 therapy in a substantial proportion of patients. In previous studies, 73%–87% of patients required LT4 therapy within 1 year to maintain serum TSH ≤2.0 mIU/L. In studies defining hypothyroidism by an upper-normal serum TSH limit of 4.2–5.5 mIU/L, approximately 60% of patients progressed to hypothyroidism within 2 years after lobectomy, with an additional annual incidence of approximately 2% thereafter. An interim analysis from an ongoing prospective multicenter Korean study evaluating target serum TSH levels after lobectomy in low- to intermediate-risk patients showed that, at 48 months of follow-up, 97% of patients required LT4 therapy to maintain serum TSH <2.0 mIU/L. When serum TSH was targeted to the reference range, 50%–70% still required LT4 supplementation to maintain serum TSH within the reference range. However, if LT4 therapy were initiated according to conventional treatment thresholds for subclinical hypothyroidism (TSH 7.0 or 10.0 mIU/L), only 10%–20% of patients might require supplementation.

Nevertheless, concern remains about recurrence or the development of new thyroid cancer in residual thyroid tissue when serum TSH levels are maintained above the URL. Therefore, until further evidence becomes available, low- to intermediate-risk patients who underwent lobectomy and are assessed as having an excellent response—including those previously categorized as having an indeterminate response or biochemical incomplete response—should maintain serum TSH levels within the reference range unless there are concerns about LT4-related adverse effects.

In initially high-risk patients who underwent lobectomy and achieved an excellent response without additional treatment, serum TSH is maintained within the reference range but below 2.0 mIU/L (above LRL and <2.0 mIU/L). This target represents a less intensive serum TSH target than that recommended immediately after surgery and is similar to the target recommended for patients who underwent total thyroidectomy.

#### (2) Target serum TSH levels in patients with an indeterminate response on ORS

In this guideline, the clinical utility of serum Tg and TgAb measurements after lobectomy is considered limited; therefore, these parameters have been excluded from ORS. Accordingly, patients who underwent lobectomy are no longer classified as having an indeterminate response or a biochemical incomplete response.

Even among patients who underwent total thyroidectomy, the recurrence risk associated with indeterminate response or biochemical incomplete response remains uncertain. Consequently, evidence is insufficient to define the optimal target serum TSH level for these response categories. The UK NICE guideline recommends maintaining serum TSH at 0.1–0.5 mIU/L in patients with an indeterminate response regardless of initial-risk classification and advises an individualized assessment of the potential benefits and harms when deciding whether to continue TSH suppression in patients who have maintained suppression therapy for more than 10 years.

In a Korean multicenter study, among patients who underwent total thyroidectomy and showed an indeterminate or biochemical incomplete response on the initial ORS performed within the first 6 months after surgery, serum Tg levels decreased during follow-up in 58.8% and 84.0% of patients, respectively, at 2 years. The 10-year recurrence rates in patients initially classified as having excellent, indeterminate, or biochemical incomplete responses were 0.2%, 3.2%, and 11.8%, respectively, indicating that the recurrence rate was lower in the indeterminate-response group than in the biochemical incomplete-response group. Among patients with recurrence who initially showed an indeterminate response, 7.1% progressed to a biochemical incomplete response on ORS reassessment at 2 years.

Therefore, considering dynamic changes in ORS during follow-up and the relatively low recurrence rate among patients with an indeterminate response, this guideline recommends the same target serum TSH level as that recommended for patients with an excellent response after total thyroidectomy.

#### (3) Target serum TSH levels in patients with an incomplete response on ORS

In patients who underwent total thyroidectomy and have a biochemical incomplete response, serum TSH is maintained above LRL and <2.0 mIU/L in those initially classified as low- or intermediate-risk. In patients initially classified as high risk, serum TSH is maintained below the LRL because of their higher risk of recurrence. However, because the optimal duration for maintaining these target serum TSH levels remains uncertain, serum TSH levels may be adjusted even in high-risk patients with a biochemical incomplete response after considering trends in serum Tg or TgAb levels and ultrasound findings during follow-up. Specifically, serum TSH can be maintained below the LRL for the first 5 years; if disease status remains stable thereafter, adjustment to above LRL and <2.0 mIU/L can be considered.

However, an additional analysis of the multicenter NTCTCS Registry showed a 4.9-fold higher recurrence risk (95% CI, 1.29 to 18.70) in initially high-risk patients whose serum TSH was maintained >2.0 mIU/L. Therefore, even when the target serum TSH level is readjusted in high-risk patients, maintaining serum TSH ≤2.0 mIU/L is recommended. Conversely, when serum Tg levels are markedly elevated or show a rapid increase, maintaining serum TSH <0.1 mIU/L may be appropriate depending on serum Tg trend and disease status, rather than de-escalating the target serum TSH level.

#### (4) Target serum TSH levels in patients with a structural incomplete response on ORS

In patients with a structural incomplete response—that is, when recurrent or metastatic disease is confirmed—the available evidence supports maintaining serum TSH <0.1 mIU/L. In the analysis described above, post-treatment recurrence risk was significantly higher when serum TSH was maintained ≥0.1 mIU/L than when it was maintained <0.1 mIU/L (HR, 2.27; 95% CI, 1.29 to 3.99). In patients with DTC who had residual lesions, such as distant metastasis, maintaining serum TSH ≥0.1 mIU/L was associated with a 3.3-fold higher post-treatment recurrence risk than maintaining serum TSH <0.1 mIU/L. Therefore, regardless of initial-risk stratification, patients with confirmed residual disease or distant metastases should maintain serum TSH <0.1 mIU/L during follow-up unless the potential harms associated with maintaining this target level outweigh the expected benefits.

### II.2.3. Adjustment of target serum TSH levels during long-term follow-up

Key question 4. Should target serum TSH levels be raised in patients at high risk of treatment-related complications after surgery for differentiated thyroid cancer?

II.2.3. In patients at high risk of complications related to serum TSH lowering, a less stringent target serum TSH level than that recommended according to ORS may be considered. Recommendation level 2

[Clinical considerations] Patients at high risk of treatment-related complications include postmenopausal women, adults aged ≥75 years, patients with decreased BMD, and those with a history of atrial fibrillation. In these patients, maintaining serum TSH <0.1 mIU/L may further increase the risk of complications; careful individualized assessment is warranted.

#### (1) Adjustment of target serum TSH levels during long-term follow-up

In a U.S. prospective multicenter study, maintaining serum TSH <2.0 mIU/L significantly improved overall and disease-free survival in patients with thyroid cancer across all stages; however, excessive serum TSH suppression to undetectable levels provided no additional benefit. Therefore, considering the adverse effects of long-term TSH suppression, target serum TSH levels may be individualized according to the patient’s age and comorbidities when structural disease is not evident on response assessment.

Few reports have addressed when target serum TSH levels should be readjusted during long-term follow-up. In the study by Sugitani and Fujimoto, LT4 doses were reduced only when symptoms of thyrotoxicosis, decreased BMD, or adverse effects related to strong TSH suppression, such as angina or atrial fibrillation, were observed in patients with serum TSH suppressed below 0.1 mIU/L. Klubo-Gwiezdzinska et al. stated that it is necessary to evaluate treatment-related risks every 6–12 months and adjust the degree of TSH suppression accordingly, but they did not specify a time point for de-escalating serum TSH suppression.

Since individualization of DTC treatment, including risk-adapted TSH management, was first introduced in the 2015 ATA guideline, long-term evidence remains insufficient to define the optimal timing for readjusting target serum TSH levels. However, during long-term follow-up, particularly after treatment for structural lesions, ORS is performed approximately 6–12 months after treatment to reassess disease status, and target serum TSH levels are subsequently readjusted accordingly.

When concerns arise about potential harms from lowering target serum TSH level because of patient age or comorbidities, deescalation of TSH suppression may be appropriate. In a U.S. Surveillance Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER)-based study covering 1975–2016, causes of death other than thyroid cancer became predominant from 3 years after thyroid cancer diagnosis, accounting for 46% of deaths; cardiovascular disease was the most common cause, accounting for 21%. In a Korean single-center study, progression of osteoporosis was accelerated when serum TSH was maintained below 0.1 mIU/L for more than 4 years. Therefore, particularly in patients initially classified as high risk who have a biochemical incomplete response and are recommended to maintain serum TSH below the LRL, further de-escalation of the target serum TSH level may be considered at approximately 5 years of follow-up after initial or additional treatment. This decision should be made after weighing benefits and potential harms, even if the ORS classification itself has not changed, particularly when serum Tg or TgAb levels are stable or decreasing.

It remains uncertain whether further de-escalation is needed during long-term follow-up when target serum TSH levels are already maintained at or above the LRL, with the upper target defined as either 2.0 mIU/L or the URL. However, LT4 should not be prescribed in excess of the dose needed to maintain the recommended target serum TSH level. In a recent survey of U.S. endocrinologists and surgeons, TSH suppression therapy was used in 30% of patients with DTC at very low risk of recurrence, indicating a substantial gap between clinical practice and guideline recommendations. These findings support continued efforts to avoid unnecessary TSH suppression therapy and its associated complications in routine practice.

#### (2) Target serum TSH adjustment according to comorbidities and complication risk

In patients with DTC, the risk of cardiovascular complications may increase with long-term TSH suppression therapy. A recent meta-analysis showed that TSH suppression therapy with a median duration of 8.6 years was significantly associated with increased risks of atrial fibrillation (HR, 1.58; 95% CI, 1.40 to 1.77), ischemic stroke (HR, 1.14; 95% CI, 1.09 to 1.20), and all-cause mortality (HR, 2.04; 95% CI, 1.02 to 4.07). In addition, excessive TSH suppression to levels below 0.02 mIU/L in patients with DTC was associated with lower survival, and this association was more pronounced in patients with an excellent response to treatment. Therefore, target serum TSH levels should be individualized by balancing the potential benefits of lower target serum TSH levels against treatment-related risk.

Patients at high risk for complications related to serum TSH lowering include postmenopausal women, patients with a history of osteopenia or osteoporosis, older adults aged ≥75 years, and patients with a history of arrhythmia; the harms associated with TSH suppression therapy may be greater in these groups. Several studies have reported significant bone loss in postmenopausal women receiving TSH suppression therapy. In a randomized controlled study by Sugitani and Fujimoto, BMD decline was observed from 1 year after TSH suppression therapy in postmenopausal women, with more pronounced bone loss when serum TSH was excessively suppressed below 0.1 mIU/L. Furthermore, TSH suppression therapy lasting more than 6 years may be a major risk factor for vertebral fractures.

Older age further increases the risk of complications associated with TSH suppression therapy. In patients aged ≥75 years, TSH suppression therapy is associated with a higher risk of arrhythmia, osteoporosis, and insomnia. Therefore, even in high-risk patients for whom TSH suppression below the LRL is recommended, target serum TSH levels need to be readjusted during long-term follow-up according to age, comorbidities, and complication risk. In addition, appropriate monitoring and management of potential treatment-related complications should be considered in perimenopausal women at risk of bone loss, including assessment of the need for calcium and vitamin D supplementation and monitoring for osteoporosis progression. In older adults, beta-blockers may be considered to control tachycardia and other cardiovascular manifestations associated with thyroid hormone excess.

### II.3. Follow-up surveillance modalities in differentiated thyroid cancer

The principal surveillance modalities in patients with DTC include serum measurements of TSH, thyroid hormones, Tg, and TgAb, together with neck ultrasound. Additional investigations, including ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration (FNA), FNA washout Tg measurement, neck and chest CT, diagnostic RAI WBS, and 18F-FDG PET/CT may be performed when clinically indicated.

### II.3.1. Thyroid function tests

II.3.1.A. Serum TSH and thyroid hormone levels are measured at least annually in all patients receiving LT4. Recommendation level 2

II.3.1.B. Serum free thyroxine (T4) measured before the daily LT4 dose is maintained within the reference range whenever possible. Recommendation level 2

II.3.1.C. The minimum LT4 dose required to maintain the target serum TSH level is administered. Recommendation level 2

#### (1) Serum TSH

In all patients receiving LT4, serum TSH should be measured at least annually to guide LT4 dose adjustment. Serum TSH is also measured to confirm that the target serum TSH level is being appropriately maintained for recurrence prevention. This guideline recommends individualized target serum TSH levels according to ORS based on continuous disease-status reassessment during follow-up, while also considering initial-risk stratification and balancing potential benefits and harms. Consequently, during long-term follow-up, the target serum TSH level is maintained within the reference range for many patients.

However, the appropriate interval for measuring serum TSH remains unclear in low-risk patients who maintain target serum TSH levels without LT4 therapy. Approximately 60% of patients progress to hypothyroidism within 2 years after lobectomy, and an additional 2% develop new-onset hypothyroidism each year thereafter. Therefore, serial serum TSH monitoring is recommended to detect this progression. Thyroid function should also be measured whenever hypothyroidism is clinically suspected, including during long-term follow-up beyond 5 years after surgery.

#### (2) Serum thyroid hormones

To reduce adverse effects related to serum TSH lowering, the minimum LT4 dose required to maintain the target serum TSH levels should be administered. Serum free T4 measurement may assist with dose adjustment. Because serum free T4 measured immediately before the daily LT4 dose represents the lowest daily concentration, predose serum free T4 should be maintained within the reference range whenever possible.

Compared with serum free T4 and TSH, serum free triiodothyronine (T3) exhibits greater diurnal variation. In addition, serum free T4 levels tend to increase with aging, whereas serum free T3 levels gradually decline, making serum free T3 measurement less useful than serum free T4 measurement in many clinical settings. However, serum free T3 measurement may be helpful in selected patients, including older adults undergoing evaluation for hypothyroidism and patients being assessed for hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis dysfunction. Older adults with symptoms of hypothyroidism despite normal serum free T4 levels may have low serum free T3 levels. In addition, approximately 10%–15% of patients, particularly after thyroidectomy, continue to report persistent hypothyroid symptoms despite adequate LT4 replacement therapy. In such patients, serum free T3 measurement may provide additional clinical information.

Most current guidelines do not recommend combined LT4 and triiodothyronine (LT3) therapy as first-line treatment for hypothyroidism. Although some studies have suggested that combination therapy may benefit selected patients with low serum total T3 or free T3 levels after consideration of residual thyroid function, other studies have not shown this association. Further studies are therefore needed.

### II.3.2. Measurement of serum thyroglobulin and thyroglobulin antibody

II.3.2.A. Serum Tg level should be measured using the same assay method throughout follow-up and interpreted together with simultaneously measured serum TgAb levels. Recommendation level 1

[Clinical considerations] If TSH-unstimulated serum Tg increases during follow-up, repeat measurement is recommended to determine whether the increase reflects assay variability or analytical error.

Serum Tg measurement is a key component of surveillance for residual or recurrent disease, and serial changes in serum Tg trends are particularly informative. Progressive increases in TSH-stimulated or TSH-unstimulated serum Tg levels suggest that structural disease may subsequently become detectable. A shorter Tg doubling time is also a useful predictor of recurrence.

The ATA recommends the BCR-457 reference standard for serum Tg measurement and the use of a sensitive Tg assay. Most second-generation Tg assays currently used in Korea are standardized against BCR-457 and are highly sensitive, with a detection limit below 0.1 ng/mL and a functional sensitivity below 0.2 ng/mL. However, variability in serum Tg measurements may occur because of differences in the Tg epitopes recognized by assay antibodies, glycosylation patterns, molecular heterogeneity of Tg, and interference from other serum components. Consequently, results obtained with different assay methods may not be directly comparable, even when the same reference standard is used. Accordingly, serial serum Tg measurements should ideally be performed using the same assay method throughout follow-up. If the assay method changes, including when testing is performed at a different institution, this should be considered when interpreting serial serum Tg results. In addition, when an unexpected increase in serum Tg is observed during follow-up, repeat measurement is recommended to exclude assay variability or analytical error.

False-negative serum Tg results may occur in the presence of serum TgAb. Caution is required because interference from serum TgAb or other circulating antibodies may result in falsely low or undetectable serum Tg concentrations. Therefore, serum Tg should always be interpreted together with simultaneously measured serum TgAb levels.

A transient increase in serum TgAb may occur as a part of the immune response after surgery and may also be observed after RAI therapy. When Hashimoto thyroiditis is identified on surgical pathology, the possibility of serum TgAb positivity should be considered. If preoperative serum TgAb is negative, repeat serum TgAb measurement using an alternative assay during follow-up may be appropriate. If serum TgAb is high immediately after surgery in patients without Hashimoto thyroiditis, the presence of residual disease should be considered. Therefore, serum TgAb measurement before RAI therapy is recommended. Similarly, recurrence or progression should be considered when serum TgAb is initially positive, subsequently becomes undetectable, and then gradually increases during follow-up. Conversely, declining serum TgAb levels indicate a favorable response to treatment. Serial serum TgAb measurement using the same assay method may serve as a quantitative surrogate marker for changes in residual thyroid tissue or tumor burden and may aid in the interpretation of serum TgAb positivity associated with Hashimoto thyroiditis.

In patients who underwent total thyroidectomy with RAI remnant ablation and have no evidence of persistent disease during follow-up, serum TgAb levels usually become undetectable within approximately 3 years. Newly detected or rising serum TgAb levels are associated with a high risk of recurrent or persistent disease and have also been associated with increased mortality.

#### (1) Serum Tg in patients who underwent RAI remnant ablation

II.3.2.B. In patients with DTC who have undergone total thyroidectomy and RAI remnant ablation, TSH-unstimulated serum Tg levels are measured periodically. Recommendation level 2

II.3.2.C. In patients with DTC who have undergone total thyroidectomy and RAI remnant ablation, TSH-stimulated serum Tg levels may be measured when clinically indicated. Recommendation level 2

II.3.2.D. In patients with DTC who have undergone total thyroidectomy and RAI remnant ablation, additional evaluation for recurrence is considered when TSH-unstimulated serum Tg rises during follow-up. Recommendation level 2

[Clinical considerations]

1) Repeated TSH-unstimulated serum Tg measurements showing levels ≥1.0 ng/mL, or levels of 0.2–1.0 ng/mL with a persistent rising trend, are consistent with a biochemical incomplete response. Imaging studies may be considered to identify recurrent disease.

2) When TSH-unstimulated serum Tg levels are 0.2–1.0 ng/mL with a stable or declining trend, findings are consistent with an indeterminate response, and serum Tg levels may be monitored periodically.

3) When TSH-unstimulated serum Tg levels remain below 0.2 ng/mL during long-term follow-up, the interval for unstimulated serum Tg measurement may be extended, or the test may be performed only when recurrence is clinically suspected.

In patients who underwent total thyroidectomy and RAI remnant ablation, serum Tg is a highly sensitive and specific marker for detecting residual or recurrent disease and can also predict long-term disease-free survival in patients at low recurrence risk. However, false-negative TSH-stimulated serum Tg results may occur, even when sensitive assays are used, particularly in patients with small residual lesions, positive serum TgAb, or tumors with reduced Tg production or secretion. Therefore, serum Tg concentration should be regarded as a surveillance marker for clinically significant residual or recurrent disease.

TSH stimulation by LT4 withdrawal or recombinant human TSH (rhTSH) increases the sensitivity of serum Tg measurement for predicting recurrent disease. However, this evidence was derived from assays with a functional sensitivity of approximately 1 ng/mL. With the highly sensitive Tg assays currently used in Korea (functional sensitivity <0.2 ng/mL), TSH-unstimulated serum Tg measurement provides comparable predictive performance without the need for TSH stimulation.

In addition, when serum TgAb is negative and stimulated serum Tg level is <0.5–1.0 ng/mL, the probability of remaining recurrence-free during follow-up is approximately 98.0%–99.5%. Therefore, repeat stimulated serum Tg measurements during follow-up are generally unnecessary in patients with undetectable unstimulated serum Tg and no abnormal findings on neck ultrasound. Accordingly, in patients who underwent total thyroidectomy and RAI remnant ablation, serum Tg and TgAb are routinely measured under TSH-unstimulated conditions during long-term follow-up while maintaining the target serum TSH level. Stimulated serum Tg measurement should be reserved for selected patients when clinically indicated.

When unstimulated serum Tg remains below 0.2 ng/mL during long-term follow-up, the interval between serum Tg measurements may be extended, or testing may be reserved for situations in which recurrence is clinically suspected. This guideline recommends measurement intervals for unstimulated serum Tg according to the initial-risk stratification and ORS response category (Fig. II.2.A). Serum Tg is measured every 1–2 years in all low-risk patients, regardless of ORS response category, and in intermediate-risk patients with an excellent response. In intermediate-risk patients with an indeterminate or biochemical incomplete response, and in all high-risk patients, serum Tg is measured every 6–12 months. The measurement interval may be extended if disease status remains stable after approximately 5 years of follow-up in low- to intermediate-risk patients and after approximately 5–10 years in high-risk patients (Fig. II.2.A).

In some studies, approximately 20% of patients without clinical evidence of recurrent or metastatic disease and with unstimulated serum Tg levels <1.0 ng/mL had stimulated serum Tg levels >2.0 ng/mL at 12 months after initial RAI therapy. Approximately one-third of these patients were subsequently found to have persistent or recurrent disease, accompanied by rising unstimulated serum Tg levels. Therefore, even in patients with an excellent response, follow-up assessment should integrate clinical, biochemical, and imaging findings whenever clinically indicated.

#### (2) Serum Tg measurements in patients who underwent total thyroidectomy without RAI remnant ablation

Key question 5. In patients with differentiated thyroid cancer who did not undergo radioactive iodine remnant ablation after total thyroidectomy, is serum thyroglobulin measurement useful for predicting recurrence?

II.3.2.E. TSH-stimulated serum Tg measurement is not recommended in patients who have undergone total thyroidectomy without RAI remnant ablation. Recommendation level 2

II.3.2.F. In patients with DTC who have undergone total thyroidectomy without RAI remnant ablation, TSH-unstimulated serum Tg and TgAb levels are measured periodically during follow-up to assess the risk of recurrence. Recommendation level 2

II.3.2.G. In patients with DTC who have undergone total thyroidectomy without RAI remnant ablation, additional evaluation for recurrence should be considered when TSH-unstimulated serum Tg levels increase during follow-up. Recommendation level 2

[Clinical considerations]

1) When repeated measurements of TSH-unstimulated serum Tg show levels ≥5.0 ng/mL, or levels of 0.2–5.0 ng/mL with a persistent rising trend during follow-up, consistent with a biochemical incomplete response, imaging studies may be considered to identify recurrent disease.

2) When TSH-unstimulated serum Tg levels are 0.2–5.0 ng/mL with a stable or declining trend, consistent with an indeterminate response, serum Tg may be monitored periodically.

3) When TSH-unstimulated serum Tg levels remain below 0.2 ng/mL during long-term follow-up, the interval for unstimulated serum Tg measurement may be extended, or the test may be performed only when recurrence is clinically suspected.

In low- and intermediate-risk patients who did not undergo RAI remnant ablation or adjuvant treatment after total thyroidectomy, follow-up with TSH-unstimulated serum Tg measurement and neck ultrasound every 9–12 months has shown no abnormal ultrasonographic findings in most patients. In addition, TSH-unstimulated serum Tg remains <1.0 ng/mL, and serial Tg values remain low (<2.0 ng/mL) or gradually decline without additional treatment. Because more than half of these patients have stimulated serum Tg levels >1.0 ng/mL after rhTSH administration, routine TSH-stimulated serum Tg measurement is unnecessary. Instead, surveillance can be performed with periodic measurement of serum TSH and unstimulated serum Tg during LT4 therapy.

Although no definitive serum Tg cutoff has been established to distinguish residual thyroid cancer from remnant normal thyroid tissue, serial increases in serum Tg strongly suggest persistent or recurrent disease. In a Korean multicenter study of 9,753 patients who underwent total thyroidectomy without RAI therapy, unstimulated serum Tg levels tended to increase over time in patients who had recurrence, even when the initial serum Tg concentration was <0.2 ng/mL. These findings support the importance of monitoring serial serum Tg trends through continuous follow-up. If unstimulated serum Tg increases during follow-up, repeat measurement should be performed to exclude analytical error. If repeated measurements show serum Tg ≥5.0 ng/mL, or 0.2–5.0 ng/mL with a persistent rising trend during follow-up, imaging studies should be considered to identify recurrent disease. Conversely, when serum Tg levels are 0.2–5.0 ng/mL with a stable or declining trend, findings are consistent with an indeterminate response; periodic serum Tg monitoring should be continued.

Although recurrence rates are low in patients with an excellent or indeterminate response, and unstimulated serum Tg levels remain persistently below 0.3–1.0 ng/mL in most patients, recurrence can occur more than 5 years after surgery. Therefore, evidence remains limited regarding which patients can safely undergo longer intervals between unstimulated serum Tg or TgAb measurements, or when follow-up can be discontinued. However, when unstimulated serum Tg remains consistently below 0.2 ng/mL during long-term follow-up, the interval between measurements may be extended, or testing may be reserved for cases in which recurrence is clinically suspected.

This guideline recommends that the interval for unstimulated serum Tg measurement be determined according to initial-risk stratification and ORS response category, as in patients who underwent RAI remnant ablation (Fig. II.2.A). Accordingly, unstimulated serum Tg is measured every 1–2 years in all low-risk patients, regardless of ORS response category, and in intermediate-risk patients with an excellent response. In intermediate-risk patients with an indeterminate or biochemical incomplete response, and in all high-risk patients, unstimulated serum Tg is measured every 6–12 months. The interval between measurements may be extended if disease status remains stable after approximately 5 years of follow-up in low- to intermediate-risk patients and after approximately 5–10 years in high-risk patients (Fig. II.2.A).

#### (3) Serum Tg in patients who underwent lobectomy

Key question 6. Is serum thyroglobulin measurement useful for predicting recurrence in patients with differentiated thyroid cancer who underwent thyroid lobectomy?

II.3.2.H. TSH-stimulated serum Tg testing is not recommended in patients who have undergone lobectomy. Recommendation level 2

II.3.2.I. In patients with DTC who have undergone lobectomy, measurement of TSH-unstimulated serum Tg levels during follow-up may be considered. Recommendation level 3

II.3.2.J. In patients with DTC who have undergone lobectomy, additional evaluation for recurrence is considered when TSH-unstimulated serum Tg levels are markedly elevated or show a persistent rising trend during follow-up. Recommendation level 2

[Clinical considerations]

1) The predictive value of TSH-unstimulated serum Tg and TgAb measurements for recurrence remains uncertain, and clinically meaningful thresholds have not been clearly established. However, these measurements may be particularly helpful in the following situations:

- Measurement 6–12 weeks after surgery to detect unexpectedly elevated serum Tg or TgAb levels.

- When recurrent disease or distant metastasis, particularly bone metastasis, is clinically suspected during follow-up.

2) When no recurrent lesion is identified despite a persistent rise in TSH-unstimulated serum Tg or TgAb levels, ongoing surveillance with serial TSH-unstimulated serum Tg and TgAb measurement is recommended.

Unlike in patients who underwent total thyroidectomy with RAI therapy, serum Tg and TgAb measurements after lobectomy have limited predictive value for recurrence or metastasis because the presence of remnant normal thyroid tissue complicates interpretation of their levels. In this population, postoperative baseline serum Tg levels vary widely, and no definitive thresholds for identifying recurrence have been established; therefore, serum Tg results should be interpreted with caution. High-quality evidence also remains limited regarding the optimal frequency of Tg measurement and the clinical significance of serial Tg trends, underscoring the need for further research.

Because the clinical utility of routine serum Tg monitoring after lobectomy remains uncertain, periodic serum Tg measurement may not be necessary in all patients. When serum Tg levels remain stable or show no rising trend, measurement may be reserved for situations in which recurrence is clinically suspected. Ultimately, the decision to monitor serum Tg after lobectomy should be individualized according to the patient’s clinical circumstances.

Given these limitations and the need for validation in large prospective and retrospective studies, this guideline does not incorporate serum Tg or TgAb measurements into the ORS system for patients who have undergone lobectomy. However, serum Tg or TgAb measurement may still be useful in selected clinical situations. Specifically, measurement 6–12 weeks after surgery may help establish a baseline and detect unexpectedly elevated serum Tg or TgAb levels. Serial measurement may also be useful during follow-up when recurrent disease or distant metastasis, particularly bone metastasis, is clinically suspected.

When unstimulated serum Tg levels are markedly elevated or show a persistent rising trend, additional diagnostic evaluation for recurrence is considered. Even in the absence of a structurally confirmed recurrent lesion, patients with continuous increases in unstimulated serum Tg or TgAb require close ongoing surveillance. Finally, because serum TgAb can interfere with serum Tg measurements and lead to falsely low or undetectable serum Tg levels, this potential assay interference should always be considered when interpreting serum Tg results.

### II.3.3. Neck ultrasound

Key question 7. Is neck ultrasound effective for detecting thyroid bed recurrence and cervical lymph node metastases during follow-up after thyroidectomy in patients with differentiated thyroid cancer?

II.3.3.A. Neck ultrasound should be performed after thyroid cancer surgery to detect thyroid bed recurrence and cervical LN metastasis. Recommendation level 1

Neck ultrasound, performed using a high-frequency transducer (10–15 MHz), is a sensitive imaging modality for detecting thyroid bed recurrence and cervical LN metastases in DTC, particularly papillary thyroid carcinoma. It is also useful for evaluating suspicious findings and residual disease after surgery. Accordingly, multiple international guidelines recommend neck ultrasound as the primary imaging modality for detecting thyroid bed recurrence and cervical LN metastases, and other suspicious postoperative lesions. When ultrasound demonstrates findings suspicious for metastatic LNs, the likelihood of true metastasis is high (73%–90%).

Neck ultrasound performed during postoperative follow-up should include evaluation of the thyroid surgical bed and all cervical LN compartments. Although specific ultrasonographic criteria for thyroid bed recurrence have not been established, several studies have reported common suspicious findings, including hypoechoic lesions, increased internal vascularity, microcalcifications, irregular margins, and an anteroposterior dimension greater than transverse dimension.

According to the 2021 Korean Society of Thyroid Radiology guideline, ultrasonographic features suggestive of metastatic LNs include focal or diffuse cortical hyperechogenicity, echogenic foci or calcifications, cystic change, and abnormal peripheral or diffuse vascularity on color Doppler imaging. The presence of any one of these features may be considered suggestive of metastatic LN involvement. LNs that lack suspicious ultrasonographic features but also lack a visible echogenic hilum or hilar vascularity are classified as indeterminate.

International and Korean guidelines recommend performing neck ultrasound 6–12 months after surgery to evaluate residual disease, thyroid bed recurrence, and cervical LN metastases. However, neck ultrasound may be performed earlier when clinically indicated to evaluate suspected residual disease. The 2013 European Thyroid Association guideline recommended ultrasonographic evaluation of the lateral cervical LNs at the time of RAI remnant ablation (1–3 months after total thyroidectomy) in selected cases: when cancer was incidentally diagnosed on final pathology after thyroid surgery; when detailed preoperative ultrasound was unavailable to assess cervical LN metastasis; when significant uptake outside the thyroid bed was observed on post-ablation WBS; or when serum Tg before RAI remnant ablation was substantially higher than expected based on the residual thyroid tissue identified on post-treatment WBS.

When neck ultrasound is performed, clinical and biochemical findings should also be considered. The risk of thyroid bed recurrence and cervical LN metastases is associated with the presence and number of LN metastases at the time of initial diagnosis. In particular, larger metastatic LNs (>3 cm), a greater number of metastatic LNs, extranodal extension of metastatic LNs, and incomplete macroscopic tumor removal are associated with increased risk of cervical recurrence. Therefore, even when no residual disease has been identified after surgery, additional imaging studies, including neck ultrasound, should be considered when clinically indicated (Fig. II.2.A).

In patients with serum Tg <2.0 ng/mL after RAI remnant ablation, neck ultrasound performed 6–12 months after surgery had a high negative predictive value (97.7%) when no suspicious findings of thyroid bed recurrence or cervical LN metastases were identified. In addition, neck ultrasound may provide additional information regarding residual disease, thyroid bed recurrence, or cervical LN metastases when serum Tg results are difficult to interpret in the early postoperative period. After the initial examination at 6–12 months, subsequent surveillance intervals are determined according to recurrence risk and serum Tg levels. Accordingly, this guideline recommends surveillance intervals for neck ultrasound and the use of additional imaging according to initial-risk stratification and ORS response category (Fig. II.2.A).

### II.3.4. Ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration (FNA) and FNA washout Tg assay

Key question 8. Are ultrasound-guided FNA and thyroglobulin measurement in FNA washout fluid useful for diagnosing thyroid bed recurrence and cervical lymph node metastases after thyroidectomy in patients with differentiated thyroid cancer?

II.3.4.A. Ultrasound-guided FNA may be considered when neck ultrasound demonstrates findings suspicious for thyroid bed recurrence or cervical LN metastases. Recommendation level 2

II.3.4.B. When neck ultrasound demonstrates findings suspicious for thyroid bed recurrence or cervical LN metastases, Tg measurement in FNA washout fluid may be considered. Recommendation level 2

In patients with DTC who are suspected of having cervical LN metastases after surgery, ultrasound-guided FNA and FNA washout Tg measurement are recommended according to LN size and ultrasound findings. According to the 2021 Korean Society of Thyroid Radiology guideline, FNA is recommended for LNs with suspicious ultrasonographic features and a short-axis diameter >3–5 mm, or for an indeterminate node with a short-axis diameter >5 mm.

FNA washout Tg measurement is generally recommended for lateral cervical LNs. For central cervical LNs, it is recommended for suspicious lesions when contamination from residual normal thyroid tissue is unlikely. However, for small suspected recurrent lesions in the thyroid bed or suspected cervical metastatic LNs with a short-axis diameter ≤8–10 mm, ultrasonographic surveillance without FNA may be appropriate if immediate treatment is not planned. If the lesion subsequently enlarges or invasion of adjacent vital structures is suspected, FNA may be considered to guide subsequent management. Therefore, the decision to perform FNA should be based on clinical findings, patient factors, lesion location, the risk of invasion into vital structures, and anticipated management following the diagnosis of recurrent disease, including active surveillance, nonsurgical ablation, and surgery.

When FNA washout Tg measurement is performed together with FNA in patients with suspected thyroid bed recurrence or cervical LN metastases after surgery, diagnostic sensitivity is higher than that of FNA alone. In a meta-analysis of 2,257 patients, the diagnostic sensitivities of FNA alone, FNA washout Tg measurement alone, and combined FNA with FNA washout Tg measurement were 81.3%, 94.5%, and 96.8%, respectively, and the corresponding diagnostic specificities were 96.4%, 88.8%, and 93.2%. Because cervical LN metastases with marked cystic change may yield nondiagnostic FNA results, combined assessment with FNA washout Tg measurement is recommended in these cases.

FNA washout Tg measurement is not significantly affected by serum TSH or TgAb. However, its limitations include the lack of standardized assay methods, undefined diagnostic thresholds, and the possibility of false-positive results caused by blood contamination in patients with high serum Tg levels. Although definitive diagnostic thresholds have not been established, when FNA washout Tg levels exceed 1.0 ng/mL in patients who underwent lobectomy or total thyroidectomy, reported sensitivities for detecting metastatic LNs range from 90.8% to 100%, with corresponding specificities ranging from 90.0% to 96.5%. In addition, metastatic LNs can be suspected when FNA washout Tg levels exceed serum Tg levels. In a study of 68 metastatic LNs, this criterion showed a sensitivity of 98.2% and a specificity of 77.8%.

FNA washout Tg measurement may yield false-negative results in non-DTC malignancies and some papillary thyroid cancers, and false-positive results may occur in ectopic or residual thyroid tissue that mimics metastatic LNs. Therefore, FNA washout Tg results should be interpreted together with FNA cytology results.

Possible adverse effects after FNA include pain, bleeding, and tumor seeding. If an adequate specimen is not obtained, repeat aspiration may be required. However, a recent systematic review concluded that FNA is safe because adverse effects are generally minor and major complications are rare. Therefore, after appropriate counseling regarding its potential adverse effects, FNA can be performed safely when clinically indicated.

Currently, ultrasound-guided FNA combined with FNA washout Tg measurement is the most commonly used diagnostic approach for evaluating suspected recurrent disease. However, core needle biopsy may be useful when undifferentiated thyroid carcinoma, medullary thyroid carcinoma, or thyroid lymphoma is suspected. Core needle biopsy has demonstrated high diagnostic performance for metastatic LNs or LN involvement by nonthyroidal malignancies. Recent studies have also demonstrated that core needle biopsy is safe and diagnostically accurate for thyroid surgical-bed lesions, suggesting that it may be considered as a complementary diagnostic modality for suspected recurrent disease.

### II.3.5. Neck and chest computed tomography

Key question 9. Is neck CT effective for detecting locoregional recurrence during follow-up after thyroidectomy in patients with differentiated thyroid cancer?

II.3.5.A. Contrast-enhanced neck CT may be considered when neck ultrasound demonstrates findings suspicious for thyroid bed recurrence or cervical LN metastases after thyroid surgery. Recommendation level 3

II.3.5.B. Contrast-enhanced neck CT may be considered when neck ultrasound shows no suspicious findings despite elevated serum Tg levels, when lesions are large or extensive and cannot be adequately evaluated by ultrasound, or when invasion of the respiratory or digestive organs is suspected. Recommendation level 3

II.3.5.C. Chest CT may be considered in high-risk patients or in patients with elevated serum Tg or TgAb levels. Recommendation level 3

International and Korean guidelines do not recommend contrast-enhanced neck CT as a primary surveillance modality after thyroid cancer surgery but rather as an adjunct to neck ultrasound. Contrast-enhanced neck CT is useful for evaluating suspected recurrent disease that is difficult to assess by ultrasound, determining invasion of adjacent structures such as the airway and esophagus, and defining the relationship between ultrasonographically detected lesions and adjacent vessels. It is also useful for evaluating central and mediastinal LN metastases and lesions posterior to the trachea that are difficult to assess by ultrasound. A study comparing CT and ultrasound for differentiating recurrent disease from postoperative cervical traumatic neuromas demonstrated higher sensitivity and specificity for CT; therefore, CT may be considered as an additional imaging modality when recurrence is suspected.

According to the ATA and Korean Society of Thyroid Radiology guidelines, contrast-enhanced neck CT is recommended when thyroid bed recurrence or cervical LN metastases are suspected, when lesions are too large or extensive to be adequately evaluated by ultrasound, or when invasion of the upper respiratory or digestive tract by recurrent thyroid cancer is suspected. Neck CT is also recommended when serum Tg or TgAb levels are elevated or show a persistent rising trend but no structural lesion is identified on neck ultrasound or RAI imaging, particularly for evaluating mediastinal lesions or lesions posterior to the trachea that are difficult to assess by ultrasound.

Neck magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can also be used to evaluate the neck and mediastinum. Although it is not recommended as a primary surveillance modality, it may be considered for further assessment of the extent of tumor invasion or deep tissue involvement when advanced recurrent cancer is suspected to invade the airway, esophagus, or other adjacent structures. However, evidence supporting the diagnostic performance of MRI for detecting recurrent cancer remains limited.

Chest CT is useful for detecting pulmonary metastases including micrometastases, as well as mediastinal LN or pleural metastases, and may be considered in high-risk patients or in those with elevated serum Tg or TgAb levels. In general, contrast-enhanced CT is preferred for evaluating thoracic metastatic disease; however, noncontrast chest CT can be performed in patients with contraindications to iodinated contrast media or a history of adverse reactions to contrast agents.

### II.3.6. Diagnostic radioactive iodine whole-body scan

Key question 10. In patients with differentiated thyroid cancer undergoing diagnostic radioactive iodine whole-body scanning, does the addition of SPECT/CT improve diagnostic performance?

II.3.6.A. Diagnostic RAI WBS may be considered as part of ORS in patients with DTC under specific conditions, such as the presence of abnormal uptake outside the thyroid bed on post-treatment WBS after RAI therapy. Recommendation level 2

II.3.6.B. Diagnostic RAI WBS is not recommended during follow-up in low-risk patients with DTC who show an excellent response after RAI therapy. Recommendation level 1

II.3.6.C. In intermediate- and high-risk patients with DTC, diagnostic RAI WBS may be considered 6–12 months after RAI adjuvant therapy to evaluate treatment response. Recommendation level 2

II.3.6.D. In intermediate- and high-risk patients who demonstrate an excellent response on evaluation including WBS, additional diagnostic RAI WBS during follow-up is generally not recommended. Recommendation level 2

[Clinical considerations] Additional diagnostic RAI WBS may be considered during follow-up according to ORS response category.

II.3.6.E. When abnormal uptake is suspected on planar imaging during diagnostic RAI WBS, additional SPECT/CT is recommended. Recommendation level 1

In low-risk patients who show no RAI uptake outside the thyroid bed on post-treatment WBS after RAI remnant ablation and demonstrate an excellent response on ORS, repeat diagnostic RAI WBS is unnecessary. An excellent response is defined as the absence of clinical evidence of residual disease, undetectable TSH-unstimulated serum Tg levels, and negative findings on neck ultrasound.

Diagnostic RAI WBS may be considered in the following situations:

● Abnormal uptake outside the thyroid bed is identified on post-treatment WBS.

● Extensive RAI uptake in the thyroid remnant makes accurate assessment of RAI uptake in cervical LN regions difficult.

● Serum TgAb is positive, limiting the reliable interpretation of post-treatment serum Tg levels.

For RAI WBS, either a diagnostic dose (typically 2–5 mCi) or a therapeutic dose (30–150 mCi) of RAI is administered before whole-body imaging. When abnormal RAI uptake is identified, additional spot images of the corresponding region or tomographic imaging with single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) or SPECT/CT may be performed. SPECT/CT accurately localizes abnormal RAI uptake by integrating anatomical and functional imaging information, while the associated radiation exposure is not substantially greater than that of contrast-enhanced CT. The addition of SPECT/CT to WBS improves the detection or localization of LN metastases, provides additional information for lesions without definite RAI uptake on planar imaging, reduces equivocal findings, and changes clinical management in a substantial proportion of patients. Therefore, additional SPECT/CT is recommended when abnormal or equivocal findings are identified on planar RAI WBS.

For diagnostic WBS, 123I is preferred over 131I because 123I delivers a lower radiation dose and provides higher-quality images. To confirm the presence of residual thyroid tissue or residual thyroid cancer and to assess RAI avidity, either rhTSH stimulation or LT4 withdrawal can be considered before imaging. Although no significant difference in scan results has been reported between the two preparation methods, rhTSH is preferred when preservation of quality of life is an important consideration.

### II.3.7. 18F-FDG PET/CT

Key question 11. In patients with differentiated thyroid cancer who have elevated serum Tg or TgAb levels but negative RAI WBS during follow-up, does 18F-FDG PET/CT provide additional diagnostic value?

II.3.7.A. In postoperative patients with DTC who have elevated serum Tg or TgAb levels but negative RAI WBS findings, 18F-FDG PET/CT should be considered. Recommendation level 1

II.3.7.B. 18F-FDG PET/CT may also be considered in the following situations. Recommendation level 3

B1. To identify lesions in patients with suspected metastatic disease.

B2. To assess prognosis in patients with rapidly progressive disease or a high risk of disease-specific mortality

B3. To assess treatment response after systemic or local treatment of residual or recurrent disease

B4. When RAI-refractory disease is suspected in patients with structural disease

18F-FDG PET/CT is useful in postoperative patients with DTC who have elevated serum Tg or TgAb levels despite negative RAI WBS findings. Several meta-analyses have reported its high diagnostic performance in this setting, with sensitivity of 86%–93% and specificity of 81%–85%.

Several factors can influence the diagnostic performance of 18F-FDG PET/CT. Sensitivity is higher in patients with aggressive histological types, including poorly differentiated thyroid carcinoma (PDTC), aggressive subtypes of papillary thyroid cancer, invasive oncocytic carcinoma, and high-grade differentiated thyroid carcinoma. Although some studies have suggested that TSH stimulation may slightly improve the sensitivity of 18F-FDG PET/CT, meta-analyses have shown no significant difference in diagnostic performance between TSH-stimulated and TSH-suppressed states. Several studies have reported a trend toward increasing sensitivity with elevated serum Tg levels, but there is currently insufficient evidence to define a serum Tg threshold for determining whether 18F-FDG PET/CT should be performed.

The Guideline Committee conducted a meta-analysis to evaluate the usefulness of 18F-FDG PET/CT for follow-up surveillance in postoperative patients with DTC. In the meta-analysis, which included a total of 1,988 patients from 24 studies published between 2012 and 2023, the pooled sensitivity and specificity of 18F-FDG PET/CT were 87% (95% CI, 83% to 92%) and 84% (95% CI, 80% to 89%), respectively. When patients with elevated serum Tg alone and those with elevated serum TgAb alone were analyzed separately, the diagnostic performance of 18F-FDG PET/CT did not differ significantly.

A pooled analysis of six studies evaluating the impact of 18FFDG PET/CT on the treatment strategy showed that the management changed after 18F-FDG PET/CT in 40% of patients (95% CI, 34% to 47%). Treatment changes included radical surgery for recurrent lesions detected on 18F-FDG PET/CT, a change from planned radical surgery to targeted therapy or RAI therapy because of detection of additional lesions, and modification of planned RAI therapy, including dose adjustment or conversion to surgery or external beam radiation therapy. In addition, when 18F-FDG PET/CT was performed in patients with suspected structural recurrence identified on prior imaging studies, the management strategy changed in 50% of cases.

In nine studies, recurrent lesions detected by 18F-FDG PET/CT were located in the thyroid bed and cervical LNs in 62% of cases, lungs in 8.4%, mediastinal LNs in 10.5%, bone in 6.3%, and other distant metastatic sites in 1.0%. At the patient level, locoregional recurrence and distant metastases were identified simultaneously in 12.5% of patients. When patients with isolated distant metastasis were also included, distant metastasis was present in 38.7% of the patients overall. Because 18F-FDG PET/CT has high diagnostic performance and provides whole-body evaluation in a single examination, it can be considered in patients with elevated serum Tg or TgAb levels and suspected recurrent disease. However, few studies have directly compared the diagnostic performance of different imaging modalities in those situations. Therefore, further evidence regarding diagnostic performance and cost-effectiveness is needed to determine the optimal imaging modality in this clinical setting.

18F-FDG PET/CT may also play a complementary role in patients with RAI-avid metastatic disease by identifying coexisting non-RAI-avid metastatic lesions through 18F-FDG uptake. In addition, 18F-FDG uptake in metastatic DTC is an important negative predictor of response to RAI therapy and an independent predictor of poorer survival. Although high 18F-FDG uptake is not included in the current definition of RAI-refractoriness, it tends to be associated with low RAI uptake, and lesions with this pattern may be RAI-refractory. 18F-FDG PET/CT may also be useful for assessing treatment response and predicting prognosis during systemic therapy for RAI-refractory DTC.

### II.4. Follow-up surveillance according to ORS in DTC

There is no consensus regarding long-term surveillance intervals after surgery. However, the type and frequency of follow-up surveillance should be determined according to the initial postoperative risk stratification and the ORS response category (Table II.1.A, Fig. II.2.A). The surveillance plan should also take into account the extent of surgery and whether RAI therapy was performed. TSH-unstimulated serum Tg and TgAb measurements and neck ultrasound are the primary surveillance tools. In patients who received RAI therapy, TSH-stimulated serum Tg testing may be performed when clinically indicated.

When serum Tg or TgAb levels show a persistent rising trend during follow-up, imaging studies, including CT, diagnostic RAI WBS, and 18F-FDG PET/CT, may be considered. Even in the absence of changes in serum Tg or TgAb levels, additional structural or functional imaging may be needed when recurrence or metastasis is clinically suspected.

To confirm that the target serum TSH level is being maintained and to determine the appropriate LT4 dose, serum TSH, free T4, and, when indicated, free T3 should be measured periodically together with serum Tg. Patients receiving LT4 should be followed at least annually to maintain an appropriate LT4 dose.

#### (1) Excellent response

II.4.A. In low- and intermediate-risk patients who demonstrate an excellent response, TSH-unstimulated serum Tg and TgAb levels are measured every 1–2 years. Recommendation level 3

II.4.B. TSH-stimulated serum Tg and TgAb measurements are not recommended during follow-up in low- and intermediate-risk patients who demonstrate an excellent response. Recommendation level 2

II.4.C. In high-risk patients who demonstrate an excellent response, TSH-unstimulated serum Tg and TgAb levels may be measured every 6–12 months. Recommendation level 3

II.4.D. In high-risk patients who demonstrate an excellent response, TSH-unstimulated serum Tg measurement may be used instead of TSH-stimulated serum Tg measurement. Recommendation level 2

[Clinical considerations]

1) After lobectomy, serum Tg and TgAb have limited clinical utility; therefore, routine measurement is generally not recommended, although it may be considered when clinically indicated.

2) The interval for serum Tg measurement during long-term follow-up may be adjusted according to the clinical findings and duration of follow-up.

3) Periodic neck ultrasound is performed as part of follow-up surveillance.

4) Additional imaging studies may be considered when clinically indicated, such as when serum Tg or TgAb levels increase or when recurrence or metastasis is clinically suspected.

In initially low- to intermediate-risk patients with DTC who demonstrate an excellent response on ORS, follow-up surveillance consists of clinical evaluation and serum Tg/TgAb every 1–2 years, with neck ultrasound every 1–3 years as clinically needed. Repeat neck ultrasound may be considered if clinically indicated based on serum Tg and TgAb levels. A recent study suggested that patients who have maintained an excellent response for 5–8 years after completion of initial treatment can be followed every 1–2 years with clinical evaluation and TSH-unstimulated serum Tg and TgAb measurement, without routine neck ultrasound. That study reported a high negative predictive value of 98.2% for cervical LN recurrence at 72 months in low- to intermediate-risk patients with papillary thyroid cancer who showed an excellent response at the 1-year post-treatment assessment. On this basis, the authors proposed follow-up with TSH-unstimulated Tg and TgAb measurements alone, without periodic neck ultrasound. However, neck ultrasound is more sensitive than serum Tg measurement for detecting locoregional LN recurrence, and 45%–60.5% of patients with FNA-confirmed cervical LN metastases had unstimulated serum Tg levels <1.0 ng/mL. Therefore, this guideline recommends TSH-unstimulated serum Tg and TgAb measurement every 1–2 years in initially low- to intermediate-risk patients with an excellent response. In patients who underwent lobectomy, serum Tg and TgAb measurements may be performed only when clinically indicated. Neck ultrasound may be performed every 1–3 years according to clinical need, and the surveillance interval may be extended if disease status remains stable for approximately 5 years.

Careful follow-up remains necessary in initially high-risk patients, even when they show an excellent response. Serum Tg and TgAb measurements and neck ultrasound may be performed every 6–12 months. If disease status remains stable for 5–10 years, the surveillance interval for serum Tg measurement and neck ultrasound may be extended. However, even in initially high-risk patients classified as having an excellent response, clinicians should remain attentive to disease that may not be detected by neck ultrasound or serum Tg measurement.

Additional structural or functional imaging may be warranted when serum Tg or TgAb levels increase during follow-up or when recurrent or metastatic disease is clinically suspected.

#### (2) Biochemical incomplete response or indeterminate response

II.4.E. In low-risk patients who demonstrate a biochemical incomplete response or an indeterminate response, serum Tg and TgAb levels are measured every 1–2 years. Recommendation level 3

II.4.F. In intermediate-risk patients who demonstrate a biochemical incomplete response or an indeterminate response, serum Tg and TgAb levels are measured every 6–12 months. Recommendation level 3

II.4.G. In high-risk patients who demonstrate a biochemical incomplete response or an indeterminate response, serum Tg and TgAb levels are measured every 6–12 months. Recommendation level 3

II.4.H. In low- or intermediate-risk patients who have undergone total thyroidectomy with RAI remnant ablation and demonstrate a biochemical incomplete response, TSH-stimulated serum Tg measurement, with or without RAI WBS, may be performed. Recommendation level 2

II.4.I. In patients who have undergone total thyroidectomy with RAI remnant ablation and demonstrate a biochemical incomplete response, TSH-stimulated serum Tg and TgAb levels may be measured to reassess recurrence risk when TSH-unstimulated serum Tg levels decrease. Recommendation level 3

[Clinical considerations]

- The interval for serum Tg measurement during long-term follow-up may be adjusted according to clinical findings and follow-up duration.

- Periodic neck ultrasound is performed as part of follow-up surveillance.

- Additional imaging studies may be considered when clinically indicated, such as when serum Tg or TgAb levels increase or when recurrence or metastasis is clinically suspected.

For patients with an indeterminate response, the recommended target TSH level is the same as that for patients with an excellent response. However, until reclassification to another ORS response category is achieved through subsequent ORS assessments, serum Tg and TgAb measurements and appropriate imaging studies for nonspecific lesions should be performed at the same interval recommended for patients with a biochemical incomplete response. In initially low-risk patients who demonstrate a biochemical incomplete response or an indeterminate response, follow-up surveillance consists of clinical evaluation and serum Tg and TgAb measurements every 1–2 years. Neck ultrasound may be performed according to the serum Tg and TgAb results. In initially intermediate- or high-risk patients who demonstrate a biochemical incomplete response or an indeterminate response, serum Tg and TgAb measurements and neck ultrasound are performed every 6–12 months. If serum Tg or TgAb levels increase during follow-up, imaging studies, including CT, diagnostic RAI WBS, and 18F-FDG PET/CT, may be considered. Once an excellent response has been achieved on ORS, the intensity and frequency of follow-up surveillance may be reduced, and the target TSH level may be de-escalated. These changes are particularly relevant in initially intermediate-and high-risk patients. This guideline recommends considering extension of follow-up surveillance intervals after 5 years in initially low- to intermediate-risk patients and after 5–10 years in initially high-risk patients when an excellent response has been achieved or serum Tg and TgAb levels remain stable.

Nevertheless, a rising trend in unstimulated Tg levels is associated with an increased risk of recurrence. In a study of 145 patients with DTC who had an indeterminate response on ORS, 9% were reclassified as having a biochemical or structural incomplete response during a median follow-up of 68 months. These patients demonstrated a continuous increase in unstimulated serum Tg levels, with a mean increase of 27.1% at each follow-up visit. Therefore, careful follow-up is warranted when serum Tg or TgAb levels increase. In patients with a biochemical incomplete response, the likelihood of detecting structurally identifiable locoregional disease or distant metastases increases as serum Tg or TgAb levels increase during follow-up. In such cases, additional imaging studies, including CT, 18F-FDG PET/CT, or diagnostic RAI WBS, should be performed promptly.

Although there is no evidence that RAI therapy improves survival in patients with elevated serum Tg levels and negative RAI WBS findings, increased progression-free survival has been reported in patients with suspected micrometastatic disease. Therefore, empirical RAI therapy may be considered when no structurally identifiable lesions are present but micrometastatic disease is suspected (see Chapter III.5).

#### (3) Structural incomplete response

II.4.J. In patients who demonstrate a structural incomplete response, TSH-unstimulated serum Tg and TgAb levels are measured every 3–6 months. Recommendation level 3

[Clinical considerations]

- Imaging studies may be performed as clinically indicated according to the patient’s disease status.

Regardless of initial recurrence risk, serum Tg and TgAb are measured every 3–6 months in patients with a structural incomplete response, and imaging of known structural lesions should be performed every 3–12 months. When no recurrent lesion is confirmed in the neck, neck ultrasound may be considered at 6–24-month intervals. Additional treatments, including surgery, RAI therapy, external beam radiation therapy, and targeted therapy, may be performed; alternatively, selected patients may be managed with continued follow-up. Management should be individualized based on multiple factors, including lesion size, lesion location, growth rate, RAI uptake, and response to previous treatment.

## CONCLUSIONS

Because DTC generally has an excellent prognosis, facilitating patients’ return to their previous health status after successful initial treatment is an important goal of long-term management. Following standard initial treatment, treatment response and recurrence risk should be reassessed continuously using ORS, with patients reclassified as clinically appropriate. These reassessments provide the basis for readjusting target serum TSH levels and individualized follow-up strategies according to each patient’s clinical status. Although evidence remains limited in some areas, this guideline is intended to provide a practical framework for clinical decision-making and to support high-quality, individualized patient care.
