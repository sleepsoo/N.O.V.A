# Expert Consensus Study on the Redesign of the Sexually Transmitted Infection Surveillance System in Korea

## Abstract

### Background

The management of sexually transmitted infections (STIs) at the national level is necessary for the health and well-being of individuals in society. The purpose of this study was to propose a redesign of the STI surveillance system by collecting opinions from experts.

### Methods

After the major STIs were reviewed, 1) gonorrhea, 2) chancroid, 3) Mycoplasma genitalium infection, and 4) trichomoniasis were selected as diseases requiring a redesign of the surveillance system through the Delphi method, a technique for solving problems by collecting opinions from experts. The expert panel was selected from experts on STIs (n = 17) and included members of the Korean Association of Urogenital Tract Infection and Inflammation, the Korean Society of Infectious Diseases, and the 3rd advisory committee on STIs.

### Results

The experts agreed upon the exclusion of surveillance for chancroid on the basis of the low incidence, with an agreement rate of 83%. The experts agreed upon the inclusion of M. genitalium in the surveillance system, with a high agreement rate of 88%. However, there was disagreement among the experts on whether to include trichomoniasis in the surveillance system and whether to switch to a mandatory surveillance system for gonorrhea.

### Conclusion

Although a consensus was not reached in some of the surveys in this study, it involved the collection of various expert opinions. Therefore, policies should be promoted with a prioritized focus on items with high agreement, but items for which no consensus was reached need to be reviewed in greater depth through additional research and investigation.

## INTRODUCTION

Sexually transmitted infections (STIs) are very common infectious diseases worldwide that are transmitted between sexual partners through various forms of sexual contact. STIs are among the important reasons for visiting medical institutions for diagnosis and treatment.

STIs can cause potentially serious complications if not treated appropriately, resulting in significant economic losses and a significant burden on the medical system. Gonorrhea and chlamydia are major causes of pelvic inflammatory disease and infertility in women. In addition, human papillomavirus (HPV) infection can cause cervical and other cancers. Genital herpes, gonorrhea, and syphilis may also increase the risk of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection. Vertically transmitted STIs can cause stillbirth, neonatal death, low birth weight, and premature birth.

Therefore, the management of STIs at the national level is necessary for the health and well-being of individuals in society. Currently, in Korea, STIs, including genital herpes, chancroid, gonorrhea, chlamydia, condyloma acuminatum, and HPV infection, are classified as grade 4 infectious diseases subject to sentinel surveillance according to the Infectious Disease Prevention Act. Syphilis was converted to a grade 3 infectious disease that is subject to mandatory surveillance in Korea on January 1, 2024. For the sentinel surveillance system, agencies must report the occurrence of STIs within 7 days of identification. However, the mandatory surveillance system mandates that in the event of an outbreak of a grade 1 to grade 3 infectious disease, the responsible party must report it to the local public health center immediately or within 24 hours.

The mandatory surveillance system has the advantages of being able to conduct epidemiological investigations for each infectious disease and thoroughly analyze the epidemic spread. Therefore, in the case of severe infectious diseases or infectious diseases with a rapidly increasing incidence, it may be necessary to switch from the current sentinel surveillance system to a mandatory surveillance system. Moreover, there is a need to reexamine the designation of infectious diseases with a low incidence as statutory notifiable diseases.

The purpose of this study was to propose a redesign of the STI surveillance system through the Delphi method, a technique for solving problems by collecting opinions from experts.

## METHODS

### Disease selection

#### Chancroid

Chancroid is an STI caused by infection with Haemophilus ducreyi and is characterized by painful ulcers on the genitals. However, chancroid may disappear naturally, rarely affects parts of the body outside the genitals, and does not cause systemic disease. Chancroid is treated with antibiotics such as azithromycin, ciprofloxacin, and erythromycin. Ulcers disappear approximately one week after treatment, and individuals are considered to no longer be infectious when the symptoms disappear. For this reason, the diagnosis of chancroid at medical institutions or clinics is rare. According to data from the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA), only 1.3 cases of chancroid have been reported each year (27 total cases between 2001 and 2021). In the United States, fewer than 10 cases of chancroid are reported each year, and in Japan, chancroid is not designated a statutory notifiable disease. Therefore, there is a need to reexamine the designation of chancroid, which has a low incidence, as a statutory notifiable disease.

#### Mycoplasma genitalium

Recently, M. genitalium has emerged as an important STI worldwide, and increased resistance of M. genitalium to azithromycin has continuously been reported. Increased resistance to macrolides is highly correlated with M. genitalium treatment failure. Owing to these issues of antibiotic resistance, the recommended treatment guidelines for M. genitalium were changed in the 3rd edition of the STI treatment guidelines revised in 2023. Therefore, it is important to conduct epidemiological investigations of M. genitalium infection and thoroughly analyze its epidemic spread.

#### Trichomoniasis

Trichomoniasis is one of the most common sexually transmitted diseases, with an estimated 150 million cases occurring each year. Considering asymptomatic patients, the number of infections is likely to be much greater. In contrast, no epidemiological investigations of trichomoniasis have been conducted in Korea. However, trichomoniasis can cause serious complications in pregnant women, including premature birth and low birth weight, and contributes to the spread of HIV. In addition, metronidazole, which is still effective in treating trichomoniasis, requires pharmacological alternatives due to increasing resistance. Therefore, the designation of trichomoniasis as a statutory notifiable disease should be considered.

#### Gonorrhea

Owing to increasing resistance to ceftriaxone, azithromycin was added as a recommended treatment for gonorrhea in the 2nd edition of the STI treatment guidelines of Korea. However, resistance to azithromycin is also increasing worldwide. In addition, high-dose ceftriaxone is more effective against isolates with increased minimum inhibitory concentrations, although low-dose ceftriaxone may be appropriate for treating most cases of gonorrhea. Owing to the issue of antibiotic resistance, the recommended treatment guidelines for gonorrhea were changed in the 3rd edition of the STI treatment guidelines revised in 2023. Therefore, it may be necessary to switch from the current sentinel surveillance system to a mandatory surveillance system in the future. ### Delphi survey

The Delphi method was developed to collect opinions from experts on urgent national defense and social issues. This method utilizes written or web-based research to overcome the monopoly and opposition difficulties (bandwagon) of face-to-face discussions and promote consensus through repetition and feedback. The Delphi method emphasizes anonymity to eliminate interaction within the group, which is a disadvantage of the typical face-to-face method, guarantees the opportunity to modify responses (controlled feedback), and involves repeated rounds (iteration). It has the methodological feature of statistically aggregating the opinions of the entire group and presenting the distributional characteristics of the opinions (statistical group response).

In this study, the final panel number was selected as 19 to minimize errors in survey results while considering the risk of lower recovery rates during repeated surveys. The expert panel was selected from experts on STIs and included members of the Korean Association of Urogenital Tract Infection and Inflammation, the Korean Society of Infectious Diseases, and the 3rd Korean advisory committee on STIs. The Korean advisory committee on STIs comprises highly specialized individuals with more than five years of practical experience in each field and is appointed by the commissioner of the KDCA upon recommendations from relevant academic societies.

The expert panel selected included at least one urologist, one infectious disease specialist, one obstetrician/gynecologist, one preventive medicine specialist, one dermatologist, and one pediatrician. A participation email was sent to 19 experts, among whom 17 responded, and a Delphi survey was conducted with 17 experts in STIs.

The collected data were used to calculate the agreement rate, degree of convergence, and degree of consensus for the Delphi questionnaire. If the agreement rate was 80.0% or higher, the degree of convergence was 0.5 or lower, and the degree of consensus was 0.75 or higher, the validity was determined to be secured (Degree of Convergence = [(Q3 – Q1)/2] and Degree of Consensus = 1 – [(Q3 – Q1)/Mdn], respectively; Q1: 25th percentile, Q3: 75th percentile, Mdn: median). The experts respond to the items by using a five-point Likert scale as follows: 1) strongly disagree, 2) disagree, 3) neutral, 4) agree, and 5) strongly agree. The data are presented as counts and percentages.

### Questionnaire analysis

The experts were asked the following questions in the Delphi survey:

- Q1. Do you agree that chancroid should be excluded from sentinel surveillance?
- Q2. Do you agree that M. genitalium should be included in the sentinel surveillance system?
- Q3. Do you agree that trichomoniasis should be included in the sentinel surveillance system?
- Q4. Do you agree that surveillance for gonorrhea should be switched to mandatory surveillance?

This survey used the Welphi system (www.welphi.com), which is an online survey system that implements the Delphi technique through a series of rounds. In addition to answering the questions posed, participants can anonymously share their opinions with other participants and reach a consensus. The number of rounds in a Delphi survey is determined by the stability of the responses. Because the changes in the responses are the greatest in the initial 1–2 rounds and there are little changes in the responses beyond the 5th round, 2–3 surveys are often conducted. In this study, the Delphi survey was terminated at the second round because it was clearly confirmed which items had a consensus and answers to which items had not changed.

### Ethics statement

This study was performed in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from each subject prior to their participation in this study. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of The Catholic University of Korea (Approval number: VC24QNSI0216).

## RESULTS

### Results of the first round

A total of 17 experts responded to the first survey. Sixty-five percent of the respondents agreed with the exclusion of chancroid from the surveillance system, 18% did not agree, and the remaining 18% responded neutrally. In terms of whether to include M. genitalium in the sentinel surveillance system, the agreement rate was high at 88%, and the disagreement rate was relatively low at 12%. On the other hand, with respect to the inclusion of trichomoniasis in the surveillance system, the agreement rate was only 47%, with 29% of the experts disagreeing and 24% responding neutrally, indicating a large difference in opinion among the respondents. The agreement rate for switching gonorrhea surveillance to mandatory surveillance was 53%, with only 29% of the experts disagreeing and 12% responding neutrally, which was relatively low (Table 2, Fig. 1).

### Results of the second round

In the first round, three questions with differing opinions among the experts were identified. Accordingly, in the second round, only the questions on which opinions were not established (Q1, Q3, and Q4) were selected, and the survey for these questions was conducted again with the same 17 experts.

The experts agreed upon the exclusion of chancroid for surveillance, with an agreement rate of 83% in the second round. However, the experts still disagreed on whether to include trichomoniasis in the surveillance system and whether to switch to a mandatory surveillance system for gonorrhea (agreement rate of 53%). Overall, the exclusion of chancroid from the surveillance system received high support, whereas questions related to trichomoniasis and gonorrhea showed relatively low agreement rates (Table 3, Fig. 2).

## DISCUSSION

In this study, various opinions from experts on STIs were collected through the Delphi method to propose a redesign of the STI surveillance system. The authors provided the KDCA with the results of the Delphi survey along with the following expert opinions.

The experts who participated in the survey reached a high agreement rate of 83% with respect to the exclusion of chancroid from the sentinel surveillance system. Experts who expressed positive opinions noted that the incidence of chancroid is very low, making the surveillance system less efficient. They rated chancroid as having low public health significance because it does not cause serious systemic complications. In addition, they mentioned that the diagnosis of chancroid is difficult because it is not included in the multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR) test commonly used in clinics in Korea.

Some experts who expressed negative opinions argued that although the low incidence rate of chancroid might reduce the effectiveness of the surveillance system, surveillance was still necessary because chancroid is a contagious disease.

Most experts were in favor of including M. genitalium in surveillance systems (agreement rate of 88%), for the following reasons: 1) as the number of M. genitalium infections has recently increased and resistance to macrolides has emerged, monitoring domestic outbreaks and introducing macrolide resistance testing for M. genitalium infection are essential; 2) M. genitalium infection requires management, as it can lead to serious complications such as preterm labor and pelvic inflammatory disease; and 3) as treatment failure for M. genitalium infection has increased worldwide, M. genitalium infection has become an international concern.

Some experts emphasized that M. genitalium infection was relatively low in severity and had a lower incidence than other major infectious diseases did, making it difficult to include it as a priority in the surveillance system.

In terms of the inclusion of trichomoniasis in the surveillance system, the agreement rate was 53%, a slight increase compared with the first round (agreement rate of 47%), but the experts’ opinions still did not clearly converge. This indicates that there is disagreement among experts about the importance of trichomoniasis from a public health perspective. Therefore, it is necessary to collect additional epidemiological data to make a final decision on whether to include trichomoniasis in the surveillance system. Experts in favor of including trichomoniasis in the surveillance system provided the following reasons: 1) trichomoniasis has distinct symptoms, and accurate diagnosis is possible using multiplex PCR, and 2) although the incidence rate is decreasing, many cases are still being reported, so a surveillance system for appropriate management is necessary.

Experts who expressed negative opinions (33%) emphasized that the decreasing incidence of trichomoniasis makes it difficult to include it as a priority in the surveillance system. There was also an opinion that there is no need for surveillance because trichomoniasis can be easily diagnosed and treated clinically. In the end, a more in-depth discussion is needed to make a final decision on whether to include trichomoniasis in the surveillance system.

The results of the survey on switching from sentinel surveillance to mandatory surveillance for gonorrhea showed a low agreement rate in both the first and second rounds, with a failure to reach a clear consensus. This shows that there is a large difference in opinions among experts regarding mandatory surveillance for gonorrhea and suggests that the collection of additional evidence is necessary to make policy decisions. The experts who agreed upon the change to mandatory surveillance for gonorrhea provided the following reasons: 1) as the resistance of gonorrhea to antibiotics is a serious problem, it is necessary to strengthen management through a comprehensive surveillance system, and 2) most regions in Korea have sufficient laboratory capacity for the diagnosis of gonorrhea, so a transition to mandatory surveillance would be possible.

The experts rated that gonorrhea was being managed well through the current sentinel surveillance system. They raised concerns that the usefulness of switching to a mandatory surveillance system may be limited. In addition, the experts stated that there is a lack of clear evidence indicating that the risk of public health due to gonorrhea has increased. Therefore, it is desirable to maintain the existing sentinel surveillance system and increase its efficiency.

The results of this study were submitted to the KDCA to be used as a reference for policy decisions. Although a consensus was not reached in some of the surveys in this study, this study is expected to be a useful basis for establishing future policies because it involved the collection of various expert opinions and provides a reference for future policy decision-making processes.

Therefore, the authors suggested that the KDCA gives priority to items with high agreement rates in policies to redesign the Korean STI surveillance system. However, items for which no consensus was reached need to be reviewed in greater depth through additional research and investigation. In particular, for trichomoniasis, additional data on the national epidemiological situation and disease burden are needed before considering whether to include trichomoniasis in the surveillance system. In addition, a cost-effectiveness study on the transition to the mandatory surveillance system is needed for gonorrhea.
