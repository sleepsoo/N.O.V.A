# Low-Dose Chest CT Lung Cancer Screening for School Cafeteria Workers: A Position Paper from the Korean Society of Thoracic Radiology

## Abstract

The Korean Society of Thoracic Radiology (KSTR) conducted an assessment on the necessity of low-dose chest CT (LDCT) lung cancer screening for Korean school cafeteria workers. This assessment was prompted by growing concerns about the potential risk of lung cancer due to exposure to carcinogenic cooking fumes. To reach a consensus on various aspects of LDCT screening for Korean school cafeteria workers, thoracic radiologists were involved in a survey that utilized the Delphi method. Key issues addressed in the survey included the need for tailored environmental assessments for this specific group, guidelines for interpreting LDCT results, and the evaluation of cooking occupations as a potential risk factor for lung cancer. The survey results revealed a lack of evidence supporting the benefits of LDCT lung cancer screening for Korean school cafeteria workers. Furthermore, there was no consensus reached on the criteria for determining which Korean school cafeteria workers should undergo LDCT screening, guidelines for interpreting the results, or the level of evidence supporting cooking tasks as a risk factor for lung cancer. In this position paper, the KSTR critically examines these aspects and emphasizes the importance of an evidence-based approach to lung cancer screening by considering the unique occupational and environmental context of Korean school cafeteria workers.

## INTRODUCTION

Lung cancer remains a significant cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, posing a major challenge to public health systems. While it is commonly associated with smokers, lung cancer also affects non-smokers who are exposed to various environmental and occupational hazards. According to 2020 data from Global Cancer Incidence, Mortality, and Prevalence (GLOBOCAN), Asia accounted for 60% of new lung cancer cases globally, with approximately 1315136 reported cases. In addition, lung cancer-related deaths in Asia reached around 1112517, representing 62% of lung cancer-related mortalities worldwide. Notably, the incidence of lung cancer in non-smoking women is higher in Asia than in Western countries.

In 2023, the Taiwan Lung Cancer Screening in Never Smoker Trial (TALENT) employed low-dose chest CT (LDCT) to screen 12011 individuals who have never smoked for lung cancer. Initial LDCT scans in the study revealed a lung cancer detection rate of 2.6% among non-smokers, higher than the rates observed in smoking populations, which were 1.1% in the National Lung Screening Trial (NLST) and 0.9% in the Nederlands-Leuvens Longkanker Screening Onderzoek (NELSON) study. Among the patients included in this study, 36.6% had a risk factor with a cooking index equal to or greater than 110, and 1.8% had a risk factor associated with cooking without a ventilator. Notably, cooking oil fumes and chemicals are particularly significant risk factors for the development of lung cancer. Therefore, Korean school cafeteria workers may also be part of this unique population. In 2022, the Ministry of Education in the Republic of Korea conducted LDCT lung cancer screenings for 24065 school cafeteria workers who were either over 55 years old or had been working in school cafeterias for more than 10 years. Preliminary findings revealed that among these individuals, 139 (0.58%) had pulmonary nodules suspicious or very suspicious for lung cancer, and 31 (0.13%) were diagnosed with lung cancer. Recognizing this, the Korean Society of Thoracic Radiology (KSTR) conducted a comprehensive review to assess the potential role of LDCT lung cancer screening for this population.

The purpose of this position paper is to evaluate the current evidence and provide specific guidelines for lung cancer screening tailored to the needs and risks of school cafeteria workers in the Republic of Korea. By considering international guidelines, national statistics, and emerging research, we aim to present a balanced perspective on the benefits and risks of LDCT lung cancer screening for Korean school cafeteria workers. Additionally, as a group of experts on this issue, we aim to publish recommendations based on our consensus regarding the necessity of LDCT lung cancer screenings for this specific population. In this introduction, we will present the problem statement and the rationale behind prioritizing school cafeteria workers for lung cancer screening. Furthermore, this manuscript will outline the position of the KSTR regarding the important issue of lung cancer screening for individuals employed as school cafeteria workers.

## DEVELOPMENT OF A CONSENSUS OPINION

This study, supported by the KSTR, was conducted with the participation of 15 thoracic radiologists. The corresponding author (G.Y.J.) invited the radiologists to participate, considering an equitable distribution across age, research and clinical experience within relevant fields, and region. The survey was conducted using a three-round Delphi method. In the first round, panelists were asked to voice their opinions regarding LDCT lung cancer screening for Korean domestic school cafeteria workers. They were given the opportunity to freely describe their thoughts. After completing the first survey, panelists received a second survey along with the results of the initial survey. In the second round, panel members were asked to vote on specific issues using a scale from 1 (strongest disagreement) to 9 (strongest agreement). Consensus was considered achieved when at least 70% of participants rated the issue as 7 or higher. Ratings of 1 to 3 indicated a dissenting view, while ratings of 7 to 9 indicated an assenting view. Panelists were able to review the responses of others before completing the second questionnaire. By comparing their own opinions with those of others, they reconsidered and potentially revised their initial opinions. Discrepancies were observed in the outcomes of a specific item (key question 4) in the second round. As a result, the panel members were queried again in a third round. The first, second, and third rounds of the survey were conducted with 15, 10, and 10 thoracic radiologists, respectively. Notably, of the 15 panel members who participated in the first round, 5 did not respond in the second and third rounds. Therefore, the surveys in rounds 2 and 3 were conducted with the remaining 10 panel members (Table 1). ## CONSENSUS STATEMENT

### NECESSITY OF A LUNG CANCER SCREENING PROGRAM FOR KOREAN DOMESTIC SCHOOL CAFETERIA WORKERS

Before determining the necessity of a lung cancer screening program for Korean domestic school cafeteria workers, it is essential to conduct an environmental assessment and situation analysis tailored to Korea’s national circumstances in order to provide evidence-based justification. Environmental assessment refers to evaluating the cooking facilities where cafeteria workers are employed, while situational analysis involves comparing lung cancer screening outcomes with similar groups and smokers.

### EVIDENCE FOR THE NECESSITY OF LUNG CANCER SCREENING FOR KOREAN DOMESTIC SCHOOL CAFETERIA WORKERS

To implement lung cancer screening for Korean domestic school cafeteria workers, there must be evidence that such screening can reduce mortality rates.

### GUIDELINES FOR THE INTERPRETATION OF LDCT LUNG CANCER SCREENING RESULTS FROM KOREAN DOMESTIC SCHOOL CAFETERIA WORKERS

Education for lung cancer screening for Korean domestic school cafeteria workers should align closely with the protocols of the national lung cancer screening program. Currently, there is no evidence supporting the use of lung-reporting and data systems (lung-RADS) in lung cancer screening for Korean domestic school cafeteria cooks.

### EVIDENCE LEVEL FOR COOKING OCCUPATION AS A RISK FACTOR FOR LUNG CANCER

Evidence is currently limited regarding cooking occupations as a risk factor for lung cancer.

### BENEFITS AND HARMS OF IMPLEMENTING LDCT LUNG CANCER SCREENING FOR KOREAN DOMESTIC SCHOOL CAFETERIA WORKERS

The benefits of LDCT lung cancer screening for Korean domestic school cafeteria workers include early diagnosis of lung cancer and the acquisition of research data. However, implementing LDCT lung cancer screening may lead to unnecessary screening and repeated radiation exposure for these workers, which can be considered potentially harmful.

### NEED FOR COLLABORATION WITH OTHER EXPERT GROUPS REGARDING LDCT LUNG CANCER SCREENING FOR KOREAN DOMESTIC SCHOOL CAFETERIA WORKERS

To screen for lung cancer in Korean domestic school cafeteria workers, it is necessary to consult with departments of occupational and environmental medicine, preventive medicine, pulmonology, radiology, related academic societies, and the Korean Association for Lung Cancer.

### MANAGEMENT SYSTEM FOR LOW-DOSE CT LUNG CANCER SCREENING TARGETING KOREAN DOMESTIC SCHOOL CAFETERIA WORKERS

The management system for LDCT lung cancer screening targeting Korean domestic school cafeteria workers should ideally be determined through collaboration with institutions related to lung cancer, such as the National Cancer Center, Korean Society of Radiology, Korean Association of Lung Cancer, or occupational disease-related organizations.

## DISCUSSION

This position paper from the KSTR contributes significantly to the ongoing discourse on lung cancer screening for school cafeteria workers. Deliberations and consensus statements highlight several critical aspects worthy of discussion (Table 1, Fig. 1).

### NECESSITY OF LDCT LUNG CANCER SCREENING FOR KOREAN DOMESTIC SCHOOL CAFETERIA WORKERS

The necessity of LDCT lung cancer screening for Korean domestic school cafeteria workers requires a comprehensive analysis, particularly considering their unique occupational exposure. The implementation of LDCT screening in this group is based on the growing understanding of occupational hazards associated with cooking fumes and their potential as carcinogens. Multiple studies have highlighted the presence of harmful substances in cooking fumes, including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, heterocyclic amines, and acrolein, which are known carcinogens. The chronic exposure of school cafeteria workers to these substances increases their risk of developing lung cancer compared to the general population.

Due to the occupational risk described above, targeted lung cancer screening strategies may be necessary, with LDCT being a standout choice due to its sensitivity and specificity in detecting early-stage lung cancer. Nevertheless, based on the current evidence, it does not seem justified to implement lung cancer screening protocols for school cafeteria workers in the Republic of Korea. The lung cancer detection rate among Korean domestic school cafeteria workers who underwent LDCT lung cancer screenings, as reported by the Ministry of Education, is 0.13%, which is lower than the 2.6% diagnostic rate observed in the TALENT study conducted in Taiwan, which specifically targeted never-smoker females. This rate is also lower than the rates reported for smokers in the NLST (1.1%) and the NELSON study (0.9%). In addition, Korean school cafeterias were found to have lower levels of cooking fume concentrations compared to Chinese school cafeterias. Additionally, prolonged use of fume extractors showed up to a 50% reduction in lung cancer risk, which is an aspect that has not been adequately considered.

It is important to note that the Ministry of Education in the Republic of Korea’s LDCT lung cancer screening for cafeteria workers does not accurately measure exposure to cooking fumes. Instead, it solely relies on an assessment of cooking history. However, research has shown a distinct link between the amount of exposure to cooking fumes and the development of lung cancer. A focused analysis on Chinese non-smoking women revealed that the method of cooking (such as deep-frying, frying, or stir-frying) has a significant association with lung cancer incidence, particularly when exposure has exceeded 101 dish-years based on multivariable models. Overall, while Korean domestic school cafeteria workers undergo exclusive screening for lung cancer using LDCT, current data do not support the hypothesis that these employees, including cooks, are exposed to higher levels of cooking fumes compared to their counterparts in standard restaurant settings or individuals who cook at home.

Moving to the broader context of lung cancer screening, it is crucial to consider the overarching goals of these health initiatives. The primary objective of screening for lung cancer is to reduce mortality rates associated with the disease. Therefore, when considering lung cancer screening for Korean domestic school cafeteria workers, it is essential to align this specific initiative with the broader goal. Given the potential exposure risks, lung cancer screening for these workers should not only address immediate occupational hazards but also contribute to the overall objective of reducing mortality. This approach necessitates further research to optimize screening protocols and tailor them to the specific needs and risks of this population.

### GUIDELINES FOR INTERPRETATION OF LDCT LUNG CANCER SCREENING FOR KOREAN DOMESTIC SCHOOL CAFETERIA WORKERS

If lung cancer screening for Korean domestic school cafeteria workers is justified, the development of guidelines for interpreting LDCT lung cancer screenings is crucial for enhancing the effectiveness of lung cancer detection and management in this occupational group. Due to their unique exposure to potential carcinogens in cooking fumes, this subgroup requires tailored guidelines that address both general principles of LDCT lung cancer screening and the specific risks associated with their occupation. While widely used nodule guidelines such as the 2017 Fleischner Society guideline and Lung-RADS exist, there is still no evidence regarding which nodule guideline is most suitable for this specific group.

Despite the absence of specific guidelines for interpretation, it is still necessary to provide appropriate education to radiologists and physicians involved in lung cancer screening. In the Republic of Korea, only institutions with radiologists trained in lung cancer screening interpretation and consulting physicians who can provide face-to-face consultations after completing relevant education are designated as national lung cancer screening facilities. This approach is also supported by the Delphi survey conducted in this study, which reached a consensus that lung cancer screening education for school cafeteria workers should be managed through this training approach.

### CURRENT EVIDENCE ON COOKING TASKS AS A RISK FACTOR FOR LUNG CANCER OCCURRENCE

The relationship between cooking tasks and the risk of lung cancer has recently become a focal point of research. This interest stems from the growing awareness that occupational exposure to cooking fumes may significantly contribute to the occurrence of lung cancer. A comprehensive meta-analysis has found a discernible association between exposure to cooking oil fumes and an elevated risk of developing lung cancer in non-smoking Chinese women, with a risk ratio of 1.74 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.57–1.94). This risk ratio increases to 2.11 (95% CI: 1.54–2.89) in kitchens lacking adequate ventilation. However, the quality of evidence in the current literature is not robust. Previous research primarily consists of observational studies, many of which use retrospective methodologies. These approaches are susceptible to biases, particularly recall bias, which can significantly influence study results. Additionally, these studies have notable constraints, including small sample sizes, a lack of consideration for the influence of smoking, and a focus primarily on Chinese populations.

In 2023, Wang et al. showed that exposure to both tobacco smoke and cooking fumes are risk factors for non-adenocarcinoma of the lungs. However, the impact of cooking fumes on this risk appears to be less significant when compared to the effects of smoking. Due to these factors, the KSTR committee has not reached a consensus on the reliability of the evidence level of various reports stating that cooking tasks are a risk factor for the development of lung cancer. Controversial results have also emerged regarding the benefits of lung cancer screening for Korean domestic school cafeteria workers. While the level of evidence supporting cooking tasks as a risk factor for lung cancer development is low, there is a consensus that LDCT lung cancer screening is beneficial. However, there has been no consensus on whether there is evidence to suggest that cooking tasks are a risk factor for lung cancer or whether LDCT lung cancer screening would be beneficial for Korean domestic school cafeteria workers.

The agreement regarding the potential benefits of LDCT lung cancer screening for Korean domestic school cafeteria cooks might have been affected by the preliminary findings of the recent TALENT study, which showed a high lung cancer detection rate of 2.6%. The TALENT study was conducted from February 2015 to July 2019 and enrolled a cohort of 12011 individuals who had never smoked. Cooking index values and cooking without ventilation as a hazard were used as inclusion criteria. However, most panel members might not have been aware of recently published data that suggests a cooking index ≥110 was significantly associated with a lower risk of lung cancer. Among the study’s results, cooking without ventilation did not show a significant association with lung cancer detection. Notably, the TALENT study’s primary focus was lung cancer detection rate; thus, it did not evaluate mortality reduction directly linked to lung cancer. This gap in the study’s scope is especially pertinent given concerns regarding overdiagnosis in lung cancer screening.

### BENEFITS AND HARMS OF LDCT LUNG CANCER SCREENING FOR KOREAN DOMESTIC SCHOOL CAFETERIA WORKERS

The primary advantage of LDCT lung cancer screening lies in its ability to detect lung cancer at earlier stages when it is more treatable. Given that school cafeteria workers are exposed to cooking fumes, which could increase the risk of lung cancer, early screening can be a vital preventive measure. However, it is important to weigh the benefits of LDCT against potential harms in order to take an informed and balanced approach. While LDCT uses lower radiation doses than traditional CT scans, the frequency of these screenings over a long period of time may contribute to a higher overall radiation exposure. This increased exposure could potentially raise the risk of various cancers. The financial implications of widespread LDCT lung cancer screening must also be considered. It is crucial to assess whether the benefits of screening outweigh the costs, especially in terms of resource allocation in the healthcare system. Additionally, one of the major concerns about LDCT lung cancer screening is the risk of overdiagnosis, which could lead to unnecessary treatments for cancers that may not progress or significantly impact an individual’s life expectancy.

### NEED FOR COLLABORATION WITH OTHER EXPERT GROUPS IN LDCT LUNG CANCER SCREENING FOR KOREAN DOMESTIC SCHOOL CAFETERIA WORKERS

A collaborative, interdisciplinary approach involving various expert groups is essential for the effective implementation of LDCT lung cancer screening programs for Korean domestic school cafeteria workers. This collaboration not only enhances the efficacy of the screening process but also ensures comprehensive care and support for workers, reflecting a holistic approach to occupational health and public health management. Expert groups include departments of occupational and environmental medicine, preventive medicine, pulmonology, radiology, related academic societies, the National Cancer Center, and the Korean Association for Lung Cancer. When addressing the necessity for collaboration among various expert groups, several key points emerge, including an interdisciplinary approach, understanding occupational exposure, tailored screening protocols, educational and support programs, policy development and advocacy, long-term monitoring and research, ethical considerations, and community engagement.

### LIMITATIONS

This position paper has several limitations. Firstly, there is a concern about whether the 15 thoracic radiologists can represent the entire KSTR. Nevertheless, the corresponding author selected the panel with consideration of each individual’s career, expertise, and regional representation to gather diverse perspectives. Additionally, this position paper was undertaken as a policy project by the KSTR. After the completion of the research, the findings were validated through a presentation at a symposium. Secondly, the paper is based on survey results that reflect the opinions of the panel members and lacks substantial medical evidence. Many of the panel members lack experience with LDCT lung cancer screening among Korean school cafeteria workers. This should be considered in light of the current absence of definitive scientific evidence in this field. Despite the high level of interest in this issue, there is a notable lack of scientific research on the topic in Korea. This position paper aims to promote further research in this area.

## CLOSING REMARKS

Although there is increasing societal awareness and concern regarding the occurrence of lung cancer among school cafeteria workers, there is a conspicuous absence of official positions or pronouncements from pertinent scholarly associations and specialists in this domain. This position paper recently elucidated by KSTR systematically articulates a consensus among experts regarding the adoption of LDCT lung cancer screening in this demographic. This consensus is grounded in an evaluation of existing literature and prevailing contextual realities. However, negative results were obtained regarding the benefits of LDCT lung cancer screening for Korean domestic school cafeteria workers. Due to limited comprehensive evidence in this specific field, consensus has not been reached regarding the definition of Korean domestic school cafeteria workers who require LDCT lung cancer screening, guidelines for interpretation, or the level of evidence concerning cooking tasks as a risk factor for lung cancer. Consequently, ongoing research in this area is essential. Additionally, the strategy for lung cancer screening among Korean domestic school cafeteria workers necessitates careful consideration of our nation’s unique social, environmental, and medical contexts. Among these considerations, the overarching objective of such screening programs and the reduction of lung cancer mortality rates warrant meticulous deliberation.
