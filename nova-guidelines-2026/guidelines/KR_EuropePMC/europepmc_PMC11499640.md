# Medication recommendation for Parkinson’s disease based on dynamics of symptom progression

## Abstract

Parkinson’s Disease (PD) is a chronic condition, with extensive research on initial medication selection for treatment, but limited guidance on long-term medication management. This study aims to identify optimal medication adjustment strategies based on patient clusters, focusing on either maximizing time spent in favorable health states or minimizing time spent in unfavorable ones while avoiding adverse effects. To guide treatment, we developed decision models using prescription dosages converted into standardized units for various medications. Using data from the Parkinson’s Progression Markers Initiative, we employed a multivariate time-series clustering approach to capture symptom progression dynamics. This analysis identified four distinct clusters: two representing desirable and undesirable states, and two highlighting motor-focused and non-motor-focused failures across multiple domains. We developed two separate Markov Decision Process (MDP) models to address these dual objectives, which were then integrated into a comprehensive framework that suggests optimal actions and cautions against risky ones for each patient state. This model provides valuable insights for clinical decision-making by offering flexible guidance on adjusting medication intensity rather than prescribing specific medication types, enhancing its applicability in clinical practice.

## Introduction

Parkinson’s Disease (PD) is a chronic degenerative disorder that involves both motor and non-motor symptoms. Symptoms of PD usually progress slowly, making the trajectory of PD hard to predict. Although environmental factors such as head injuries and genetic factors are known to play a role in PD, the exact cause is yet to be defined. As of current, there are no cure for PD, but treatments are available to help relieve the symptoms and maintain quality of life. These treatments include supportive therapies (physiotherapy, occupational therapy, speech, and language therapy), surgery (deep brain stimulation), and medication. Medications are especially effective in the early stages that enable many people with Parkinson’s to live a mild symptom life for a few years. Since many of PD symptoms are due to low level of dopamine resulting in brain malfunction, dopaminergic medications such as levodopa are commonly used. The usual practice for patients in early stages begin with lower potency medication which can have a milder adverse effect profile and lower dosage. Medication comes with side effects especially when given in high doses, so most patients start with low dosage. As the disease progresses, medication is adjusted to obtain optimal symptom control, reduce the “off” period (periods when benefit from Parkinson disease medications wears off and symptoms reemerge), or minimize medication adverse effects. Strategies for reducing such “off” time include increasing the dosage or adding another dopaminergic medication. However, while high-quality evidence is available for initial treatment selection, guidelines for medication adjustment strategies to optimally manage adverse PD symptoms and medication adverse effects are limited.

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a complex neurodegenerative disorder that presents significant challenges in long-term management. Prevailing research have mostly focused on predicting the onset of PD where machine learning and deep learning methodologies have been utilized to enhance predictive accuracy on multi-modal dataset. In addition to onset prediction, the progression of PD has been studied as well. Zhang et al. conducted a study on PD subtyping using deep learning algorithms where each PD patient was represented as a multidimensional time series. Their findings revealed that the initial severity of patients is not related to future progression, and the proposed approach allows for a comprehensive understanding of overall PD progression. Darko et al. compared the progression of patients classified as tremor-dominant (TD) or postural instability gait disorders (PIGD). Their results confirmed that PIGD patients exhibit more severe symptoms in the early stages. On the contrary, studies that address the relationship between treatments and PD progression have been rather scarce.

Kim et al.represents one of the first efforts where reinforcement learning (RL) was utilized to recommend medication options for PD patients. The study developed an MDP model to derive optimal drug combinations to minimize motor symptoms. Such an effort contributes to improving patient treatment and management through collaboration of artificial intelligence and medical practitioners. However, it should be noted that this study had limitations in that it relied solely on a motor symptom related scoring index when assessing PD progression. Also, only the types of medication were considered without accounting for its dosage. Given that PD patients’ medication prescription mostly involves starting with a small dose and gradually adjusting its dosage until it takes effect, prescription dosage would be a key factor in prescription policy. Overall, data-driven evidence in PD medication management is not well supported in the literature.

Our study aims to build a Markov Decision Process (MDP) model to provide guidelines on optimal medication adjustment policy for PD patients. Unlike previous approaches, our objective function focuses on either minimizing deterioration or maximizing time spent in a fair state while minimizing adverse drug events. As an advancement to standard approaches that define states based on current feature values, we present a novel time-series clustering-based approach. This method defines states based on the progression of both motor and non-motor symptoms, offering a more dynamic and comprehensive representation of patient condition. By integrating medication intensity considerations, our model represents the first medication recommendation system for PD patients that suggests appropriate medication intensities based on comprehensive symptom progression.

## Methods

We propose a sequential decision-making model that can provide guidelines on recommended and undesirable medication adjustment strategies based on the patients’ current PD progression dynamics (see Fig. 1). Specifically, we present two novel contributions regarding patient state derivation and MDP construction. First, to capture the dynamics of disease progression, we utilize a time-series clustering-based state definition to represent the state space. Second, we model two separate MDPs with distinct objectives; one aimed to stay in the desirable state (Reward MDP) and the other aimed to prevent entering the undesirable state (Penalty MDP). The purpose of such separation is to provide different insights regarding medication adjustment strategies intended for varying needs. Furthermore, via such separation, we can derive the optimal action to reach the best state and the worst action that most likely leads to the worst state, hence obtaining both the recommended and undesirable medication strategies. This would be more informative as providing optimal policy alone may not be feasible in clinical practice due to patient preference, medical insurance, or other clinical factors not observed in the dataset. In this case, clinicians could at least be aware of the riskiest action to avoid.

### Data description

Our study built the sequential decision-making model based on the Parkinson’s Progression Marker Initiative (PPMI) dataset. PPMI is a prospective observational study, that collects various clinical assessments of PD progression (demographics, clinical features, bio-specimen, imaging) and medication prescription information. A complete list of inclusion and exclusion criteria for the study can be found in the PPMI study protocol (https://www.ppmi-info.org/sites/default/files/docs/archives/Amendment-12.pdf). All participating PPMI sites received approval from an ethical standards committee prior to study initiation and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All methods performed in this study was conducted in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. To investigate the relationship between medication and PD progression, our study focuses on early-stage PD patients with sufficient visit record length for analysis. That is, we used the following criteria for patient inclusion: (1) patients with initial Hoehn and Yahr stage of 2 or lower, (2) patients with more than 5 years of record, (3) patients with recordings for all features of interest. As a result, 552 patients with a total of 5,052 visit records from 2010 to 2023 are selected as the study cohort. Since visit records were timestamped by months with varying time intervals, monthly data was aggregated to obtain year-wise time-series dataset. Year-wise aggregation was used because, firstly, visit record frequency varied substantially among patients and secondly, PD progression is known to be slow which necessitates a longer time interval to capture the dynamics. If multiple visit records fall under the same year, a year-wise record was obtained through arithmetic mean of the visits. If a patient had no visit for a full year, feature values from the two nearest visit records were interpolated for imputation. Inclusion of all clinical features available within PPMI would lead to several hundreds of features with sparse data since many of the variables are only recorded for a small subset of patients. Hence, to avoid data missingness as much as possible, we aimed to only include the major features exhibiting high data resolution. This would also be beneficial in model interpretation which is essential in healthcare domain. As a result, we considered 10 different clinical assessments which can be divided into motor related measurements and non-motor related assessments.

#### Motor assessments

- Movement Disorder Society Sponsored Revision of the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale II ∼ IV (MDS-UPDRS): MDS-UPDRS is a comprehensive and widely used tool designed to evaluate the severity and progression of Parkinson’s disease. This scale includes parts of total 50 questions that thoroughly assess both motor and non-motor symptoms associated with the condition. Part II captures the patient’s experience with tasks such as dressing, eating, and walking, providing insight into how Parkinson’s disease impacts their quality of life. Part III involves a clinical evaluation of motor function by a healthcare professional. It includes tests for rigidity, tremor, bradykinesia (slowness of movement), and postural instability, offering an objective measure of the patient’s motor abilities. Part IV examines the complications that arise from long-term use of Parkinson’s medications, such as levodopa-induced dyskinesias and motor fluctuations..

#### Non-motor assessments

- Hoehn & Yahn (HnY) Stage: HnY stage divides the stages of PD from 1 to 5 where 1 being the least severe stage.
- MDS-UPDRS I: Part I of MDS-UPDRS consists of non-motor experiences of daily living.
- SCOPA-AUT: The Scale for Outcomes in Parkinson’s disease for Autonomic symptoms (SCOPA-AUT) is an evaluation of autonomic symptoms in patients with PD. The scale is self-completed by patients and consists of 25 items assessing the following items: gastrointestinal (7), urinary (6), cardiovascular (3), thermoregulatory (4), pupillomotor (1), sexual (2 for male, 2 for female).
- Rapid Eye Movement Sleep Behavior Disorder Questionnaire (RBD): RBD is a parasomnia involving dream enactment behavior associated with loss of atonia during rapid eye movement (REM) sleep. In Rapid Eye Movement Sleep Behavior Disorder Questionnaire, 10 questions are answered in yes or no. REM sleep behavior disorder is common for prodromal PD.
- Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCa): MoCa is a rapid screening instrument for mild cognitive impairment (MCI).
- Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT): SDMT screens for organic cerebral dysfunction in both children and adults. It is an effective marker of brain processing speed, attention and working memory.
- Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS): GDS is a 30-item questionnaire that aims to measure the degree of depression. A total GDS scale of <9 is considered normal, ≤19 mild depression and ≥20 is considered severe depression.

#### Medications

Medications can be used to improve the main symptoms of Parkinson’s disease where different medication options come with varying short- and long-term effects on the patient. The most common medications are levodopa, dopamine agonists, and monoamine oxidase-B (MAO-B) inhibitors [4]. To compare the dosage across different types of medications, we utilized a feature named Levodopa Equivalent Daily Dose (LEDD) to compute the contribution made by each medication. LEDD uses the estimate of the levodopa equivalent dose (LED) which is the amount of levodopa that has a similar effect as the drug taken and adding together all the LEDs in a day output the LEDD. Specifically, we focus on the change of LEDD as a representation of medication policy change and aim to extract the optimal policy via an MDP. Any missing LEDD values were imputed using features Levodopa Equivalent Factor, Daily Dose and Frequency through the following equation: LEDD=(LevodopaEquivalentFactor)×(DailyDose)×(Frequency). If any of the three features were missing, LEDD was estimated as the median of LEDDs with similar medication labels. The detailed statistics of all the features utilized in this study are shown in Table 1. After imputation and dropping null values, our medication dataset consisted of 5,802 different prescription data.

#### Time series clustering based states

To build an MDP model, an accurate representation of the dynamics of patient health state is necessary. As PD progression tends to be slow, we aimed to capture the progression of feature values over multiple years. For this, 3-year time series data were extracted with rolling window of one year for each time-series recording of the patients. Subsequently, K-means clustering was performed to extract K number of clusters that exhibit similar time-series patterns. Specifically, as we are clustering time-series data (length of 3), we utilized dynamic time warping (DTW) to perform time-series clustering. DTW is an effective algorithm for measuring similarity between temporal sequences.

Prior studies showed that PD can be subtyped into 4 clusters: mild, non-motor focused, motor focused, severe on motor and non-motor–. Clustering with K=4 showed relevant results with our PPMI dataset where the four clusters are depicted in Fig. 2. The clusters are named Fair State (FS), Worsening State (WS), Motor-focused worsening State (MS), and Deteriorating State (DS) based on the dynamics of feature value changes over the three years. In FS, all features are kept constant on average. In WS, mild deterioration over various feature categories can be observed, while in MS, major worsening in MDS-UPDRS III can be found which can be seen as a major motor-failure signal. In DS, all features are deteriorating on average. Figure 2 depicts the detailed change over 3-year period where the shaded area represents standard deviation and thick colored line in the middle shows the average progression. High positive slope over 3-year period represents corresponding feature worsening, while low slope represents mild maintenance. The analysis of our 4 clusters can be validated in Fig. 3, where average changes in regularized feature values over 3-year are visualized. Positive number or darker blue color represents improvement and negative number or darker red color refers to deterioration. The entire column for FS is mostly colored in blue, meaning all features improve or at least maintain, while the column for DS is mostly colored in red or orange, meaning most features are deteriorating. In WS and MS, the columns are colored in a variety of orange, yellow and light blue. While WS shows more yellow or orange over non-motor features, MS shows a strong orange color in MDS-UPDRS III, representing motor-symptom deterioration.

#### Markov decision process formulation

A Markov Decision Process (MDP) is represented as a tuple (S,A,P,R,γ), where S is the state space, A is the action space, P:S×A→Δ(S) is the transition probability function (which gives a distribution over next states), R:S×A→R is the reward function, and γ∈[0,1) is the discount factor. A policy π:S→Δ(A) is a function that maps each state to a probability distribution over actions (a single action if deterministic). The objective of the MDP is to find the optimal policy π∗ that maximizes the expected cumulative reward, π∗=argmaxπE∑t=0∞γtR(st,at), where st and at represent the state and action at time t, and at∼π(st). To determine π∗, we assumed stationary policy and used policy iteration, which relies on iteratively optimizing the Bellman Expectation Equation: π′(s)=argmaxa∈AR(s,a)+γ∑s′∈SP(s′|s,a)Vπ(s′), where Vπ(s)=R(s,π(s))+γ∑s′∈SP(s′|s,π(s))Vπ(s′) is the value function for state s, and s′ is the next state. The optimal policy π∗ is derived by selecting the action that maximizes the expected future reward. Policy iteration guarantees convergence to the globally optimal policy in polynomial time. We developed two infinite-horizon MDP models, Reward MDP aimed to stay in desirable state and Penalty MDP aimed to avoid undesirable state. The two MDPs share all elements except the reward function. Our goal is to find the optimal policy for both models, as well as considering the worst policy.

- States: States are defined as the four time-series clusters FS, WS, MS, DS obtained via DTW based K-Means.
- Actions: Actions are defined based on the difference in LEDD prescription between two adjacent decision epochs. Instead of using the value itself, we discretized LEDD difference into three groups to differentiate between no change, subtle increase, and significant increase in LEDD. Note that as medications are hardly discontinued for Parkinson’s patients, we assume decrease in LEDD is not feasible. Analysis of the dataset confirms the validation of this assumption. The thresholds are determined through consideration of frequency and distribution of LEDD difference where LEDD difference of 0 is considered as ‘maintaining’ action, LEDD difference of (0,75] is considered as ‘subtle increase’ action, and any LEDD difference greater than 75 is considered as ‘increase’ action. Additionally, since this is based on the average LEDD prescription values over three years, it should be taken into consideration when prescribing. Moreover, when reviewing the actual retention rates of prescribed drugs, we observed that this rate gradually decreased and drug prescriptions became more diverse as action increased from ‘maintaining’ to ‘increase’.
- Transition Probability: Transition probability is computed for each action via maximum likelihood estimation using the data and is shown in Table 2. It can be observed that transition probabilities do not necessarily show monotonicity in state or action ordering. Instead, increasing LEDD intensity tends to be more effective in MS and FS states while for DS state, increasing LEDD is not so effective. Hence, higher intensity medications may be more effective for relatively well maintaining PD patients or those mainly with motor-failure symptoms while prescribing higher dosage may not be much of effect for already deteriorating patients.
- Reward Function: Reward function of MDP must reflect the objective of the model where we aim for desirable (or prevent undesirable) health states with minimal medication dosage increase. For Reward MDP, a reward of +1 is given for each stay in FS, while for Penalty MDP a penalty of -1 is given for each stay in DS. In addition, medication comes with side effects especially when given in high doses (e.g., levodopa may be linked with dyskinesias, dopamine agonists can cause development of compulsive behaviors). Thus, we penalize the action of increasing LEDD where ‘subtle increase’ action penalty is denoted as -α and ‘increase’ action as -β where α≤β≤1 is assumed since higher dosing requires higher penalty, and this penalty should not be a dominant factor over the 1 penalty/reward given for the states.
- Discount Factor: Discount factor of γ=0.9 is used meaning we put similar importance on late rewards/penalties as opposed to early ones.

## Results

### Medication policy

The MDP model was solved through policy iteration where we started with an initial policy and the Bellman expectation was iteratively improved until convergence. A general solution for all possible hyperparameter settings is depicted in Fig. 4. The x-axis represents β and y-axis represents α values where only the lower right half triangle is depicted as α≤β should hold. For the left (right) four subplots, each subplot refers to the four states and different colors indicate the varying combinations of optimal (worst) policies derived from the Reward MDP and Penalty MDP model represented as a tuple. We note that an increase in either α or β results in the polices being more reluctant towards corresponding medication increase policy, while a decrease results in more generous policies towards corresponding medication usage. These results coincide with intuition as increasing either α or β implies imposing larger penalty for increasing medication usage. Although Reward MDP model and Penalty MDP model aim for different goals of maximizing the stay time in FS and minimizing the stay time in DS, respectively, optimal and worst policies show similar patterns. ‘Increase’ action is only recommended for FS or MS states while it is never recommended for WS or DS states. In fact, ‘increase’ is determined as the worst policy for these two states under all parameter value settings. Hence, it can be inferred that higher dosage medications tend to be less effective once symptoms begin to deteriorate across various assessment categories. Comparing the two states MS and WS, stronger medication policy is recommended for MS, implying that medication tends to be more effective in relieving motor symptoms. Also, state FS is most sensitive to hyperparameter value changes meaning depending on how one views the relative penalty of LEDD increase, medication prescription policy for well-performing patients is subject to vary accordingly. Between the two MDP models, the Reward MDP model suggests higher LEDD dosage compared to the Penalty MDP model under the same α,β settings. Hence, prescription policy intended to reach the desirable state tends to be stronger in terms of medication intensity, i.e., higher LEDD is more useful for staying in FS than avoiding DS.

Comparing optimal policy (left) and worst policy (right), in general, ‘maintaining’ action largely dominates in optimal policy while ‘subtle increase’ and ‘increase’ are commonly selected as the worst policy. The cutting lines tend to be more complex for worst policy plots implying high-risk actions (actions to avoid) are impacted by the interactive effects of α and β values rather than depending on a single parameter value. Among the four states, the cutting line for FS is most complex while that for DS is the simplest. For patients in DS state, maintaining LEDD is recommended while increasing LEDD is suggested to be avoided for both MDP models under most hyperparameter value settings.

#### Integrated policy

Each MDP model generates two outputs for each patient state: recommended prescription (optimal policy) and high-risk prescription (worst policy), leaving us with four outputs in total. To provide a final single prescription policy, we integrate our policy into one optimal policy and one worst policy by choosing the optimal policy from the Reward MDP model and worst policy from the Penalty MDP model. Optimal policy is selected from the Reward model to suggest the recommended action that most likely will maintain fair status while worst policy is selected from the Penalty model to warn against high-risk action that most likely will lead to deteriorating status. The resulting policy is illustrated in Fig. 5 for all possible hyperparameter value settings. Each subplot refers to the four states and the different colors refer to the (optimal action, worst action) tuple.

Similar to Fig. 4, states FS and MS show similar pattern, while states WS and DS behave similarly. It can be observed that ‘maintaining’ action is never chosen as the worst action, which suggests prescribing the same LEDD level is considered as relatively low risk action. For states FS and MS with small α settings, as β increases, the value of ‘increase’ action drops, thus changing the optimal action from ‘increase’ to ‘maintaining’ and the worst action from ‘subtle increase’ to ‘increase’. As mentioned earlier, the Reward model tends to tolerate higher α and β values compared to the Penalty model, resulting the change of worst policy to occur before that of optimal policy. Thus, there exists a small range of parameter settings in which ‘increase’ action becomes both the worst policy of Penalty model and the optimal policy of the Reward model. This small region is observed just before the optimal policy changes to ‘maintaining’ and is depicted as dark red color for states FS and MS in Fig. 5. Note that for the worst state DS, optimal action is to maintain and worst action is to increase under most parameter settings. This again emphasizes the potential harm high intensity medications can bring for PD patients whose symptoms are already deteriorating.

## Discussion

### Principal results

Our research presented MDP models to derive optimal medication policy for early-stage PD patients. To capture the disease progression, time-series data consisting of clinical feature value changes over a 3-year period were extracted to which DTW based K-means clustering was applied. Four distinct clusters were identified and were subsequently used to define the states of the MDP model which include desirable health state showing improvement or maintain in status, undesirable health state showing deteriorating status, and two opposing states where one shows motor-related failures and the other shows varying patterns across different categories. We utilized LEDD to represent the Parkinson’s-related medication dosage and focused on the prescription change in LEDD and its relationship with PD progression. An MDP model aiming for a desirable health state with minimal medication was constructed to find the optimal medication policy. In addition, an opposing MDP model aiming to prevent the stay in an undesirable health state was developed to derive the worst policy which can be regarded as high-risk prescription policy. The optimal policy from the Reward model and the worst policy from the Penalty model were selected and integrated to provide the final LEDD-based medication continuation policy for PD patients. To be clinically applicable, we provide flexibility on medication selection since the optimal policy from our model provides guidance on the strength of the medication rather than deciding the exact medication type. For instance, regarding the optimal action of ‘increase’, clinical practitioners can choose to either change the medication type to a stronger one or increase the dosage of the current medication type. We believe this type of decision should be determined in clinical practice by considering expert knowledge, external factors, or patient preferences.

#### Limitations

There exist several limitations to our study. First, the hyperparameters α and β represent the degree of penalty imposed for increasing medication dosage associated with ‘subtle increase’ and ‘increase’ actions. The choice of hyperparameter values depend on clinical preference of how much emphasize to put on relieving PD symptoms vs. avoiding side effects of medications. Future research direction can consider the occurrence of side effect or adverse drug event to directly model such penalty. Also, we considered limited number of clinical assessments when defining the health state to avoid data missingness as much as possible. Inclusion of all features in the dataset could allow consideration of more aspects of PD progression at the cost of interpretation. Another important point to consider is that all research, including model training and validation, was conducted on a subset of the PPMI dataset, specifically patients with an initial HnY stage below 2. This cohort is generally younger, more educated, affluent, and in the early stages of the disease. If validation were extended to a different cohort, such as one consisting of patients in later and more severe stages of Parkinson’s disease, we anticipate that our optimal policies may perform worse, potentially no better than random actions. Consequently, additional caution is necessary when generalizing the findings of this research to other patient populations. In addition, our research can be extended to incorporate multiple modalities. Recent studies, such as Makarious et.al. have proposed multi-modal predictive models that leverage genetic, clinical demographic, and transcrip-tomics features. Furthermore, Kim et.al. conducted research that additionally considered adverse events. Similarly, our research aims to advance by integrating various multi-modal datasets.

#### Conclusion

Our research identified subtypes of PD and reconfirmed the existence of mild, non-motor focused, motor-focused, and severe clusters using a novel time-series clustering approach. We developed two MDP models: one aimed at maximizing the time spent in the favorable state FS and the other minimizing time in the unfavorable state DS. These models were integrated into a comprehensive framework by combining the optimal policy from the Reward Model and the worst policy from the Penalty Model. This framework for long-term medication management incorporates LEDD and time-series data, providing a dynamic understanding of patient outcomes, particularly for younger, more educated, affluent, and earlier-stage PD patients. By using two separate MDPs with distinct objectives, we found that prescription policies aimed at maintaining a desirable health state tend to involve higher LEDD, as increased doses are more effective for staying in FS than avoiding DS. Compared to clinician prescription patterns, our model suggests a more adaptive medication adjustment policy, potentially leading to increased time in FS or reduced time in DS, thereby enhancing symptom management.

Our approach offers several advancements over existing algorithms in PD management. Unlike previous studies that primarily focused on onset prediction or limited aspects of progression,, our method employs a time-series based representation of patient disease progress, capturing both motor and non-motor symptoms. This improves upon prior reinforcement learning approaches that considered only motor symptoms and medication types without accounting for dosage. Our model’s incorporation of LEDD provides a more comprehensive approach to medication management. Furthermore, while some studies used deep learning for PD subtyping, our MDP framework uniquely combines subtype identification with dynamic disease progression and treatment recommendations, while maintaining interpretability. These findings have significant implications for long-term medication decisions in PD, providing clinicians with a data-driven tool to optimize patient outcomes over time. This integration of time-series analysis and MDP modeling for medication management represents a novel contribution to the field, addressing the gap in data-driven, long-term medication decision support for PD. Additionally, this framework can be extended to different patient demographics, disease progressions, or treatment strategies, laying the foundation for more personalized approaches in PD care.
