# Developing evidence-based Korean medicine clinical practice guidelines for pediatric anorexia

## Abstract

### Background

Pediatric anorexia is a common condition that can adversely affect the physical growth, nutritional status, and psychosocial development of children. Despite the clinical relevance of Korean medicine, standardized treatment guidelines rooted in the same have not yet been established. This study aimed to develop evidence-based Korean Medicine Clinical Practice Guidelines (KM-CPGs) for pediatric anorexia to support clinical decision-making and ensure safe and effective care.

### Methods

The KM-CPG were developed using evidence-based methodologies, including systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and expert consensus (Delphi method). The level of evidence and grade of recommendation were derived using the grading of recommendation assessment, development, and evaluation (GRADE) approach.

### Results

A total of 15 key recommendations were derived for Korean medicine interventions, which included herbal medicine, acupuncture, Chuna manual therapy, and acupoint herbal patching, either as monotherapies or in combination with conventional medicine. Most Korean medicine interventions showed moderate to low levels of evidence, but demonstrated meaningful improvements in anorexia-related outcomes, with a low risk of adverse events.

### Conclusions

The KM-CPG for pediatric anorexia present standardized, evidence-based guidance on Korean medicine treatments and may serve as a foundation for improved clinical care and further research.

## Background

Although the proportion of children with anorexia-related insufficient caloric intake has decreased due to improvements in dietary patterns following recent economic developments, parents remain concerned about their children’s poor appetite. The primary clinical features of anorexia include a prolonged loss of appetite and reduced food intake, although symptom severity varies among individuals. The reported prevalence rates of pediatric anorexia vary across studies, ranging from approximately 14–50% in preschool-aged children and 7–27% in school-aged children. It most commonly occurs between the ages of 1 and 6, typically beginning during the spoon-feeding or self-feeding stages. Children who experience prolonged anorexia may develop generalized physical weakness and nonorganic failure to thrive, which can contribute to short stature, developmental delays, and behavioral disorders. Sustained nutritional insufficiency may result in nutritional imbalances, leading to malnutrition, anemia, rickets, and weakened immunity, necessitating appropriate management.

In 2023, patients under 20 years old with anorexia were more frequently seen at Korean medicine institutions than at conventional medical facilities, indicating a preference for Korean medicine in managing pediatric anorexia. Additionally, the effectiveness and safety of Korean medicine treatments, such as herbal medicine, acupuncture, and Chuna manual therapy, for pediatric anorexia have been established. However, to the best of our knowledge, Korean medicine clinical practice guidelines (KM-CPG) for pediatric anorexia have not yet been developed. Establishing such guidelines is essential to ensure standardized and evidence-based treatments, improve clinical outcomes, and enhance patient safety. Although international organizations such as the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), Guidelines International Network (G-I-N), and National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) have published 19 guidelines on psychiatric eating disorders such as anorexia nervosa, none target idiopathic anorexia. Additionally, in China, the Guideline for Diagnosis and Treatment of Common Diseases in Traditional Chinese Medicine Pediatrics addressing pediatric anorexia was published in 2012, but it requires updating to reflect current evidence.

Therefore, in response to the need for the development of KM-CPGs, the “Korean Medicine Innovative Technologies Development” was launched by the Ministry of Health and Welfare in 2020. This project supports the development and revision of KM-CPGs and plans to develop 51 new guidelines and update 25 existing guidelines over 10 years. Pediatric anorexia was also selected as a target disease for the new KM-CPG development project, as it is a frequent disease in the Korean medicine clinical setting.

The KM-CPG for pediatric anorexia aimed to establish a standardized Korean medicine-based treatment for pediatric anorexia by integrating research evidence and expert opinion, applying methodologies such as evidence-based medicine (EBM) and the De Novo process, promoting effective and evidence-based treatment, enhancing consistency in care, and improving patient outcomes.

## Methods

### Target population, users, and healthcare setting

The KM-CPG targets pediatric and adolescent patients aged under 20 years who exhibit reduced food intake and maladaptive eating behaviors for more than one month. Specifically, it includes children who fall under the diagnostic category of Undereating Disorder in the Diagnostic Classification of Mental Health and Developmental Disorders of Infancy and Early Childhood (DC:0–5™) or Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder (ARFID) as defined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5). Patients with secondary anorexia resulting from underlying medical conditions were excluded from the study.

The intended users of the KM-CPG included primary care Korean medicine doctors, pediatric specialists in Korean medicine, and other healthcare professionals with relevant clinical knowledge involved in the diagnosis and treatment of pediatric anorexia. Additionally, researchers and students in the field of Korean medicine, as well as patients and their caregivers, may refer to the contents of this guideline.

These clinical practice guidelines apply to outpatient and inpatient care in both primary Korean medicine clinics and secondary or tertiary Korean medicine hospitals where pediatric anorexia is treated.

### Guideline development group composition

The multidisciplinary development group was confirmed following the approval of the development plan for the “KM-CPG for Pediatric Anorexia”. After the clinical Korean medicine practitioner panel of the “Korean Medicine Innovative Technologies Development” was assigned, the development group was finalized.

The development group is composed of a 24-member Development Committee, which is responsible for the practical development and drafting of the clinical practice guidelines, and a 10-member Review Committee, which is responsible for the final approval of the guidelines. The Development Committee is composed of a Chair (overall coordination), Working Members (responsible for developing clinical questions, conducting systematic literature reviews and meta-analyses, and drafting the guidelines), and Panel Members (participating in the development of clinical questions and Delphi consensus processes and providing expert advice on systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and guideline writing). The Review Committee includes clinical and methodological experts who are not part of the Development Committee.

All members hold a master’s or doctoral degree in Korean medicine and are currently employed as clinical professors or assistant professors at domestic Korean medicine universities, or as researchers at the Korea Institute of Oriental Medicine or graduate schools of Korean medicine. All members are also actively engaged in academic and research activities in Korean medicine. Furthermore, all members completed certified training in clinical practice guideline methodology.

Detailed information regarding this guideline and its development group is publicly available on the National Clearinghouse for Korean Medicine: http://101.79.85.177/nckm/module/practiceGuide/view.do?guide_idx=326&menu_idx=14.

### Development protocol and registration

This KM-CPG for pediatric anorexia was developed in accordance with the protocol outlined in the “Manual for developing Evidence-based Clinical Practice Guidelines of Korean Medicine 2.0,” published by the Korean Medicine Innovation and Technology Development Project Group (http://101.79.85.177/nckm/board/view.do?menu_idx=32&manage_idx=24&board_idx=23541&group_depth=0&parent_idx=0&group_idx=0&search_type=title%2Bcontent&search_text=&rowCount=12&viewPage=1). The guideline was also pre-registered with the National Clearinghouse for Korean Medicine (https://nikom.or.kr/nckm/module/practiceGuide/index.do?menu_idx=32&manage_idx=24&board_idx=23541&group_depth=0&parent_idx=0&group_idx=0&search_type=title%2Bcontent&search_text=&rowCount=12&viewPage=1).

### Declaration of conflicts of interest

The members of the Development Committee have declared no conflicts of interest with any specific institution or researcher related to the KM-CPG. All members signed a conflict of interest disclosure form in December, 2021. Accordingly, no conflicts of interest arose among the participating researchers. Although the results from a clinical study conducted by the principal investigator were used as evidence for this guideline, the investigator was not involved in the initial selection of the literature to minimize the relevant risk of bias.

### Establishment of key questions

The research team initially developed a set of key questions through literature review and internal meetings. Subsequently, the finalized key questions were refined based on expert consultation and feedback from the Association of Pediatrics of Korean Medicine. The study population included children and adolescents aged under 20 years with idiopathic anorexia. Interventions included Korean medicine treatments, such as herbal medicine, acupuncture, and Chuna manual therapy. The comparator groups included waiting lists, sham/placebo interventions, conventional medicine (e.g., domperidone, digestive enzymes, and probiotics), and dietary supplements (e.g., vitamins, minerals, and amino acids). Outcome measures included indicators related to pediatric anorexia: anthropometric (weight, height, body mass index [BMI], abdominal subcutaneous fat [ASF]); dietary (food intake, appetite recovery time [ART]); gastric motility (electrogastrography [EGG], gastric half emptying time [GHET], gastric antrum contraction frequency [GACF]); biochemical (hemoglobin); hormonal (leptin, neuropeptide Y [NPY], gastrin, ghrelin, motilin, orexin); digestive enzymes (amylase); and nutritional markers (Zn, Fe, Ca, Mg, Pb). Additionally, the total effective rate (TER), based on the improvement in anorexia symptoms and recurrence rate, was included. Only randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were included for acupuncture and Chuna manual therapy. For herbal medicine, RCTs and non-randomized clinical studies (e.g., cohort studies, case-control studies, before-and-after studies, and case reports) were included as well, to reflect the realities of clinical practice in Korean medicine. The key questions are described in detail in Appendix 1 of the Supplementary Materials.

### Literature search and selection

Database selection was determined through a development committee meeting that included a literature search specialist, and they included Medical Literature Analysis and Retrieval System Online (MEDLINE), Embase, the Cochrane Library, Allied and Complementary Medicine Database (AMED), China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang Data, VIP Information, Citation Information by National Institute of Informatics (CiNii), Oriental Medicine Advanced Searching Integrated System (OASIS), Korean Medical Database (KMbase), Korean studies Information Service System (KISS), and Science & Technology Information Open Nexus (ScienceON). The search covered all available literature prior to the date of the search without language restrictions. Search terms were based on standardized terminology from each country and constructed with reference to the key questions. The full search strategy is presented in Appendix 2 of the Supplementary Materials.

For acupuncture and Chuna manual therapy, the level of evidence was determined by a meta-analysis that included only RCTs and their systematic reviews. For herbal medicine, however, both RCTs and domestic non-randomized clinical studies (such as cohort studies, case-control studies, before-and-after studies, and case reports) were included to reflect the medical reality in Korea.

To avoid conflicts of interest, any studies conducted by a member of the development committee were excluded from the literature selection, risk of bias assessment, and analysis. During the literature exclusion process, studies were removed if they did not address pediatric anorexia, did not use Korean medicine treatment in the treatment group, or were otherwise considered inappropriate for the selection criteria by the researchers.

Based on these key questions, at least two researchers independently conducted searches and organized the results using EndNote 20 (Clarivate, Philadelphia, PA, USA). To increase the sensitivity of clinical evidence identification, comparators and outcomes were excluded from the search strategy, which was developed through consensus of the full development committee. Pairs of reviewers performed duplicate removal, title and abstract screening, and full-text reviews. Discrepancies were resolved through a consensus involving the principal investigator when necessary. PRISMA flow charts for each clinical question are included in Appendix 3 of the supplementary material.

### Risk of bias assessment

To assess the risk of bias in the selected literature, studies were categorized based on their design into randomized controlled trials and non-randomized controlled studies (Non-randomized Controlled Trial, NRCT; Case-control Trials, CCT). The assessments were conducted independently by at least two researchers using The Cochrane Collaboration’s tool for assessing risk of bias (ROB) and the Risk of Bias Assessment tool for Non-randomised Study (RoBANS), respectively.

### Evidence synthesis and analysis

A meta-analysis of studies with the same intervention, comparator, and outcome was performed using Review Manager 5.4 (Copenhagen: The Nordic Cochrane Center, The Cochrane Collaboration, 2014). For continuous data, results were presented as either mean difference (MD) or standardized mean difference (SMD) with a 95% confidence interval (CI). Dichotomous data were analyzed using risk ratios (RRs) with 95% CI. Heterogeneity across studies was assessed using Higgins I2 statistics and the chi-square test.

### Level of evidence and grade of recommendation

The level of evidence was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach developed by the GRADE Working Group and was categorized into four levels of evidence (Table 1). The recommended grades are classified into six categories (Table 2).

### Consensus building and external review

A consensus on the recommendations, including their strengths and supporting evidence levels, was achieved through the Delphi method, involving a panel of nine multidisciplinary experts. A questionnaire was distributed along with relevant evidence, and consensus was defined as a score of 7, 8, or 9 from at least two-thirds of the respondents.

To enhance the quality of the draft guidelines, revisions were made based on an external review conducted in March, May, and November 2022 by a research advisory group, technical review group, and Korean Medicine Innovative Technologies Development.

### Korean medicine monotherapy

#### Herbal medicine

To improve the symptoms of pediatric patients with anorexia, herbal medicine may be considered (R1, grade of recommendation: C; level of evidence: Low).

Several clinical studies have shown that herbal medicine treatment for pediatric anorexia produces significant effects compared to waiting lists or conventional medicine. Based on the level of evidence and the direction of the supporting sub-recommendations, a Delphi consensus among expert panel members resulted in the assignment of a grade C recommendation with a low level of evidence.

To improve the symptoms of pediatric patients with anorexia, herbal medicine should be considered rather than observation (R1-1, grade of recommendation: B; level of evidence: Moderate).

This recommendation is based on four RCTs and one before-and-after study. Compared to waiting list or placebo control groups, children receiving herbal medicine showed a greater increase in body weight (MD 0.86 kg, 95% CI [0.40, 1.32]) and a higher TER (RR 1.52, 95% CI [1.33, 1.74]), indicating greater symptom improvement. No significant differences in adverse events were observed, suggesting that the herbal medicines are safe. In one before-and-after study (reference 5 in Appendix 6 of the Supplementary Materials), the median K-CEBQ score increased from 66 to 95, with the subscale score for “slowness in eating/satiety responsiveness” increasing from 20 to 26.

The benefits of herbal medicine are clear, and the overall level of evidence is moderate. Therefore, the confidence in the effect estimate is moderate, indicating that the actual effect is likely to be close to the estimate, but may differ substantially. The adverse events associated with herbal medicine did not differ significantly from those associated with conventional medicine, suggesting a low risk of harm. According to a 2021 survey, herbal medicine was the most commonly used intervention for pediatric anorexia in Korean medicine, as reported by 97.7% of respondents, indicating high clinical applicability. Given its clear benefits and high applicability in clinical practice, herbal medicine was assigned a grade B recommendation.

To improve the symptoms of pediatric patients with anorexia, herbal medicine may be considered rather than conventional medicine (R1-2, grade of recommendation: C; level of evidence: Very Low).

This recommendation is based on a meta-analysis of 134 RCTs, which included studies utilizing representative prescriptions corresponding to four pattern identification types: the “stomach qi deficiency” pattern: Igongsan (Yigong-san (R1-2-1), Sagunjatang (Sijunzi-tang) (R1-2-2), Samryeongbaekchulsan (Shenlingbaizhu-san) (R1-2-3), and Chilmibaekchulsan (Qiweibaizhu-san) (R1-2-4); the “stomach yin deficiency” pattern: Yangwijeungaektang (Yangweizengye-tang) (R1-2-5); the “food accumulation” pattern: Geonbihwan (Jianpi-wan) (R1-2-6) and Bohwahwan (Baohe-wan) (R1-2-7); and the “liver depression and spleen deficiency” pattern: Jojungtang (Tiaozhong-tang) (R1-2-8) and Tongsayobang (Tongxieyao-fang) (R1-2-9).

Compared to the conventional medicine control group, the herbal medicine group showed greater improvements across multiple outcomes: body weight increased by 0.89 kg (MD 0.89, 95% CI [0.23, 1.54]), weight gain by 0.52 kg (MD 0.52, 95% CI [0.30, 0.73]), BMI by 2.03 (MD 2.03, 95% CI [1.57, 2.49]), and food intake by 7.23 g/day (MD 7.23, 95% CI [5.24, 9.22]). GACF increased by 1.37 times/min (MD 1.37, 95% CI [1.17, 1.57]), the proportion of normal EGG was 1.94 times higher (RR 1.94, 95% CI [1.32, 2.86]), and TER was 1.25 times higher (RR 1.25, 95% CI [1.22, 1.28]). GHET decreased by 14.00 min (MD − 14.00, 95% CI [− 19.94, − 8.06]), and the recurrence rate was lower at 0.15 times that of the control group (RR 0.15, 95% CI [0.09, 0.25]). Fewer adverse events were reported in the herbal medicine group, suggesting that herbal medicines are a safe treatment option. There were 4 adverse events in the treatment group (1 case of vomiting, 2 of nausea, and 1 of abdominal pain) and 9 in the control group (2 cases of mild nausea and vomiting, 1 of skin rash, 1 of dizziness, 3 of vomiting, and 2 of abdominal pain).

Compared to conventional medicine, the benefits of herbal medicine appear to be valid; however, owing to the overall very low level of evidence, there is very little confidence in the effect estimate, and the actual effect is likely to differ substantially from the estimate. Fewer adverse events were observed in the herbal medicine group than in the control group, suggesting a lower risk of harm. Considering its clinical applicability and potential benefit, and reflecting the expert opinion that the strength of the recommendation may be upgraded, a grade C recommendation was assigned.

To improve the symptoms of pediatric patients with anorexia with the “stomach qi deficiency” pattern, Bojungikgitang (Buzhongyiqi-tang), Samchulgeonbitang (Shenzhujianpi-tang), Hyangsayukgunjatang (Xiangshaliujunzi-tang), Hyangsayangwitang (Xiangshayangwei-tang), Insamyangwitang (Renshenyangwei-tang) are recommended based on the expert group consensus (R1-3, grade of recommendation: GPP).

A 2021 survey identified several herbal prescriptions that are frequently used for pediatric anorexia with the “stomach qi deficiency” pattern. Although RCTs were not available for some of these formulas, bibliographic evidence and expert consensus support their clinical application. As these prescriptions are widely cited in Korean medicine literature and commonly used in clinical practice, they were assigned a GPP grade recommendation for improving symptoms in children with anorexia presenting with the “stomach qi deficiency” pattern.

To improve the symptoms of pediatric patients with anorexia with the “stomach yin deficiency” pattern, Sogeonjungtang (Xiaojianzhong-tang) is recommended based on the expert group consensus (R1-4, grade of recommendation: GPP).

Sogeonjungtang (Xiaojianzhong-tang), which showed the highest usage rate of 59.5% in the 2021 survey, was assigned a GPP recommendation based on research evidence and expert consensus.

#### Acupuncture, moxibustion, and acupoint herbal patching

To improve the symptoms of pediatric patients with anorexia, acupuncture should be considered (R2, grade of recommendation: B; level of evidence: Moderate).

Studies have shown that acupuncture produces significantly better outcomes than waiting lists or conventional medicine. The expert panel assessed the level of evidence as moderate and, through the Delphi consensus, assigned a grade B recommendation.

To improve the symptoms of pediatric patients with anorexia, acupuncture should be considered rather than observation (R2-1, grade of recommendation: B; level of evidence: Moderate).

This recommendation was based on a meta-analysis of three RCTs. In studies using acupuncture at Sifeng (四縫, EX-UE10), the treatment group showed greater improvements than the waiting list group, with increases in ASF by 0.58 cm (MD 0.58, 95% CI [0.57, 0.60]), body weight by 0.35 kg (MD 0.35, 95% CI [0.20, 0.50]), and TER by 7.72 times (RR 7.72, 95% CI [5.11, 11.67]). There was no significant difference in adverse events between the groups, indicating that Sifeng acupuncture is a safe treatment (2 mild adverse events in the treatment group; 0 in the control group).

The benefits of acupuncture are evident. With a moderate level of evidence, there is moderate confidence in the effect estimate, although the actual effects may differ substantially. Given the low risk of harm and high clinical applicability, acupuncture was assigned a grade B recommendation.

To improve the symptoms of pediatric patients with anorexia, acupuncture should be considered rather than conventional medicine (R2-2, grade of recommendation: B; level of evidence: Moderate).

This recommendation was based on a meta-analysis of 10 RCTs. The acupuncture group showed a 1.28 times higher TER than the conventional medicine group (RR 1.28, 95% CI [1.19, 1.37]), indicating greater improvement in anorexia symptoms. No adverse events have been reported with acupuncture at Sifeng, suggesting that it is a safe treatment option.

The benefits of acupuncture are clear and the overall level of evidence is moderate. Confidence in the effect estimate is also moderate, meaning that the actual effect is likely to be close to the estimate but may differ substantially. Given the absence of reported adverse events, a low risk of harm, and high clinical applicability, a grade B recommendation was assigned.

To improve the symptoms of pediatric patients with anorexia, moxibustion therapy may be considered rather than conventional medicine (R3, grade of recommendation: C; level of evidence: Low).

This recommendation was based on a meta-analysis of two RCTs. The moxibustion group showed a 1.16 times higher TER than the conventional treatment group (RR 1.16, 95% CI [1.04, 1.31]), indicating greater improvement in anorexia symptoms. Adverse events associated with moxibustion were limited to mild superficial burns that did not interfere with treatment, and there were no significant differences in adverse events between the groups (2 cases of superficial burns in the treatment group; 1 case of nausea and 1 case of constipation in the control group).

While the benefit of moxibustion appeared valid, the overall level of evidence was rated as low, meaning that there was limited confidence in the effect estimate, and the actual effect may differ substantially. Given the lack of significant differences in adverse events, the risk of adverse effects was low. Considering its potential benefits and clinical applicability, a grade C recommendation was assigned.

To improve the symptoms of pediatric patients with anorexia, acupoint herbal patching may be considered rather than conventional medicine (R4, grade of recommendation: C; level of evidence: Low).

This recommendation was based on a meta-analysis of five RCTs. The group receiving herbal acupoint patching showed a 1.32 times higher TER than the conventional treatment group (RR 1.32, 95% CI [1.22, 1.43]), indicating greater improvement in anorexia symptoms. Adverse events were not significantly different between the groups, suggesting that acupoint herbal patching is a safe treatment (3 cases of skin itching in the treatment group; 3 cases of nausea and 1 case of constipation in the control group).

Although the benefits of acupoint herbal patching appear to be valid, the level of evidence was rated low, indicating limited confidence in the effect estimate and the possibility that the actual effect may differ substantially. The risk of harm was considered low owing to the lack of significant differences in adverse events. Although not yet widely used, it has potential for future clinical applications. Given its demonstrated benefits and moderate applicability, a grade C recommendation was assigned.

To improve the symptoms of pediatric patients with anorexia, acupressure may be considered rather than conventional medicine (R5, grade of recommendation: C; level of evidence: Low).

This recommendation was based on an analysis of one RCT. The acupressure group showed a 1.46 times higher TER than the conventional treatment group (RR 1.46, 95% CI [1.13, 1.89]), indicating greater improvement in anorexia symptoms. However, as no adverse events related to acupressure have been reported, its safety could not be conclusively evaluated.

While the potential benefit of acupressure appears valid, the level of evidence was rated as low and confidence in the effect estimate was limited, with the actual effect possibly differing substantially. Due to the absence of reported adverse events, the risk of harm remains unclear and warrants further investigation. Although not yet widely used in clinical practice, it has potential for future applications. Given the limited evidence, a grade C recommendation was assigned.

#### Chuna manual therapy

To improve the symptoms of pediatric patients with anorexia, Chuna manual therapy may be considered (R6, grade of recommendation: C; level of evidence: Low).

Although the level of evidence for the supporting sub-recommendations was rated as low, the expert panel, through the Delphi consensus, acknowledged the clinical applicability of Chuna manual therapy and supported its use. Consequently, a grade C recommendation, with a low level of evidence, was assigned.

To improve the symptoms of pediatric patients with anorexia, Chuna manual therapy may be considered rather than observation (R6-1, grade of recommendation: C; level of evidence: Low).

This recommendation was based on a meta-analysis of two RCTs. The group receiving Chuna manual therapy showed a 1.27 times higher TER than the waiting list group (RR 1.27, 95% CI [1.14, 1.41]), suggesting a greater improvement in anorexia symptoms. Although the recurrence rate was lower in the treatment group, the relative risk did not differ significantly. No studies have reported adverse events related to Chuna manual therapy; therefore, its safety could not be conclusively evaluated.

While the benefits of Chuna manual therapy appear clear, the overall level of evidence was rated low, indicating limited confidence in the effect estimate and the possibility that the actual effect may differ substantially. Although not yet widely used in clinical practice, Chuna manual therapy has the potential for broader application. Therefore, a grade C recommendation was assigned.

To improve the symptoms of pediatric patients with anorexia, Chuna manual therapy should be considered rather than conventional medicine (R6-2, grade of recommendation: C; level of evidence: Moderate).

This recommendation is based on a meta-analysis of 24 RCTs. The group receiving Chuna manual therapy showed 1.24 times higher TER (RR 1.24, 95% CI [1.15, 1.32]) and a 3.37-minute reduction in GHET (MD − 3.37, 95% CI [− 5.76, − 0.98]) compared to the conventional treatment group, suggesting greater improvement in anorexia symptoms. No significant differences were observed in recurrence rate. No adverse events were reported in the Chuna manual therapy group, indicating that it is a safe treatment.

Chuna manual therapy demonstrated a clear benefit compared to conventional medicine, and the overall level of evidence was rated as moderate. Therefore, confidence in the effect estimate is moderate, meaning that the actual effect is likely to be close to the estimate but may differ. The risk of harm was considered low in the absence of any reported adverse events. Although Chuna manual therapy is not yet widely used for anorexia in current clinical settings, it has the potential for broader applications. Considering these factors, a grade B recommendation was assigned.

### Combined Korean medicine treatments

#### Herbal medicine and acupuncture

To improve the symptoms of pediatric patients with anorexia, the combination of herbal medicine and acupuncture therapy may be considered (R7, grade of recommendation: C; level of evidence: Low).

Studies have shown that the combined use of herbal medicine and acupuncture produces significantly better outcomes than the waiting list and conventional medicine. Based on the Delphi consensus among the expert panel members, the level of evidence was rated as low. However, considering its high applicability in clinical practice, the recommendation was ultimately assigned a grade C with a low level of evidence.

To improve the symptoms of pediatric patients with anorexia, the combination of herbal medicine and acupuncture therapy may be considered rather than observation (R7-1, grade of recommendation: C; level of evidence: Low).

This recommendation is based on a single RCT. The group receiving both herbal medicine and acupuncture showed a 0.60 cm increase in ASF compared with the waiting list group (MD 0.60, 95% CI [0.58, 0.62]), indicating greater improvement in anorexia symptoms. Since no adverse events were reported, the safety of this treatment could not be conclusively assessed.

While the benefit of combined herbal medicine and acupuncture appears valid, the overall level of evidence was rated as low, meaning that confidence in the effect estimate was limited, and the actual effect may differ. Due to the absence of reported adverse events, the risk of harm remains unclear, and further research is needed. Given its demonstrated benefits and high clinical applicability, a grade C recommendation was assigned.

To improve the symptoms of pediatric patients with anorexia, the combination of herbal medicine and acupuncture therapy should be considered rather than conventional medicine (R7-2, grade of recommendation: B; level of evidence: Moderate).

This recommendation was based on a meta-analysis of 12 RCTs, including a sub-recommendation (R7-2-1) involving the use of Samryeongbaekchulsan (Shenlingbaizhu-san).

The group receiving both herbal medicine and acupuncture showed a 1.20 times higher TER (RR 1.20, 95% CI [1.14, 1.26]) and a 1.28-minute shorter GHET (MD − 1.28, 95% CI [− 1.35, − 1.21]) than the conventional treatment group, indicating greater improvement in anorexia symptoms. Fewer adverse events were reported in the treatment group than in the control group, supporting the conclusion that it is a safe treatment (2 cases in the treatment group: vomiting [1] and fatigue [1]; 10 cases in the control group: vomiting [2], diarrhea [4], and fatigue [4]).

The combination of herbal medicine and acupuncture demonstrated clear benefits, with a moderate level of evidence supporting moderate confidence in the effect estimate. Although the actual effect is likely to be close to the estimate, it may differ. Given the lower incidence of adverse events and the high clinical applicability, a grade B recommendation was assigned.

To improve the symptoms of pediatric patients with anorexia, the combination of herbal medicine and pharmacopuncture may be considered rather than conventional medicine (R7-3, grade of recommendation: C; level of evidence: Low).

This recommendation is based on a single RCT. The TER of the group receiving herbal medicine and pharmacopuncture was 1.17 times higher than that of the conventional treatment group (RR 1.17, 95% CI [1.01, 1.36]), suggesting greater improvement in anorexia symptoms. However, as no study has reported adverse events related to this combined treatment, its safety could not be assessed.

Although the benefit of combining herbal medicine and pharmacopuncture appears to be valid, the overall level of evidence was rated low, indicating limited confidence in the effect estimate and the possibility that the actual effect may differ. According to a 2021 survey, herbal medicine was frequently used (97.7%) in Korean medicine to treat pediatric anorexia, whereas pharmacopuncture was less commonly applied. Therefore, the clinical applicability of this combination was considered moderate. Considering these factors, a grade C recommendation was assigned.

### Combined Korean and conventional medicine treatments

#### Herbal medicine and conventional medicine

To improve the symptoms of pediatric patients with anorexia receiving conventional medicine, herbal medicine should be considered (R11, grade of recommendation: B; level of evidence: Moderate).

This recommendation is based on a meta-analysis of 71 RCTs, including studies utilizing representative prescriptions based on pattern identification: Igongsan (Yigong-san) (R11-1), Sagunjatang (Sijunzi-tang) (R11-2), and Samryeongbaekchulsan (Shenlingbaizhu-san) (R11-3) for the “stomach qi deficiency” pattern; Yangwijeungaektang (Yangweizengye-tang) (R11-4) for the “stomach yin deficiency” pattern; Geonbihwan (Jianpi-wan) (R11-5), Sahwangsan (Xiehuang-san) (R11-6), Gokmaekjichultang (Qumaizhizhuzhu-tang) (R11-7), and Hyanggyulhwan (Xiangju-wan) (R11-8) for the “food accumulation” pattern.

Compared to conventional medicine alone, the group receiving combined herbal and conventional medicine showed greater improvement in anorexia symptoms, including increases in weight by 1.34 kg (MD 1.34, 95% CI [1.03, 1.66]), weight gain by 0.3 kg (MD 0.3, 95% CI [0.27, 0.33]), ASF by 0.09 cm (MD 0.09, 95% CI [0.05, 0.13]), height by 4.97 cm (MD 4.97, 95% CI [0.81, 9.13]), BMI by 0.89 (MD 0.89, 95% CI [0.64, 1.13]), food intake by 37.96 g/day (MD 37.96, 95% CI [23.13, 52.78]), TER by 1.22 times (RR 1.22, 95% CI [1.18, 1.25]), and Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory 4.0 (PedsQL 4.0™) score by 4.04 points (MD 4.04, 95% CI [1.19, 6.89]). ART was reduced by 2.09 days (MD − 2.09, 95% CI [− 2.44, − 1.75]), and the recurrence rate was 0.33 times lower (RR 0.33, 95% CI [0.23, 0.48]). The incidence of adverse events was also lower in the treatment group, supporting the conclusion that the combination therapy is safe (10 cases in the treatment group: nausea [2], vomiting [2], diarrhea [3], constipation [2], and rash [1]; 17 cases in the control group: nausea [3], vomiting [4], diarrhea [5], constipation [1], abdominal pain [2], dizziness [1], and rash [1]).

The benefit of herbal medicine in combination with conventional medicine was clear, and the overall level of evidence was moderate, indicating moderate confidence in the effect estimate. Although the actual effect is likely close to the estimate, variations may exist. A lower incidence of adverse events also suggests a low risk of harm. Given its demonstrated benefits and high clinical applicability, a grade B recommendation was assigned.

#### Herbal medicine, acupuncture, and conventional medicine

To improve the symptoms of pediatric patients with anorexia receiving conventional medicine, the combination of herbal medicine and acupuncture therapy should be considered (R12, grade of recommendation: B; level of evidence: Moderate).

This recommendation was based on a meta-analysis of three RCTs, including one sub-recommendation (R12-1) involving the use of Samryeongbaekchulsan (Shenlingbaizhu-san).

The group receiving herbal medicine, acupuncture, and conventional medicine showed significantly greater improvement than the group receiving conventional treatment alone, with an increase in weight by 1.90 kg (MD 1.90, 95% CI [0.83, 2.97]), BMI by 2.64 (MD 2.64, 95% CI [1.84, 3.44]), and TER by 1.19 times (RR 1.19, 95% CI [1.08, 1.31]). No significant differences in adverse events were observed between the groups (1 case in each group), suggesting that the treatment was safe.

The combination of herbal medicine and acupuncture with conventional medicine demonstrated clear benefits with a moderate level of evidence and confidence in the effect estimate. Although the actual effect is likely close to the estimate, variations are possible. The risk of harm was considered low because of the comparable rates of adverse events. According to a 2021 survey, herbal medicines (97.7%) and acupuncture (54.9%) are frequently used in clinical practice to treat pediatric anorexia, indicating their high clinical applicability. Based on these factors, a grade B recommendation was assigned.

#### Acupuncture and conventional medicine

To improve the symptoms of pediatric patients with anorexia receiving conventional medicine, acupuncture therapy should be considered (R13, grade of recommendation: B; level of evidence: Moderate).

This recommendation was based on a meta-analysis of three RCTs. The group receiving acupuncture at Sifeng points along with conventional treatment showed no significant difference in TER compared to the conventional treatment group (RR 1.43, 95% CI [0.90, 2.28]). The incidence of adverse events was also not significantly different between the two groups (RR 1.00, 95% CI [0.07, 15.36]; 1 case of pain in the treatment group and 1 case of infection in the control group), suggesting that the treatment was safe.

Acupuncture combined with conventional medicine demonstrated no significant benefits with a moderate level of evidence and moderate confidence in the effect estimate. Although the actual effect was likely close to the estimate, some variation was possible. Considering the comparable incidence of adverse events, the risk of harm was considered low. According to a 2021 survey, acupuncture is the second most frequently used Korean medicine treatment for pediatric anorexia (54.9%), indicating its high clinical applicability. Acupuncture monotherapy demonstrates superior efficacy to conventional medicine alone (R2-2). Unless there is evidence of greater risk, there is no reason to avoid combined therapy. Based on these considerations, a grade B recommendation was assigned.

#### Acupoint herbal patching and conventional medicine

To improve the symptoms of pediatric patients with anorexia receiving conventional medicine, acupoint herbal patching may be considered (R14, grade of recommendation: C; level of evidence: Low).

This recommendation was based on a meta-analysis of four RCTs. The group receiving acupoint herbal patching in combination with conventional medicine showed a 1.20 times higher TER than the conventional treatment group (RR 1.20, 95% CI [1.11, 1.30]), indicating a greater improvement in anorexia symptoms. Recurrence rates were also significantly lower at both 6 months (RR 0.25, 95% CI [0.07, 0.84]) and 1 year (RR 0.36, 95% CI [0.17, 0.76]) after treatment. Adverse events did not significantly differ between the groups, suggesting that the treatment was safe (6 cases in the treatment group: skin allergy [3], nausea [1], diarrhea [1], skin erythema [1]; 10 cases in the control group: vomiting and other reactions [7], nausea [1], and diarrhea [2]).

Although the combination of acupoint herbal patching with conventional medicine appears to be beneficial, the overall level of evidence was rated as low, indicating limited confidence in the effect estimate and the possibility that the actual effect may differ. As no significant differences in adverse events were observed, the risk of harm was considered low. Although not yet commonly used in clinical practice, acupoint herbal patching may have increased applications in the future. Based on these factors, a grade C recommendation was assigned.

#### Chuna manual therapy and conventional medicine

To improve the symptoms of pediatric patients with anorexia receiving conventional medicine, Chuna manual therapy should be considered (R15, grade of recommendation: B; level of evidence: Moderate).

This recommendation was based on a meta-analysis of 11 RCTs. The group that received Chuna manual therapy along with conventional treatment showed greater improvement than the control group, which received conventional treatment alone. Specifically, weight increased by 1.06 kg (MD 1.06, 95% CI [0.61, 1.51]), height by 1.34 cm (MD 1.34, 95% CI [0.21, 2.47]), TER by 1.21 times (RR 1.21, 95% CI [1.15, 1.28]), while ART was 1.02 days shorter (MD − 1.02, 95% CI [− 1.89, − 0.15]) and GHET was reduced by 3.4 min (MD − 3.4, 95% CI [− 5.81, − 0.99]). The recurrence rate did not differ significantly between the two groups (RR 0.60, 95% CI [0.15, 2.34]). No adverse events were reported, indicating that Chuna manual therapy is a safe treatment.

The combination of Chuna manual therapy and conventional medicine demonstrated meaningful benefits with a moderate level of evidence and corresponding confidence in the effect estimate. Although the actual effect is likely close to the estimate, some variations may occur. Given the absence of reported adverse events, the risk of harm was considered low. Although Chuna manual therapy is not yet widely used for treating anorexia, its clinical applicability may expand in the future. Based on these considerations, a grade B recommendation was assigned.

## Discussion

Anorexia is a common pediatric symptom characterized by persistent loss of appetite, lack of interest in food, and in severe cases, active refusal to eat. It is often expressed by caregivers as “my child doesn’t eat well” and generally refers to a state in which the desire or motivation to consume food is reduced or absent.

Despite being a major concern for many parents, the study and clinical management of pediatric anorexia has been hampered by inconsistent terminology and diagnostic criteria that fail to clearly define its scope. Various terms have been used to describe this condition, including pediatric anorexia, infantile anorexia, picky eating, food refusal, food fussiness, food aversion, food phobia, feeding difficulties, feeding problems, and feeding disorders.

In Korean medicine, the underlying pathogenesis is referred to as pattern identification (辨證), and pediatric anorexia is broadly classified into four major patterns: “stomach qi deficiency,” “stomach yin deficiency,” “food accumulation,” and “liver depression and spleen deficiency”. These guidelines present the corresponding Korean medicine treatments based on these representative pattern identifications.

According to a study by Cano et al., most preschool-aged children with anorexia show improvement by the age of six, and those with siblings generally have a more favorable prognosis. However, in a longitudinal study of children with ARFID, although the severity of malnutrition steadily improved across four assessment points (ages 2, 5, 7, and 11), 73% of the children continued to experience mild, moderate, or severe malnutrition, even at age 11 years.

Currently, there is no established treatment for anorexia in children without organic diseases, as temporary anorexia is generally considered self-limiting and is not a target for active intervention. Nevertheless, demand for treatment remains high, with pediatric anorexia accounting for 72.2% of pediatric visits to Korean medicine hospitals in Korea. In this context, the development of a KM-CPG for pediatric anorexia is meaningful because it reinforces the credibility and professionalism of Korean medicine and systematically demonstrates its safety and effectiveness. Continued efforts to validate these outcomes are expected to contribute to public healthcare through Korean medicine.

Despite the TER for acupuncture combined with conventional medicine not being significantly greater than that for conventional medicine alone, a Grade B recommendation was assigned to R13 as a result of consensus. This recommendation was based on the evidence from R2-2, which showed that acupuncture monotherapy has greater efficacy than conventional medicine monotherapy. The consensus concluded that it is difficult to assume the additional benefit of acupuncture is diminished simply because a patient is already receiving conventional medicine. The recommendation level also considered the fact that there were three supporting studies for R13 compared to ten for R2-2. This is an area that requires further research.

This CPG aims to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of Korean medical treatments for pediatric anorexia and offer corresponding clinical recommendations. However, this study has several limitations must be acknowledged. First, an objective evidence-based literature on Korean medical clinical techniques is lacking. The quantity and quality of clinical studies in Korean medicine remain limited, making it difficult to formulate strong recommendations for specific interventions. Further discussion is required to identify areas for future evidence generation through a systematic review and additional research. Second, the overall quality of the included studies was low. In particular, many studies did not implement blinding procedures, raising the risk of bias and suggesting that caution is warranted when interpreting the results. Third, one of the core principles of Korean medicine—individualized treatment based on constitution and specificity— has not been adequately reflected in most studies. This may have limited the incorporation of the unique diagnostic and therapeutic frameworks of Korean medicine. Fourth, the guidelines may not fully reflect the current clinical reality of pediatric anorexia treatment in South Korea. Due to the scarcity of domestic clinical data, most of the evidence included in this guideline was derived from studies conducted in China, highlighting the need to consider potential contextual differences. Finally, the recommendations presented in these guidelines do not encompass all possible Korean medical treatment modalities. Not all practitioners agree with every recommendation derived from the clinical studies. Thus, treatment should be flexibly tailored to individual patient characteristics and clinical circumstances, with final decisions left to the discretion of the clinician.

To date, no studies have evaluated the cost-effectiveness of Korean medical treatments included in the consensus recommendations. Therefore, cost considerations are excluded from the present guidelines, and future research on cost-effectiveness is warranted. Additionally, to clearly distinguish between representative and non-representative prescriptions within specific pattern-identification types, further studies comparing the effectiveness of different prescriptions and examining treatment outcomes based on pattern identification in Korean medicine are required.

These clinical practice guidelines will be reviewed every five years by the Clinical Practice Guideline Evaluation Committee of the Association of Pediatrics of Korean Medicine, starting in 2023. The need for updates was assessed using predefined search strategies. If new scientific evidence emerges after publication or if user feedback indicates a need to revise current recommendations based on preferences or clinical discrepancies, an updated literature search will be conducted. The newly collected evidence is then synthesized and analyzed to revise the level of evidence and recommendation grades accordingly.

## Conclusions

The KM-CPG for pediatric anorexia systematically reviews the effectiveness and safety of Korean medical treatments for pediatric anorexia, including herbal medicine, acupuncture, Chuna manual therapy, and acupoint herbal patching, and explores their potential integration with conventional medicines. This guideline may serve as a foundational reference for future research aimed at strengthening the evidence base for Korean medicine interventions and will be updated when new evidence emerges or when the development of new recommendations becomes necessary.
