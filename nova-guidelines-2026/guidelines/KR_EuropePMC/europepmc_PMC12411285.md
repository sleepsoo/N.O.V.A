# Tenecteplase in Acute Ischemic Stroke: A Scientific Statement From the Korean Stroke Society

## Abstract

### Background and Purpose

Tenecteplase (TNK) is a promising alternative thrombolytic agent for the treatment of acute ischemic stroke (AIS). However, its potential use is being impeded by the lack of regulatory approval and reimbursement policies for TNK in AIS in many countries, including South Korea. To address this therapeutic gap, the Korean Stroke Society developed scientific statement intended to inform policy changes and support the introduction of TNK in regions where it is not yet accessible, with the aim of enabling AIS patients to benefit from this advancement in thrombolytic therapy.

### Methods

We reviewed randomized controlled trials (RCTs), meta-analyses, and systematic reviews published between January 2010 and November 2024 involving AIS patients treated with intravenous TNK. Meta-analyses were included if they exclusively evaluated RCTs and provided clinical evidence on the efficacy and safety of TNK. The statements were thoroughly reviewed and finalized by international expert panels after iterative revisions.

### Results

The statements suggest that TNK at 0.25 mg/kg can be considered as an alternative to alteplase for intravenous thrombolysis within 4.5 hours of the onset of AIS. The clinical outcomes in patients with large-vessel occlusion who are candidates for endovascular thrombectomy are better for TNK at 0.25 mg/kg than for alteplase.

### Conclusions

These statements are intended to support the adoption of TNK in countries where it is not yet available, including South Korea, by providing up-to-date clinical evidence. Their implementation may broaden the therapeutic options for AIS patients and help align acute stroke care practices with international standards.

## INTRODUCTION

Recanalization therapy is a cornerstone in the management of acute ischemic stroke (AIS) that can restore the cerebral perfusion and mitigate brain injury. Intravenous (IV) thrombolysis with the recombinant tissue plasmin activator alteplase has for a long time been the gold-standard treatment for patients presenting within 4.5 hours of the onset of ischemic stroke. In South Korea, approximately 10% of AIS patients receive IV thrombolysis, a proportion that has been steadily increasing due to greater public awareness of stroke symptoms, improvements in prehospital triage systems, and advances in hospital stroke care. As healthcare systems strive to optimize the efficiency and effectiveness of acute stroke management, the increasing demand for IV thrombolysis will lead to it playing a critical role in acute stroke care.

While alteplase continues to be the primary thrombolytic agent for AIS in many countries, including South Korea, its efficacy remains inadequate, especially in cases involving large-artery occlusion (LVO), for which recanalization rates remain below 40%. Additionally, alteplase is associated with a significant risk of hemorrhage, and its short half-life interferes with its efficacy. Tenecteplase (TNK) is a genetically modified recombinant tissue plasminogen activator that offers several advantages over alteplase, including higher fibrin specificity, a longer half-life, and being administered as a single IV bolus. Recent studies have suggested that TNK not only produces reperfusion rates superior to those for alteplase, but that it also has a comparative safety profile. The ease of administration of TNK is particularly advantageous in emergency department settings, since this can potentially reduce the time to treatment. This may also reduce delays—especially in patients requiring mechanical thrombectomy—by facilitating faster intrahospital and interhospital transfers due to a reduced need for advanced paramedic care since no IV thrombolytic infusion is required at the time of transport.

Based on its advantages over alteplase, TNK was approved for use in patients with AIS by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration and in most European countries in 2024, and is now actively replacing alteplase in many clinical settings. However, TNK has not yet been approved for stroke treatment in many countries, including South Korea, and off-label use is also not currently feasible. This disparity between TNK-available countries and TNK-unavailable countries (including South Korea) underscores a critical gap in acute stroke care. To address this issue, the Korean Stroke Society (KSS) is actively advocating for evidence-based discussions on the potential of TNK in AIS management. Drawing on key clinical studies, the KSS seeks to provide updated recommendations to inform policy changes and support the introduction of TNK into South Korea’s healthcare system, to allow AIS patients to benefit from this advancement in thrombolytic therapy.

## METHODS

### Search strategy

We included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) published between January 2010 and November 2024 involving AIS patients treated with IV TNK. The PubMed database was systematically searched for studies involving TNK (search expression: “tenecteplase” OR “TNK”), IV administration (“intravenous infusions” OR “IV infusion”), and AIS-related conditions (“stroke” OR “ischemic stroke” OR “brain ischemia” OR “intracranial thrombosis” OR “carotid stenosis”), with restrictions to RCTs, human studies, and English-language publications. This search identified 51 articles, to which the following exclusion criteria were applied: trials conducted in contexts other than AIS, studies focused on pharmacological mechanisms or imaging analyses without clinical outcomes being reported, articles discussing the rationale or reviewing major trials, or substudies of major trials, which were included only if deemed relevant to the review topic by the review committee. When a trial expanded upon an earlier study with a primary focus on clinical outcomes and encompassed the implications of the prior study, only the later trial was included in the analysis. Meta-analyses were included if they exclusively analyzed RCTs and provided clinical evidence regarding the efficacy of TNK. A random-effects model was used for the meta-analysis, and publication bias was assessed using a funnel plot and Egger’s test. Inclusion was further restricted to studies that addressed topics corresponding to the subsections covered in this review and were deemed to have included an adequate number of RCTs, as determined by the committee. Two investigators (H. Kim and J.-T. Kim) used this process to systematically select a final pool of high-quality articles to utilize for the review and synthesis in this study. Disagreements about the finally selected articles were reconciled via consensus agreement between these two investigators.

## RESULTS

### Clinical evidence regarding the efficacy of TNK relative to alteplase in AIS

• Recommendation 1: In patients with AIS presenting within 4.5 hours of symptom onset, TNK at 0.25 mg/kg is superior to alteplase at 0.9 mg/kg in achieving an excellent functional outcome (score on the modified Rankin Scale [mRS] of 0 or 1) and reduced disability at 3 months, with potential advantages in reperfusion rates and a similar safety profile in terms of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH) and mortality.

• Recommendation 2: Administration of TNK at 0.4 mg/kg should be avoided in patients with AIS, since there is evidence of a higher risk of hemorrhagic complications resulting in worse functional outcomes.

The above conclusions are supported by strong evidence from multiple large-scale RCTs that systematically compared the efficacy and safety of TNK and alteplase in AIS. The potential of TNK to achieve equal or superior outcomes in vascular reperfusion and functional recovery has been consistently demonstrated in key studies, such as the Australian TNK trial, NOR-TEST (Tenecteplase versus Alteplase for the Management of Acute Ischemic Stroke in Norway) trial, AcT (Alteplase Compared to Tenecteplase in Patients With Acute Ischemic Stroke) trial, TRACE-2 (Tenecteplase Reperfusion therapy in Acute ischemic Cerebrovascular Events-2) trial, ATTEST-2 (Tenecteplase versus alteplase for acute stroke within 4.5 h of onset) trial, TASTE (Tenecteplase versus Alteplase for Stroke Thrombolysis Evaluation) trial, and ORIGINAL (Tenecteplase versus Alteplase for Acute Ischemic Stroke in China) trial. Table 1 presents a comparison of TNK and alteplase based on these trials.

To date, the following eight large-scale RCTs have directly compared the use of TNK and alteplase for IV thrombolysis in AIS:

1) Australian TNK trial: This study compared the reperfusion rates and neurological improvement at 24 hours between two doses of TNK (0.25 mg/kg and 0.1 mg/kg) and one dose of alteplase (0.9 mg/kg) in patients treated within 6 hours of stroke onset. The reperfusion rate at 24 hours was significantly higher in the TNK group (79.3% vs. 55.4%, p=0.004), which also showed a greater improvement in the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score between baseline and 24 hours (8 points vs. 3 points, p<0.001), suggesting that TNK is more effective for promoting vascular reperfusion and neurological recovery.

2) NOR-TEST and NOR-TEST 2 trials: This trial compared the outcomes between using a higher dose of TNK (0.4 mg/kg) and alteplase in AIS. The study found no significant differences in functional outcomes (mRS score=0 or 1 at 3 months) or sICH rates, highlighting comparable safety and efficacy. However, this study included patients with relatively mild ischemic stroke (median NIHSS score=4, interquartile range=2–8). Since patients with minor ischemic stroke are likely to experience favorable outcomes regardless of the intervention, the subsequent NOR-TEST 2 trial was conducted targeting patients with moderate-to-severe stroke. In patients with moderate-to-severe AIS (NIHSS score ≥6), 0.4 mg/kg TNK was associated with worse functional outcomes than for alteplase (mRS score=0 or 1 at 3 months: 32% vs. 51%; odds ratio [OR]=0.45, 95% confidence interval [CI]=0.25–0.80, p=0.006) and a higher incidence of sICH (6% vs. 1%; OR=3.68, 95% CI=1.49–9.11, p=0.003).

3) AcT trial: This study compared 0.25 mg/kg TNK with 0.9 mg/kg alteplase in patients eligible for thrombolysis within 4.5 hours of symptom onset. The primary endpoint of achieving an mRS score of 0 or 1 at 3 months was reached by 36.9% and 34.8% of those in the TNK and alteplase groups, respectively, confirming the noninferiority of TNK. Safety profiles were comparable between the two treatments.

4) TRACE-2 trial: This study confirmed the noninferiority of administering TNK relative to alteplase in AIS patients with NIHSS scores ≥5 within 4.5 hours of onset who were not candidates for endovascular thrombectomy (EVT). An mRS score of 0 or 1 at 3 months was achieved in 62% of those in the TNK group and 58% of those in the alteplase group (risk ratio [RR]=1.07, 95% CI=0.98–1.16), which exceeded the noninferiority threshold.

5) ATTEST-2 trial: This randomized, open-label study compared 0.25 mg/kg TNK with 0.9 mg/kg alteplase in 1,777 AIS patients. TNK demonstrated noninferiority for the mRS score distribution at 90 days (OR=1.07, 95% CI=0.90–1.27, noninferiority p<0.001), although superiority was not established.

6) TASTE trial: This recent trial examined 680 stroke patients with salvageable penumbral tissue evident in perfusion-weighted imaging who were not candidates for EVT. The per-protocol analysis showed that alteplase was noninferior to the comparator for favorable outcomes (mRS score=0 or 1 at 90 days; adjusted OR=1.27, 95% CI=0.90–1.81, p=0.01). Safety outcomes, including sICH and mortality, were similar for the treatments.

7) ORIGINAL trial: This recently reported study involved 1,465 AIS patients eligible for thrombolysis evaluated the noninferiority of 0.25 mg/kg TNK to alteplase. The proportion of patients with an excellent mRS score (0 or 1) at 3 months was higher for TNK than for alteplase (72.7% vs. 70.3%; RR=1.03, 95% CI=0.97–1.09), while the rates of sICH were similar in the two groups (1.2% vs. 1.2%; RR=1.01, 95% CI=0.37–2.70).

8) A meta-analysis of 11 RCTs involving 7,545 patients compared TNK and alteplase within 4.5 hours of symptom onset. TNK was superior to alteplase in achieving an excellent functional outcome at 3 months (mRS score=0 or 1; RR=1.05, 95% CI=1.01–1.10, p=0.012). Safety outcomes did not differ significantly between TNK and alteplase for sICH (RR=1.12, 95% CI=0.83–1.53, p=0.456) or 3-month mortality (RR=0.97, 95% CI=0.82–1.15, p=0.727).

We conducted a meta-analysis of the above-described eight major RCTs, which revealed that TNK was superior to alteplase in achieving an excellent functional outcome (RR=1.04, 95% CI=1.00–1.08, p=0.04) (Fig. 1). Egger’s test indicated that there was no significant publication bias (p=0.767).

### Administration of TNK compared with alteplase prior to mechanical thrombectomy

• Recommendation 3: In patients with AIS and LVO that is eligible for mechanical thrombectomy, TNK at 0.25 mg/kg is probably superior to alteplase at 0.9 mg/kg in achieving early recanalization and better 3-month functional outcomes when administered within 4.5 hours of symptom onset, and their safety profiles are similar.

Given the critical role of mechanical thrombectomy in stroke management and the complex considerations surrounding prethrombectomy thrombolysis, the EXTEND-IA TNK (Tenecteplase Versus Alteplase Before Endovascular Therapy for Ischemic Stroke) studies provide pivotal evidence supporting the use of TNK in this context (Table 2):

1) EXTEND-IA TNK part 1 study: This study compared TNK at 0.25 mg/kg and alteplase at 0.9 mg/kg in 202 patients with LVO eligible for both IV thrombolysis and EVT. The primary endpoint was good recanalization, defined as reperfusion of >50% of the ischemic lesion and the absence of thrombus in initial computed tomography angiography. TNK demonstrated superior recanalization (22% vs. 10%, p=0.03) and a better 3-month functional outcome (median mRS score: 2 vs. 3; OR=1.7, 95% CI=1.0–2.8, p=0.04). The sICH rates were similar in the two groups.

2) EXTEND-IA TNK part 2 study: This follow-up study compared two TNK dosing regimens (0.25 mg/kg vs. 0.4 mg/kg) in LVO patients eligible for both thrombolysis and thrombectomy. Substantial recanalization (>50% of the ischemic territory) rates (adjusted RR=1.03, 95% CI=0.66–1.61, p=0.89) and 3-month functional outcomes (Mrs score; OR=0.96, 95% CI=0.74–1.24, p=0.73) were similar for the two doses. However, the incidence of Sich was higher, though not statistically significant, in the 0.4 mg/kg group than in the 0.25 mg/kg group (4.7% vs. 1.3%; RR=3.50, 95% CI=0.74–16.62, p=0.12). These findings support the use of 0.25 mg/kg TNK as the preferred dose in patients with LVO due to its favorable safety and efficacy profile.

### Dose of TNK

• Recommendation 4: In patients eligible for IV thrombolysis within 4.5 hours of symptom onset, TNK at 0.25 mg/kg achieves functional outcomes and a safety profile that are superior to those for TNK at 0.1 mg/kg and 0.4 mg/kg.

Studies comparing the effects of different TNK doses have been based on trials such as the TNK-S2B (Phase 2B Study of TNK in Acute Ischemic Stroke), Australian TNK trial, and TEMPO-1 (TNK-tPA versus Standard of Care for Minor Ischemic Stroke with Proven Occlusion) trial. The TNK-S2B study demonstrated that low-dose TNK (0.1 mg/kg and 0.25 mg/kg) was more effective and safer than high-dose TNK (0.4 mg/kg). The Australian TNK and TEMPO-1 trials further suggested clinical benefits with a 0.25 mg/kg dose of TNK. In contrast, while they did not directly compare TNK doses, the NOR-TEST trials indicated that 0.4 mg/kg TNK offered no additional benefit over alteplase, and may even be harmful. Table 3 summarizes the findings related to TNK dosing:

1) TNK-S2B trial: This study compared three doses of TNK (0.1 mg/kg, 0.25 mg/kg, and 0.4 mg/kg) with alteplase (0.9 mg/kg) administered within 3 hours of symptom onset. The rate of neurological improvement at 24 hours was higher for TNK at 0.25 mg/kg (35.5%) than at other doses, while the rate of sICH was higher for the 0.4 mg/kg dose than for alteplase at 0.9 mg/kg (15.8% vs. 3.2%, respectively). The findings support 0.25 mg/kg as the optimal dose, but they were limited by early study termination due to enrollment challenges.

2) Australian TNK trial: This trial found that relative to a dose of 0.1 mg/kg, 0.25 mg/kg TNK demonstrated superior recanalization rates (88.8% vs. 69.3%, p=0.017) and greater improvement in NIHSS scores (11 vs. 7, p=0.006).

3) TEMPO-1 trial: This study assessed the feasibility and safety of TNK dose escalation from 0.1 mg/kg to 0.25 mg/kg in mild-AIS patients (NIHSS score ≤5) using prespecified safety criteria. The outcome was better for TNK at 0.25 mg/kg than at 0.1 mg/kg (mRS score=0 or 1: 76% vs. 56%), with one sICH event in the 0.25 mg/kg group that did not affect overall safety.

4) In a meta-analysis of 5,475 patients included in RCTs, TNK at 0.25 mg/kg showed superior efficacy, achieving a 45.9% rate of excellent functional recovery (Mrs score=0 or 1) at 3 months (OR=1.85, 95% credible interval [CrI]=1.44–2.37) relative to TNK at 0.4 mg/kg (OR=2.33, 95% CrI=1.38–3.94). TNK at 0.4 mg/kg was associated with the highest risk of Sich, being significantly higher than for TNK at 0.25 mg/kg (OR=7.41, 95% CrI=1.39–48.90). Another meta-analysis that included different RCTs found that TNK at 0.2 mg/kg significantly improved the rate of excellent functional outcomes at 3 months (Mrs score=0 or 1; RR=1.14, 95% CI=1.04–1.26). Different doses did not significantly affect the rates of recanalization, any intracranial hemorrhage, Sich, or mortality. TNK at 0.4 mg/kg showed inconsistent efficacy and less-favorable outcomes, supporting 0.25 mg/kg as the optimal dose for balancing efficacy and safety.

### Time window beyond 4.5 hours

• Recommendation 5: In patients with LVO presenting 4.5 to 24 hours after symptom onset and with salvageable penumbra in perfusion imaging, TNK at 0.25 mg/kg achieves better recanalization rates than standard care. In those ineligible for mechanical thrombectomy, TNK may improve functional outcomes, but it may also be associated with an increased risk of sICH.

TNK is indicated for use within the same 4.5-hour window as general IV thrombolysis. Alteplase can be extended up to 9 hours when guided by perfusion imaging, and evidence supporting the use of TNK within this extended time window (4.5-24 hours) is emerging. Recent trials, including the TIMELESS (Thrombolysis in Imaging Eligible, Late Window Patients to Assess the Efficacy and Safety of Tenecteplase) trial and the TRACE-III (Tenecteplase Reperfusion Therapy in Acute Ischemic Cerebrovascular Events-III) trial, have evaluated TNK within this extended time window. These studies highlighted both its potential and the need for careful patient selection. Table 3 summarizes the findings in this patient population, while Table 4 summarizes the research on time windows extending beyond 4.5 hours:

1) TIMELESS trial: This study enrolled patients with AIS presenting at 4.5 to 24 hours after symptom onset who had LVO and salvageable tissue identified in perfusion imaging. The 24-hour recanalization rate was higher for TNK than in the controls (76.7% vs. 63.9%, adjusted OR=1.89), while there was no significant improvement in 3-month functional recovery (median mRS score of 3 in both groups, p=0.45), and mortality rates were similar (19.7% for TNK vs. 18.2% in controls). In the present study, it is noteworthy that 77.3% of patients underwent EVT, with the procedure initiated at a median of 15 minutes following TNK administration, allowing only a brief window for the thrombolytic agent to exert its effects.

2) TRACE-III trial: This study assessed the efficacy and safety of TNK at 0.25 mg/kg in 516 AIS patients (at 4.5-24 hours from onset) with LVO and ischemic penumbra, but who were ineligible for thrombectomy. TNK significantly improved the 3-month functional outcome (mRS score=0 or 1: RR=1.37, 95% CI=1.04–1.81, p=0.03), reperfusion rate (20.1% vs. 11.8%; RR=1.70, 95% CI=1.10–2.64), and neurological outcome (16.0% vs. 6.0%; RR=2.66, 95% CI=1.51–4.69). However, TNK was associated with a 3.8-fold higher risk of sICH (RR=3.82, 95% CI=0.82–17.87).

### Special considerations

As discussed above, ongoing research has aimed to determine the usefulness of TNK in various clinical scenarios. The situations where the evidence is currently insufficient are described below, with further studies being needed to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of TNK. The findings are summarized in Table 5.

#### Mild ischemic stroke

The TEMPO-2 trial investigated the use of TNK in patients with minor ischemic stroke (NIHSS score=0–5) presenting within 12 hours of symptom onset, with evidence of either occlusion of an intracranial vessel and/or a regional perfusion defect. The study compared outcomes between the TNK group and a nonthrombolysis group. TNK administration did not result in an improved functional outcome at 3 months (RR=0.96, 95% CI=0.88–1.04, p=0.29) and was associated with a higher risk of sICH (RR=4.2, 95% CI=0.9–19.7, p=0.059). The NOR-TEST trial was a large-scale study that compared TNK and alteplase in a cohort of 1,100 patients with a mean NIHSS score of 4, where >70% of the patients presented with mild ischemic stroke. The proportion of patients achieving a favorable functional outcome (mRS score=0 or 1 at 3 months) was comparable in the TNK and alteplase groups (64% vs. 63%; OR=1.08, 95% CI=0.84–1.38, p=0.52). A substudy of the AcT trial focusing on patients with minor ischemic stroke (NIHSS score ≤5) also found no significant difference in the likelihood of achieving an mRS score of 0 or 1 at 3 months (RR=1.14, 95% CI=0.92–1.40).

The current evidence supports that administering TNK at a dose of 0.25 mg/kg to patients with nondisabling minor ischemic stroke who are eligible for thrombolysis offers no benefit in terms of reperfusion rates or functional outcomes over using either alteplase or nonthrombolytic treatments, including antiplatelet therapy. Alteplase has not demonstrated any benefit over nonthrombolytic treatments including antiplatelet therapy in nondisabling minor stroke.

#### Wake-Up stroke and stroke with unclear onset time

The current evidence for patients with wake-up stroke (WUS) who are eligible for thrombolysis is insufficient for establishing a clear therapeutic benefit. The TWIST trial (Tenecteplase in Wake-Up Ischemic Stroke Trial) included 578 patients with WUS and an NIHSS score ≥3. The trial evaluated whether 0.25 mg/kg TNK produced excellent functional outcomes (Mrs score=0 or 1) at 3 months that were better than those for nonthrombolytic treatment. The results indicated that TNK did not significantly improve the probability of achieving an excellent functional outcome at 3 months (adjusted OR=1.18, 95% CI=0.88–1.58, p=0.27). Additionally, TNK was not associated with an increase in mortality (adjusted hazard ratio=1.29, 95% CI=0.74–2.26, p=0.37) or Sich (adjusted OR=2.17, 95% CI=0.53–8.87, p=0.28) relative to the control group.

### Current TNK guidelines in some countries

The overall clinical evidence regarding the effectiveness and safety of TNK has prompted many countries worldwide to establish guidelines for its use and incorporate it into clinical practices. For example, the European Stroke Organization (ESO) guidelines and the Canadian guidelines recommend 0.25 mg/kg TNK as an alternative to alteplase for thrombolysis within 4.5 hours of onset, suggesting that it has comparable effectiveness and safety (moderate-quality evidence, strong recommendation). Notably, the Australian and New Zealand guidelines emphasize the clinical benefits of TNK, recommending 0.25 mg/kg TNK as the preferred thrombolytic agent, with alteplase suggested only when TNK cannot be used (strong recommendation).

Using 0.25 mg/kg TNK rather than alteplase is recommended for patients with LVO in both the ESO guidelines (moderate-quality evidence, strong recommendation) and the Australian and New Zealand guidelines (strong recommendation). The American Heart Association/American Stroke Association (AHA/ASA) guidelines also highlight the superiority of TNK, recommending a dose of 0.25 mg/kg for patients scheduled for mechanical thrombectomy (class of recommendation [COR] IIb, level of evidence [LOE] B-R).

The AHA/ASA guidelines suggest the use of a high TNK dose of 0.4 mg/kg only in cases of minor ischemic stroke or in patients without LVO (COR IIb, LOE B-R). In contrast, the European guidelines strongly advise against the use of 0.4 mg/kg TNK regardless of clinical criteria (low-quality evidence, strong recommendation against intervention). While the Canadian guidelines do not mention the 0.4 mg/kg dose, the practical information in the Australian and New Zealand guidelines advise against using doses higher than 0.25 mg/kg.

The recommended time window for the use of TNK is generally within 4.5 hours of onset, as outlined in IV thrombolysis guidelines. In AIS patients whose last known well time is unclear, the ESO guidelines suggest that there is insufficient evidence to support the use of TNK (low evidence, strong recommendation against intervention). However, the Australian and New Zealand guidelines have recently been updated to include a strong recommendation at 4.5-9 hours or for WUS, and a weak recommendation at 9–24 hours. ## DISCUSSION

Alteplase is currently the only available IV thrombolytic agent for treating AIS in many countries including South Korea. However, TNK is a promising alternative option based on its demonstrated clinical efficacy and safety. The KSS recommends that TNK at 0.25 mg/kg be considered as an alternative option for IV thrombolysis in patients with AIS, particularly within 4.5 hours of symptom onset. In patients with LVO requiring EVT, TNK at 0.25 mg/kg has demonstrated superiority over alteplase in terms of clinical outcomes. Additionally, TNK at 0.25 mg/kg is considered the most-appropriate dose based on both clinical efficacy and safety. For cases where salvageable penumbra tissue is confirmed in perfusion imaging at 4.5-24 hours after symptom onset and EVT is not feasible, TNK at 0.25 mg/kg may be considered a potential treatment option. However, evidence supporting the use of TNK for minor ischemic stroke or WUS with unknown onset time remains inadequate, warranting further investigations.

Recent studies have broadened the potential scope of applying TNK to diverse patient populations, such as those beyond the 4.5-hour therapeutic window who were previously excluded from thrombolytic treatment. These and other clinical findings have led to TNK being increasingly recognized as a primary option for IV thrombolysis in international guidelines. Consequently, there is substantial need to introduce TNK in countries where it is not yet available, including South Korea, to enhance the treatment options for AIS patients and align acute stroke care with global advancements in the field.
