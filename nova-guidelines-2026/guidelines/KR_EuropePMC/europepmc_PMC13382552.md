# Korean Colorectal Cancer Screening Guidelines for Asymptomatic, Average-Risk Adults: The 2025 Revision

## Abstract

### Purpose

This study aimed to develop the 2025 update to the Korean colorectal cancer (CRC) screening guidelines by systematically assessing recent evidence, integrating domestic data, and addressing changes since the 2015 guideline revision, and accordingly, provide an evidence-based standard for clinicians and policymakers.

### Materials and Methods

A multidisciplinary committee developed the guidelines using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation methodology. The process involved establishing three key questions focused on efficacy, accuracy, and optimal age and interval for screening. A systematic review of international guidelines and primary literature (327 studies included) was conducted. A utility-based analysis using the Markov model was also performed to determine optimal screening ages and intervals.

### Results

The review identified high-certainty evidence for fecal immunochemical test (FIT) in reducing CRC mortality and moderatecertainty evidence for colonoscopy. Evidence for computed tomography colonography (CTC) and stool DNA testing showed very low certainty. Based on this synthesis and cost-utility analysis, the committee conditionally recommends screening for asymptomatic, average-risk adults aged 45-74 years using either colonoscopy every 10 years or FIT every 1-2 years. CTC and stool DNA testing were not recommended owing to insufficient evidence.

### Conclusion

The 2025 Korean Guidelines for Colorectal Cancer Screening provide the latest evidence-based recommendations tailored to the domestic context. By conditionally adopting both colonoscopy and FIT for individuals aged 45-74 years, these guidelines aim to optimize public health outcomes and reduce the colorectal cancer burden in South Korea.

## Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) represents a significant public health challenge in South Korea. According to the 2024 National Cancer Registry statistics, CRC was the second most common cancer diagnosed in 2022, with 33,158 new cases, accounting for 11.8% of all cancer incidences in South Korea. While the age-standardized incidence rate showed a decreasing trend from 2010 to 2020, it has been rising again at an average annual rate of 5.5% since 2020 and continues to show an upward trajectory in the recently updated 2023 statistics. In 2023, CRC was the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths for men and the second, for women. However, South Korea has achieved a 5-year relative survival rate of 74.6% for CRC, which is notably higher than the rates reported in the United States, the United Kingdom, and Japan, underscoring the effectiveness of early detection and advanced treatment.

The Korean National Cancer Screening Program (NCSP) for CRC was introduced in 2004, based on guidelines developed in 2002. The program currently recommends annual screening with a fecal immunochemical test (FIT) for all adults aged ≥ 50 years. Under the two-step system, individuals with a positive FIT result are advised to undergo a followup colonoscopy for definitive diagnosis. The previous major guideline revision in 2015 recommended FIT for adults aged 45-80 years and suggested colonoscopy as a selective option. To supplement this framework and evaluate the feasibility of primary screening with colonoscopy, a large-scale national pilot program was conducted from 2019 to March 2024, yielding significant domestic data.

Internationally, a significant shift has been observed in CRC screening recommendations. Leading organizations, including the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) and American College of Gastroenterology (ACG), have recently lowered the recommended age to begin screening from 50 to 45 for asymptomatic, average-risk individuals, citing the rising incidence of early-onset CRC. USPSTF gives a grade A recommendation for screening in adults aged 50-75 years and a grade B recommendation for those aged 45-49 years. These international guidelines endorse a multimodal approach, with colonoscopy every 10 years and annual FIT being the primary recommended strategies, providing patients and clinicians with multiple effective options.

The 2015 Korean guidelines are due for revision for several reasons. Significant changes have occurred in the domestic medical landscape, including increased accessibility and safety of colonoscopies, alongside advancements in various screening technologies. Furthermore, a substantial body of recent medical evidence regarding the efficacy, benefits, and harms of different screening modalities has accumulated globally over the past decade. In addition, the completion of the national colonoscopy pilot program has provided crucial real-world Korean data, necessitating a systematic re-evaluation of the current screening strategy to ensure it aligns with the latest evidence and national healthcare context.

The primary objective of this guideline revision is to provide an updated, evidence-based standard for CRC screening to clinicians, public health professionals, and policymakers in South Korea. By systematically reviewing recent evidence, this revision aims to offer clear and precise recommendations on the optimal screening methods, age ranges, and screening intervals. Eventually, these guidelines are intended to serve as foundational, evidence-based data for shaping future national cancer screening policies, improving public health outcomes, and reducing the burden of CRC in the nation.

## Materials and Methods

### 1. Guideline development process and committee composition

This clinical guideline was developed by the Colorectal Cancer Screening Guideline Revision Committee, under the supervision of the National Cancer Center of Korea. The committee was multidisciplinary, consisting of experts recommended by prominent domestic professional societies, including the Korean Academy of Family Medicine, Korean Association of Internal Medicine, Korean Society for Laboratory Medicine, Korean Society for Preventive Medicine, Korean Society of Pathologists, Korean Society of Radiology, and Korean Surgical Society. The committee was tasked with revising the 2015 Colorectal Cancer Screening Guideline to consider up-to-date medical evidence and changes in the domestic healthcare landscape. The development process adhered to the internationally recognized Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) methodology to ensure a systematic and transparent approach.

### 2. Formulation of key questions and determination of outcomes

The committee established the scope of the guideline using the PIPOH framework: Population (asymptomatic and average-risk adults), Intervention (colonoscopy, FIT, guaiac fecal occult blood test [gFOBT], computed tomography colonography [CTC], and stool DNA test), Professional (all physicians, healthcare providers, and policymakers), Outcome (benefits, accuracy, harms), and Health setting (primary, secondary, and tertiary medical institutions). Based on this scope, three key questions (KQs) were formulated to address the efficacy (KQ1), diagnostic accuracy (KQ2), and optimal screening interval and age (KQ3) for each screening modality. The committee used a Delphi method to rate the importance of various outcome variables, identifying colorectal cancer-specific mortality and incidence as critical outcomes for the evidence review.

(1) Key question 1 (KQ1): In asymptomatic, average-risk adults, do the following screening interventions (colonoscopy, FIT, gFOBT, stool DNA test, and CTC) reduce CRC-specific mortality or incidence compared to no screening?

(2) Key question 2 (KQ2): In asymptomatic, average-risk adults, what is the diagnostic accuracy (i.e., sensitivity and specificity) of the screening interventions for detecting colorectal cancer?

(3) Key question 3 (KQ3): In asymptomatic, average-risk adults, what are the effective screening intervals and target age ranges for the screening interventions?

### 3. Systematic review and literature search

The evidence review process began with a systematic search for existing high-quality clinical practice guidelines published in October 2013 (literature search date for the previous guideline). Databases such as PubMed, Guideline International Network (GIN), and the USPSTF (S1 Table). After assessing the quality of 11 selected guidelines using the AGREE II tool, the 2021 USPSTF recommendation statement was chosen as the foundational evidence base. Subsequently, an updated literature search was performed on 21 January 2024 on Ovid MEDLINE, Ovid Embase, Cochrane Library, and KoreaMed to identify primary studies published after the issuance of the selected USPSTF guideline (S2 Table). A total of 10,681 articles were initially identified, and after a rigorous, multi-stage screening process by independent reviewers, 327 studies were ultimately included for evidence synthesis (Fig. 1).

### 4. Assessment of evidence quality and formulation of recommendations

The methodological quality and risk of bias for the included studies were assessed using the following design-specific tools: risk-of-bias-2 for randomized controlled trials (RCTs), Risk of Bias Assessment Tool for Nonrandomized Studies-2 for non-randomized studies (cohort studies), Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-2 for diagnostic accuracy studies, and A Measurement Tool to Assess systematic Reviews-2 for systematic reviews and meta-analyses. The overall certainty of evidence for each outcome was rated as high, moderate, low, or very low according to the GRADE framework. To formulate the final recommendations, the committee utilized the GRADE Evidence to Decision framework. This process involved a comprehensive discussion considering the balance between the benefits and harms of each intervention, the certainty of the evidence, patient values and preferences, and resource utilization. Following this structured approach, the committee voted to determine the direction and strength (strong or conditional) of each recommendation.

Specifically addressing the KQs, the committee systematically evaluated the benefits (e.g., reduction in CRC-specific mortality and incidence) and harms (e.g., complications, false positives) associated with efficacy (KQ1) as well as the diagnostic accuracy (KQ2) (e.g., sensitivity, specificity) for five distinct screening modalities: colonoscopy, FIT, gFOBT, CTC, and stool DNA testing.

For KQ3, which addresses the optimal screening start age, end age, and interval, the committee employed a dual approach combining a systematic review with a utility-based analysis. A Markov model was developed to simulate the long-term effectiveness and efficiency of various screening strategies, comparing different combinations of screening methods, ages, and intervals. The analysis primarily used quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) as the efficiency measure and calculated the number of screenings increased per incremental QALY to identify the most efficient strategies. This modeling analysis, supplemented by a systematic review of the existing literature, provided a comprehensive evidence base for recommendations on screening schedules. The final guideline was subjected to external review by 20 relevant academic societies and incorporated feedback from a public hearing to ensure clinical validity and social consensus.

## Results

### 1. Review of international guidelines

A review of 16 major international guidelines was conducted to contextualize the revision (Table 1). This analysis revealed a significant international trend toward lowering the screening initiation age from 50 to 45, a change adopted by the USPSTF (2021), ACG (2021), and American Cancer Society (2024). Tier 1 screening modalities in these guidelines predominantly include colonoscopy (10-year interval) and FIT (annual interval). The new 2025 Korean National Cancer Center recommendation to initiate screening at age 45 for an asymptomatic, average-risk population aligns with this updated international consensus.

### 2. Efficacy, accuracy, and harms of colonoscopy (KQ1, KQ2)

The evidence for colonoscopy was based on one large-scale RCT and three cohort studies (Tables 2 and 3). The RCT reported a 17% reduction in CRC incidence (relative risk [RR], 0.83; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.72 to 0.96) in the group invited to screening, but it did not find a statistically significant reduction in CRC-specific mortality (RR, 0.92; 95% CI, 0.69 to 1.21). In contrast, a meta-analysis of cohort studies demonstrated significant reductions in both CRC incidence (RR, 0.68; 95% CI, 0.65 to 0.72) and mortality (RR, 0.37; 95% CI, 0.26 to 0.53). Primary harms included serious complications, with pooled estimates for major bleeding at 0.18% and perforation at 0.05%. The overall certainty of evidence for colonoscopy was determined to be moderate.

### 3. Efficacy, accuracy, and harms of FIT (KQ1, KQ2)

For FIT, no RCTs meeting the inclusion criteria were found. However, a meta-analysis of large-scale cohort studies reported a 33% reduction in CRC incidence (RR, 0.67; 95% CI, 0.66 to 0.69) and a 53% reduction in CRC mortality (RR, 0.47; 95% CI, 0.45 to 0.49). The primary harm identified was false-positive results, occurring in an average rate of 6%-8% of the participants, necessitating follow-up colonoscopies that can cause patient anxiety. Diagnostic accuracy analysis (cutoff, 20 μg Hb/g) found a pooled sensitivity of 0.74 and specificity of 0.94. The committee rated the overall certainty of evidence for FIT as high.

### 4. Evidence for other screening modalities (KQ1, KQ2)

For gFOBT, while RCTs confirmed a reduction in CRC mortality (~15%), the modality was deemed unfeasible in South Korea as the necessary reagents are not currently produced or imported. For both CTC and stool DNA tests, the systematic review found no direct evidence from RCTs or cohort studies demonstrating a reduction in CRC incidence or mortality. Furthermore, the committee noted that the multi-target stool DNA tests evaluated in most international studies are fundamentally different from the single-target test commercially available in South Korea, limiting the applicability of existing evidence. The certainty of evidence for both CTC and stool DNA was rated very low.

### 5. Optimal screening age and interval: utility-based analysis (KQ3)

A Markov model was developed to assess the utility (QALYs) and efficiency (number of screenings increased per incremental QALY) of various screening strategies for KQ3. For colonoscopy (Fig. 2A), strategies with 5-year intervals and wider age ranges (e.g., S5, 45 to ∞) yielded the highest QALYs (10,628 per 1,000 people) but also the highest procedural burden (8,398 procedures per 1,000). The analysis identified the 15-year interval (S26, 45 to 74 years) as having the most favorable efficiency. This strategy resulted in 10,626 QALYs (per 1,000) and 1,923 colonoscopy screenings (per 1,000), yielding 0.0099 incremental QALYs per increased number of screenings compared to S32 (15-year interval, 55 to 69 years) with the lowest QALY. However, the committee determined that the 10-year interval (S16, 45 to 74 years) represented a superior balance of clinical effectiveness, efficiency, and public acceptability.

### 6. Optimal FIT strategies: utility-based analysis (KQ3)

For FIT (Fig. 2B), 1-year intervals (e.g., S5, 45 to ∞) maximized QALYs (10,627 per 1,000) but required the highest rate of follow-up colonoscopies (2,675.9 per 1,000). The analysis clearly demonstrated that the 2-year interval strategy (S16, 45 to 69 years) was the most efficient. The strategy resulted in 10,624 QALYs (per 1,000) and 815 colonoscopy screenings (per 1,000), yielding 0.0221 incremental QALYs per increased number of screenings compared to S26 (2-year interval, 55 to 69 years) with the lowest QALY. Based on these models, the committee concluded that a 1-2-year interval for FIT provides an optimal balance between maximizing health benefits and managing resource utilization.

### 7. Incidence of colonoscopy-related complications stratified by age

In the analysis of 28,815,919 colonoscopy cases, the overall rate of complications within one week was 0.47%, comprising 0.42% for bleeding and 0.03% for perforation (Table 4). Bleeding was identified as the predominant adverse event across all age groups. A distinct age-dependent increase in complication rates was observed; the incidence was lowest in the population under 20 years of age (0.27%) and escalated progressively with age. Notably, the oldest age group (≥ 85 years) exhibited the highest complication rate at 1.97%, which was driven largely by a bleeding rate of 1.75%. When the observation window was extended to one month, the cumulative complication rate increased slightly to 0.49%, with the ≥ 85 age group consistently demonstrating the highest risk at 2.10%.

### 8. Summary of recommendations

Based on the synthesis of evidence for efficacy, harm, and cost-utility, the committee formulated four final recommendations: (1) asymptomatic, average-risk adults aged 45-74 years are recommended to undergo screening colonoscopy every 10 years (conditional recommendation); (2) asymptomatic, average-risk adults aged 45-74 years are recommended to undergo screening with FIT every 1-2 years (conditional recommendation); (3) recommendation for or against CTC for screening is withheld due to insufficient evidence; and (4) recommendation for or against stool DNA testing for screening is withheld owing to insufficient evidence.

## Discussion

This 2025 revision of the Korean CRC Screening Guideline reflects the changing CRC epidemiology, advancements in screening technologies, and accumulation of recent evidence through research. Ten years following the release of the 2015 guideline, the key updates include adjusting the cessation age from 80 to 74 and conditionally recommending both FIT and colonoscopy for asymptomatic, average-risk adults. We revised the guideline recommendations for the FIT and gFOBT separately, and—considering the Korean clinical setting—excluded the gFOBT from the recommended modalities. This revision aims to provide an optimal, evidence-based screening strategy tailored to the Korean context, eventually reducing the burden of CRC.

This guideline conditionally recommends both FIT (every 1-2 years) and colonoscopy (every 10 years) as screening options. In the context of an NCSP, a conditional recommendation does not indicate insufficient justification for public implementation but rather acknowledges that multiple evidence-based strategies may be acceptable depending on policy priorities and resource availability. FIT is supported by high-certainty evidence demonstrating significant reductions in CRC incidence and mortality. Its non-invasive nature and lower cost contribute to higher patient preference and program acceptability. Conversely, colonoscopy, supported by moderate-certainty evidence for reducing CRC incidence, offers the unique advantage of detecting and removing precancerous adenomas, thereby preventing cancer development. However, it has the drawbacks of invasiveness, risk of rare but serious complications (bleeding, perforation), and higher resource requirements. Recognizing the distinct profiles of benefits, harms, costs, and patient preferences, the conditional strength reflects both the certainty of evidence and the deliberate recognition of policy-level equipoise between FIT and colonoscopy, emphasizing the importance of shared decision-making between clinicians and patients in choosing the most appropriate test.

The 10-year interval for screening colonoscopy was determined by synthesizing cost-utility analysis results, international guidelines, and the Korean healthcare context. While modeling suggested a 15-year interval might be most cost-effective, transitioning to a 10-year interval significantly improved the efficiency ratio (incremental QALY gain per additional procedure) at a reasonable cost increase, aligning better with public health goals. Furthermore, the 10-year interval is the standard recommendation in various international guidelines. Considering Korea’s high accessibility to endoscopy, skilled specialists, and public preference, a 10-year interval was deemed a practical and acceptable balance. Although a 5-year interval was discussed during public consultations, current evidence was insufficient to support its cost-effectiveness for asymptomatic, average-risk individuals.

The recommendation for a 1-2-year interval for FIT is also based on cost-utility analyses. The analysis identified a 2-year interval as the most cost-effective strategy (lowest incremental cost-utility ratio), whereas a 1-year interval maximized QALY gains. Considering that the current national screening program operates on a 1-year cycle and potential concerns about reduced participation or effectiveness with a longer interval, the committee recommended a flexible 1-2-year interval. This allows alignment with existing practices while acknowledging the high cost-effectiveness of biennial screening, striking a balance between clinical benefit and resource utilization.

CRC incidence continues to increase in older age groups, and recent advances in oncologic treatment and supportive care have improved survival and treatment feasibility even among elderly patients. Screening cessation was set at the age of 74 years, based on evidence indicating a sharp increase in colonoscopy-related complication risks and diminishing net benefit in older populations. Korean National Health Insurance claims data confirmed significantly higher rates of complications in individuals aged ≥ 75 years. While public consultations included opinions favoring extending screening to age 80, considering increased life expectancy and healthier aging, the committee prioritized the safety of a population-based program and adopted the 74-year cutoff. This decision reflects a population-level trade-off between the increasing risk of colonoscopy-related complications and the potential medical, economic, and societal consequences of delayed CRC diagnosis in older adults, rather than reliance on a single factor.

However, the guideline explicitly states that for selected individuals aged ≥ 75 years with substantial life expectancy, good functional status, limited comorbidity burden, and no recent prior screening, CRC screening may still provide net benefit and should be considered through individualized clinical decision-making, following consultation with a clinician. Thus, the upper age limit should be interpreted not as a rigid biological cutoff, but as a pragmatic population-level recommendation designed to maximize safety and effectiveness while preserving clinical flexibility for individual patients.

This guideline withholds recommendations for gFOBT, CTC, and stool DNA testing. Although gFOBT has proven efficacy in reducing mortality, its practical implementation in South Korea is impossible owing to the unavailability of necessary reagents. For CTC and stool DNA testing, direct evidence demonstrating reductions in CRC incidence or mortality was insufficient, and the certainty of evidence was rated very low. A specific limitation for stool DNA testing was the discrepancy between the multi-target tests evaluated in most international studies and the single-target test predominantly available in South Korea, hindering the applicability of existing evidence. Therefore, these modalities require further research before they can be recommended for population screening in South Korea.

In conclusion, the 2025 Korean CRC Screening Guideline provides updated recommendations based on a rigorous GRADE methodology, incorporating systematic reviews, meta-analysis, analysis of domestic data, cost-utility modeling, and stakeholder input. Its strengths lie in its evidence-based approach and consideration of the Korean context and cost-effectiveness. However, the limitations include reliance on some international data and the need for ongoing research within the Korean population. Future efforts should focus on implementing these guidelines effectively, establishing robust quality assurance programs, monitoring outcomes, addressing health disparities in screening uptake, promoting patient-centered information and decision support, and periodically updating the recommendations as new evidence emerges.
