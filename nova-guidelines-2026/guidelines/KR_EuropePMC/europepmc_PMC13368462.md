# Korean Society of Interventional Cardiology Expert Consensus for the Management of Chronic Coronary Syndrome: Part 1

## Abstract

**Author's summary**

Chronic coronary syndrome is a heterogeneous manifestation of ischemic heart disease requiring individualized diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. This expert consensus presents a stepwise approach to the diagnosis, treatment, and long-term management of chronic coronary syndrome, integrating current international evidence with Korean clinical data and practice patterns.

## INTRODUCTION

Coronary artery disease (CAD) remains the leading cause of death worldwide, accounting for approximately 13% of all deaths in 2021. In Korea, cardiovascular disease, including CAD, is the second leading cause of death after cancer, reflecting a pattern similar to that observed globally. With population aging and the increasing prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors, the burden of ischemic heart disease (IHD) has continued to rise, making cardiovascular disease the largest contributor to national health care expenditure among all disease categories.

Chronic coronary syndrome (CCS) is a unifying classification that encompasses the long-term, often fluctuating presentations of IHD, including stable angina and silent myocardial ischemia. Unlike acute coronary syndrome (ACS), which primarily results from abrupt plaque rupture and thrombosis, CCS includes a broader spectrum of pathophysiological mechanisms, ranging from obstructive epicardial disease to non-obstructive disease and microvascular dysfunction. This heterogeneity necessitates individualized diagnostic and therapeutic strategies, as both underdiagnosis and overtreatment remain common challenges in daily practice. Although CCS is generally stable, it may progress over time and occasionally evolve into ACS, underscoring the importance of preventive therapy and longitudinal care.

Since the publication of recent CCS guidelines, new evidence has emerged regarding optimal diagnostic strategies, imaging and physiological assessment, and tailored therapeutic approaches. In addition, contemporary management increasingly requires integration across multidisciplinary domains, including cardiovascular prevention, imaging-based decision-making, and revascularization planning, with a strong emphasis on patient-centered outcomes. However, the real-world application of these principles in the Korean clinical context remains heterogeneous, and evidence-based localization is needed.

This expert consensus document aims to provide a comprehensive, stepwise approach to the diagnosis, treatment, and longitudinal care of patients with CCS, reflecting the latest international and Korean evidence as well as current practice patterns.

## STEPWISE APPROACH TO THE INITIAL MANAGEMENT AND DIAGNOSIS OF CHRONIC CORONARY SYNDROME

### General clinical examination and basic tests

The initial evaluation of a patient with suspected CCS is a foundational step that requires a systematic, evidence-based approach. The primary objectives of this first encounter are 3-fold: (1) to carefully assess the patient’s symptoms and signs to determine the clinical likelihood of obstructive CAD; (2) to urgently rule out ACS or other life-threatening causes of chest discomfort; and (3) to establish a comprehensive baseline for risk stratification to guide subsequent diagnostic testing and long-term management.

#### Guideline-directed framework for initial evaluation

Contemporary international guidelines consistently endorse a structured, stepwise diagnostic approach. For example, the 2024 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines outline a 4-step process: Step 1 involves general clinical evaluation; Step 2 includes further cardiac assessment and estimation of the clinical likelihood of obstructive CAD; Step 3 proceeds to definitive diagnostic testing; and Step 4 involves initiation of appropriate therapy. This section focuses on the critical components of the first 2 steps, which form the foundation of the diagnostic pathway.

##### 1. Synthesis of international guidelines

Recommendations from major cardiology societies provide a robust framework for the initial evaluation.

- • 2024 ESC Guidelines for the Management of Chronic Coronary Syndromes: A major update in these guidelines is the strong recommendation to use a “risk factor-weighted clinical likelihood model” to estimate the pretest likelihood of obstructive CAD. This model integrates age, sex, symptom characteristics, and traditional cardiovascular risk factors, thereby improving diagnostic precision. Its implementation enables clinicians to identify a large cohort of patients—nearly half of those presenting with chest pain—as having a very low likelihood (≤5%) of obstructive CAD; in these patients, deferral of further testing is recommended. This recommendation represents a substantial shift from previous models, which often overestimated disease prevalence and contributed to frequent low-yield diagnostic testing. The new approach supports more judicious use of health care resources and minimizes patient exposure to unnecessary procedures and radiation.
- • 2023 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology (AHA/ACC) Guideline for the Management of Patients With Chronic Coronary Disease: The AHA/ACC guideline introduced the term “chronic coronary disease” to replace “stable ischemic heart disease,” reflecting the contemporary understanding of atherosclerosis as a dynamic and progressive disease continuum rather than a static condition. From the initial encounter onward, the guideline emphasizes a patient-centered approach that incorporates shared decision-making into risk assessment and diagnostic planning.
- • 2022 Japanese Circulation Society (JCS) Guideline: The JCS guideline aligns with the evolving international consensus and emphasizes that pretest probability should be refined by assessing an individual’s “clinical likelihood,” which is modified by traditional risk factors and findings from basic tests, such as the resting electrocardiogram (ECG) and echocardiography.

#### The clinical encounter: history and physical examination

##### 1. Patient history and symptom assessment

A careful and comprehensive patient history is the cornerstone of CCS diagnosis. The assessment should systematically characterize the patient’s symptoms and overall risk profile.

###### 1) Assessment of chest discomfort

A detailed characterization of the primary symptoms is essential. Clinicians should ask about the following features:

- • Location and radiation: Chest discomfort suggestive of angina pectoris is typically not focal but involves an area at least the size of the patient’s palm. It is usually described as retrosternal or left-sided chest discomfort.
- • Quality or character: The discomfort is typically described as pressure, tightness, squeezing, heaviness, or burning. Clinicians should recognize that patients may not label the sensation as “pain.”
- • Triggers: Symptoms are characteristically provoked by physical exertion and, in some patients, by severe emotional stress.
- • Relieving factors: A key diagnostic feature is relief of symptoms within minutes after rest or sublingual nitroglycerin administration.
- • Duration: Anginal episodes typically last several minutes, such as 5–15 minutes. Discomfort that is fleeting, lasting only seconds, or persistent, lasting hours or days, is less likely to be ischemic in origin.
- • Anginal equivalents and associated symptoms: Myocardial ischemia can occur without classic chest discomfort. Symptoms such as exertional dyspnea, excessive fatigue, nausea, diaphoresis, or jaw or arm pain may represent anginal equivalents. These presentations are particularly common in women, older adults, and patients with diabetes mellitus.

###### 2) Functional status assessment

Symptom severity and its effect on functional capacity should be quantified using a standardized scale. The Canadian Cardiovascular Society angina classification (Class I–IV) is widely used to establish baseline symptom burden, guide therapy, and monitor treatment response.

###### 3) Cardiovascular risk factor assessment

A systematic inventory of established cardiovascular risk factors is mandatory. This assessment should include hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, current or past tobacco use, and a family history of premature CAD, defined as CAD in a first-degree male relative aged <55 years or a first-degree female relative aged <65 years. Previous cardiovascular disease should also be documented, including cerebrovascular disease, peripheral vascular disease, chronic kidney disease, and a history of cardiovascular surgery or procedures.

###### 4) Differential diagnosis

The history should also actively assess features suggestive of non-cardiac causes. These include the relationship of symptoms to meals or posture, which may suggest gastroesophageal reflux; pleuritic features, which may suggest pulmonary or pericardial disease; and clear reproducibility with palpation or specific movements, which may suggest musculoskeletal pain.

##### 2. Physical examination

Although physical examination is often unremarkable in patients with stable CCS, it can provide important clues to advanced atherosclerosis, comorbid conditions, or alternative diagnoses.

###### 1) General appearance and vital signs

Clinicians should assess the patient for signs of distress. Vital signs, including heart rate and respiratory rate, should be recorded. Blood pressure should be measured in both arms to screen for significant interarm differences, which may suggest subclavian artery stenosis or, in an acute setting, aortic dissection.

###### 2) Cardiovascular examination

The examination should focus on identifying the following findings:

- • Signs of systemic atherosclerosis: Carotid bruits or diminished peripheral arterial pulses suggest a higher burden of systemic atherosclerotic disease and increase the clinical likelihood of CAD.
- • Signs of hyperlipidemia: Physical stigmata such as xanthelasma or tendon xanthomas indicate severe hypercholesterolemia.
- • Signs of heart failure: An S3 gallop, elevated jugular venous pressure, pulmonary rales, or peripheral edema may indicate significant left ventricular (LV) dysfunction, often resulting from prior myocardial infarction (MI).
- • Valvular or structural heart disease: Careful auscultation for heart murmurs is essential. A murmur consistent with aortic stenosis or the dynamic outflow-tract murmur of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy can identify conditions that mimic or cause anginal symptoms, even in the absence of obstructive epicardial CAD.

###### 3) Exclusion of non-cardiac causes

The examination should include palpation of the chest wall and sternocostal joints to assess for tenderness that reproduces the patient’s symptoms, a finding that strongly suggests a musculoskeletal origin. Lung auscultation is also important for detecting signs of underlying pulmonary disease.

##### 1. 12-Lead resting electrocardiogram

A resting 12-lead ECG is a mandatory, non-invasive, inexpensive first-line investigation for every patient presenting with suspected CCS. The ECG is often normal in patients with CCS, particularly when they are asymptomatic, and an abnormal ECG does not necessarily indicate IHD. However, several abnormal findings require attention, including the following:

- • Evidence of prior MI, such as pathological Q waves.
- • Signs of LV hypertrophy.
- • Conduction abnormalities, such as left bundle branch block.
- • Non-specific ST-segment and T-wave changes.

The primary role of ECG in the initial evaluation is to help exclude an acute ischemic process. Dynamic ST-segment elevation or depression should immediately shift the diagnosis from suspected CCS to a working diagnosis of ACS, requiring urgent management. Although artificial intelligence-enabled ECG analysis is an emerging approach that may complement traditional diagnostic methods, routine integration into clinical practice requires further validation before definitive recommendations can be made.

##### 2. Laboratory testing

A panel of baseline blood tests is recommended to assess cardiovascular risk factors, identify conditions that may exacerbate ischemia, and establish a baseline before therapeutic intervention.

###### 1) Cardiac biomarkers

Cardiac troponin, preferably measured with a high-sensitivity assay, should be assessed if the clinical presentation raises any suspicion of ACS. In patients with truly stable CCS, troponin levels are expected to be normal. Troponin elevation may be observed in chronic kidney disease, tachyarrhythmias, and heart failure independent of CAD.

###### 2) Risk factors and baseline assessment

The standard laboratory panel should include the following:

- • A fasting lipid profile, including total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and triglycerides, to guide lipid-lowering therapy.
- • Fasting blood glucose and/or hemoglobin A1c to screen for and monitor diabetes mellitus.
- • A complete blood count to rule out significant anemia, which can lower the ischemic threshold.
- • Serum creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate to assess baseline renal function, which is essential for appropriate dosing of many cardiovascular medications and for planning future procedures involving iodinated contrast.
- • Thyroid-stimulating hormone testing, which should be considered to exclude hyperthyroidism as a precipitant of angina.
- • N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide testing to help confirm or exclude suspected heart failure.

##### 3. Initial imaging and ancillary tests

###### 1) Resting transthoracic echocardiography

Resting transthoracic echocardiography is recommended as a core component of the initial workup for patients with suspected CCS. It has several purposes: it allows assessment of LV systolic and diastolic function, provides an estimate of left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), and can identify regional wall-motion abnormalities suggestive of prior, possibly clinically silent, MI. It is also valuable for excluding structural or valvular heart disease, such as significant aortic stenosis or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, that can present with anginal symptoms.

###### 2) Chest X-ray

A chest X-ray is not mandatory for every patient with suspected CCS. However, it is useful in selected cases, particularly when clinical signs or symptoms suggest heart failure, for assessment of cardiomegaly or pulmonary congestion, or when a pulmonary cause of chest discomfort is being considered.

###### 3) Pulmonary function tests

According to the ESC guidelines, pulmonary function testing may be considered when dyspnea is a prominent symptom or when the differential diagnosis includes a primary pulmonary condition, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, that may be contributing to the patient’s symptoms.

#### Initial risk assessment and clinical decision-making

The history, physical examination, and basic diagnostic tests together provide the basis for the initial risk assessment. This step is not intended to provide a definitive diagnosis in most cases; rather, it functions as a triage mechanism to guide the subsequent diagnostic strategy.

##### 1. Estimating the clinical likelihood of obstructive coronary artery disease

The central task at this stage is to estimate the patient’s probability of having anatomically significant obstructive CAD. The 2024 ESC risk factor-weighted clinical likelihood model is the current preferred tool for this purpose. By integrating age, sex, symptom characteristics, and cardiovascular risk factor burden, this model provides a more nuanced and accurate pretest probability than older symptom-based models, which tended to overestimate risk in contemporary populations.

##### 2. Stratification and management pathways

Based on the estimated clinical likelihood, patients can be stratified into one of 3 pathways, consistent with modern clinical decision pathways. Figure 1 summarizes these management pathways.

###### 1) Very low or low likelihood

For patients with a very low clinical likelihood of obstructive CAD, such as ≤5% according to the 2024 ESC guidelines, the focus should shift away from further ischemic testing. These patients can be reassured regarding an ischemic cause of their symptoms, and management should focus on cardiovascular risk factors and evaluation for potential non-cardiac causes. Deferral of further cardiac testing is the recommended and most appropriate course of action. In patients with a low pretest probability of obstructive CAD (>5–15%), routine diagnostic evaluation has unclear benefit; however, testing may still be considered when symptoms substantially interfere with daily life and further workup is needed.

###### 2) Moderate or high likelihood

This category encompasses the largest group of patients. For these individuals, the diagnosis of CCS remains uncertain, and non-invasive diagnostic testing is appropriate. Testing may include functional assessment to detect ischemia or anatomical assessment, such as coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA), to visualize the coronary arteries. The initial evaluation identifies this group as the population most likely to benefit from advanced diagnostic testing, which is discussed in ‘Diagnostic tests to confirm the diagnosis’ section.

###### 3) Very high likelihood

In patients with a very high clinical likelihood of obstructive CAD, particularly those with severe, lifestyle-limiting symptoms, such as Canadian Cardiovascular Society Class III–IV angina, angina at a low level of exercise, or high-risk features on initial testing, such as LV dysfunction on echocardiography, it may be appropriate to bypass non-invasive testing and proceed directly to invasive coronary angiography (ICA) for definitive diagnosis and potential revascularization.

This structured triage process helps ensure that patients receive an appropriate level of investigation, increasing diagnostic yield while minimizing unnecessary procedures.

#### Specific considerations for clinical practice in Korea

Although the framework provided by international guidelines is broadly applicable, optimal clinical practice in Korea requires consideration of local epidemiological patterns and evidence from national registries.

##### 1. Insights from the KoRean wOmen’S chest pain rEgistry (KoROSE): the female patient profile

Data from the nationwide KoROSE provide important insights into sex-specific presentations of suspected CAD in Korea. The registry has shown that Korean women who present with stable chest pain are often older and have a higher burden of cardiovascular risk factors than men. Despite this higher-risk profile, they have a paradoxically lower prevalence of obstructive CAD on invasive angiography. Their symptoms are also more frequently described in ways that have historically been labeled “atypical.” Although these “atypical” symptoms may originate from non-cardiac sources, such as costochondritis or gastroesophageal reflux disease, physicians should recognize that angina may manifest differently in women and men and should exercise caution when estimating the likelihood of CAD underlying these symptoms.

##### 2. The high prevalence of vasospastic angina

A defining feature of coronary disease in East Asian populations, including Korea, is the relatively high prevalence of coronary vasospasm. The Variant Angina Korea (VA-KOREA) registry, a large-scale, prospective, multicenter study, confirmed that vasospastic angina (VSA) is a common and clinically significant cause of chest pain in Korean patients. This finding has important implications for the diagnostic algorithm. In many Western clinical settings, a diagnostic workup that rules out obstructive CAD is often considered “negative,” and the evaluation may stop. In the Korean context, the same result should be interpreted as a clinically important branch point in the diagnostic pathway. For a symptomatic Korean patient, non-obstructive coronary arteries should not be treated as an endpoint; rather, this finding should prompt consideration of coronary vasomotor disorders. Clinicians should therefore maintain a lower threshold for specialized coronary function testing, including provocative testing with agents such as ergonovine or acetylcholine (Ach), to diagnose or exclude VSA.

##### 3. Local risk factor burden

National data from Korea show a substantial burden of major cardiovascular risk factors in the adult population. The prevalence of hypertension is approximately 27–30%, diabetes mellitus is approximately 10%, and current smoking remains prevalent at nearly 25%. This high background prevalence of atherosclerosis-related risk factors underscores the importance of thorough risk factor assessment in every patient. It also supports the use of objective models that incorporate risk factors, such as the 2024 ESC clinical likelihood score, to stratify patients accurately, because symptom presentation alone may be insufficient in a population with a high a priori risk.

#### Synthesis and transition to advanced diagnostic testing

In summary, the general clinical examination and basic testing for suspected CCS constitute a critical, multifaceted process. This initial step helps triage patients by ruling out acute and life-threatening conditions, establishing the clinical likelihood of obstructive CAD, and identifying key prognostic markers and comorbidities that inform long-term management. The output of this evaluation—a patient stratified into a low-, moderate-, or high-likelihood category—is an essential prerequisite for the subsequent phase of the diagnostic process. For patients in whom the diagnosis remains uncertain or further risk stratification is required, the next step is the judicious selection and interpretation of non-invasive functional and anatomical tests, as detailed in the following section.

#### Recommendations

- • A careful history should be obtained for all patients with suspected CCS, including symptom characteristics, anginal equivalents, especially in women, older adults, and patients with diabetes, and cardiovascular risk factors.
- • Functional capacity should be quantified using a standardized scale, such as the Canadian Cardiovascular Society classification.
- • A thorough physical examination should be performed to identify signs of systemic atherosclerosis, heart failure, or valvular disease and to differentiate non-cardiac causes through chest wall palpation.
- • A resting 12-lead ECG is mandatory for all patients with suspected CCS to help exclude ACS.
- • Baseline laboratory tests should include a fasting lipid profile, glucose and/or hemoglobin A1c, a complete blood count to assess for anemia, and serum creatinine. High-sensitivity cardiac troponin should be measured if ACS is suspected.
- • Resting transthoracic echocardiography is recommended as a core component of the workup to assess LV function, evaluate for regional wall-motion abnormalities, and exclude structural or valvular heart disease, such as aortic stenosis or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.
- • The clinical likelihood of obstructive CAD should be estimated using a validated model that integrates age, sex, symptoms, and risk factors, such as the 2024 ESC model.
- • Patients should be stratified by clinical likelihood to guide management:
 - - Very low or low likelihood: Defer further ischemic testing, manage risk factors, and investigate non-cardiac causes.
 - - Moderate or high likelihood: Proceed with non-invasive diagnostic testing, either functional or anatomical.
 - - Very high likelihood: For patients with severe symptoms, such as Canadian Cardiovascular Society Class III–IV angina, consider proceeding directly to ICA.
- • In Korea, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for VSA because of its high prevalence in East Asian populations.
- • In symptomatic Korean patients, non-obstructive coronary arteries should prompt active consideration of coronary function testing, such as ergonovine provocation, to diagnose or exclude VSA.

### Diagnostic tests to confirm the diagnosis

#### Summary of the major guidelines and recent publications

The 2024 ESC Guidelines for the Management of Chronic Coronary Syndromes and the 2023 AHA/ACC Guideline for Chronic Coronary Disease advocate a stepwise diagnostic approach that integrates pretest probability, non-invasive imaging, and invasive functional assessment to confirm the presence and mechanism of coronary disease. Both guidelines emphasize that not all patients require invasive angiography at initial presentation and that non-invasive anatomical or functional testing should be prioritized according to clinical likelihood.

#### Anatomical imaging: coronary computed tomography angiography

In the risk factor-weighted clinical likelihood model proposed in the 2024 ESC guidelines, patients are categorized as having a very low (≤5%), low (5–15%), moderate (15–50%), high (50–85%), or very high (>85%) likelihood of obstructive CAD. In patients with a very low likelihood, further diagnostic testing may be deferred, whereas patients with low-to-moderate likelihood are recommended to undergo CCTA as a first-line test (Class I, Level A). The 2023 AHA/ACC guideline also supports a selective testing strategy but places greater emphasis on symptom change and clinical context, discouraging routine testing in stable asymptomatic patients.

CCTA is emphasized as the preferred initial modality for anatomical assessment because it enables direct visualization of plaque burden and morphology, including low-attenuation plaque, positive remodeling, and the napkin-ring sign. CCTA has excellent negative predictive value for excluding obstructive CAD; however, its positive predictive value is limited, particularly in the presence of heavy coronary calcification or in-stent restenosis in small-diameter stents (<3.0 mm), owing to frequent overestimation of stenosis severity. In the SCOT-HEART trial, adding CCTA to standard care in patients with suspected CAD significantly reduced the 5-year risk of MI by enabling more accurate diagnosis and targeted preventive therapy. In cases of intermediate stenosis (40–70%), computational fractional flow reserve derived from computed tomography (CT-FFR) has been shown to improve determination of ischemic relevance. Although CT-FFR improves functional assessment of intermediate coronary stenosis, studies including the FORECAST trial showed no significant reduction in major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) or health care costs, although they did show a reduction in unnecessary ICA.

#### Stress test

For patients with a moderate-to-high clinical likelihood of obstructive CAD (>15–85%), functional ischemia imaging is recommended. Modalities include stress echocardiography, myocardial perfusion imaging, and stress cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR), which allow quantification of ischemic burden and prediction of revascularization benefit. Stress echocardiography detects wall-motion abnormalities during exercise or pharmacological stress.

Myocardial single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) assesses perfusion defects but has lower spatial resolution. Positron emission tomography (PET) provides high accuracy for myocardial blood-flow quantification. Stress CMR offers simultaneous assessment of perfusion, function, and viability without radiation exposure. Non-invasive tests for microvascular dysfunction, such as PET- or CMR-derived coronary flow reserve (CFR), can identify angina with non-obstructive coronary arteries/ischemia with non-obstructive coronary arteries (ANOCA/INOCA) phenotypes. Although exercise ECG has limited diagnostic accuracy for ischemia, it can be a practical and informative initial test in selected patients with CCS, mainly for functional and clinical assessment rather than definitive diagnosis. However, the choice among stress imaging modalities should also account for availability at individual institutions and cost-effectiveness in the clinical context.

#### Invasive tests

ICA is indicated in patients with a very high likelihood of obstructive CAD (>85%) or persistent symptoms despite optimal medical therapy. ICA with invasive coronary pressure assessment is also indicated when non-invasive assessment suggests high event risk—for example, when CCTA shows ≥50% left main stenosis, ≥70% proximal left anterior descending artery stenosis with single- or 2-vessel CAD, or ≥70% proximal 3-vessel CAD—or when any stress test shows moderate-to-severe inducible ischemia. The 2024 ESC guidelines provide a Class I recommendation for invasive physiological assessment using fractional flow reserve (FFR; ≤0.80) or instantaneous wave-free ratio (iFR; ≤0.89) for intermediate lesions to improve risk assessment and clinical decision-making and to reduce clinical events. These recommendations are supported by large outcome trials, such as FAME 1, FAME 2, DEFINE-FLAIR, iFR-SWEDEHEART, R3F, and RIPCORD, which consistently demonstrated the clinical benefits of physiology-guided revascularization. Furthermore, the 2024 ESC guidelines elevated invasive coronary function testing, including CFR, index of microvascular resistance (IMR), and ACh provocation testing, to Class I (Level B) for patients with ANOCA/INOCA. When myocardial ischemia is demonstrated on functional imaging despite the absence of obstructive CAD on CCTA or ICA, ANOCA/INOCA should be strongly considered. In these patients, invasive coronary function testing with ACh and adenosine is recommended to identify the underlying mechanism of ischemia and classify the patient into a specific pathophysiological endotype.

This structured evaluation enables differentiation into the following endotypes.

##### 1. Endothelial dysfunction

Endothelial dysfunction is defined by epicardial vasoconstriction, represented by any reduction in luminal diameter, and/or a blunted flow response, specifically a <50% increase in coronary blood flow during the second ACh infusion (2–20 μg) compared with baseline.

##### 2. Impaired vasodilation (coronary microvascular dysfunction)

Impaired vasodilation, or coronary microvascular dysfunction, is characterized by reduced vasodilatory reserve or elevated microvascular resistance, typically defined during adenosine-induced maximal hyperemia as follows:

- • CFR <2.5
- • IMR >25
- • Hyperemic myocardial velocity resistance >2.5

##### 3. Epicardial vasospastic angina

Epicardial VSA is diagnosed when ACh provokes typical symptoms and ischemic ECG changes accompanied by ≥90% transient epicardial luminal narrowing during incremental ACh doses (2–200 μg) or ergonovine administration (20–60 μg, up to 100 μg).

##### 4. Mixed endotype

A mixed endotype is defined by overlapping mechanisms, such as vasospasm plus impaired vasodilation.

##### 5. Equivocal response

An equivocal response is defined as the occurrence of symptoms without fulfillment of the diagnostic criteria for any specific endotype.

#### Evidence from Korea

Recent Korean studies have validated guideline-based diagnostic strategies in real-world practice, showing that a CCTA-first approach significantly improves diagnostic accuracy and reduces unnecessary invasive angiography in patients with an intermediate likelihood of CAD. In a nationwide cohort, the use of CCTA as a gatekeeper before invasive angiography increased from 18.6% to 28.8% between 2012 and 2018. In a multicenter study of 319 patients, on-site CT-FFR achieved an accuracy of 80.6%, sensitivity of 88.1%, and specificity of 75.6%, confirming its clinical utility for functional lesion assessment in Korea. These findings indicate that CT-FFR outperforms anatomical CCTA and support its clinical applicability for reliable functional assessment of coronary lesions in routine practice. Invasive physiological studies in Korea have shown that combined FFR and IMR measurements effectively identify patients with microvascular dysfunction who are at higher risk of recurrent angina and hospitalization for heart failure.

#### Recommendations

- • CCTA is recommended as the first-line anatomical test for patients with an intermediate pretest likelihood of CAD to confirm or exclude obstructive disease.
- • CT perfusion or CT-FFR may be considered in patients with intermediate stenoses or inconclusive CCTA findings to improve diagnostic accuracy.
- • In patients with suspected CCS and a moderate-to-high pretest likelihood of obstructive CAD (15–85%), functional ischemia testing with stress imaging, including stress echocardiography, SPECT, PET, or stress CMR, is recommended to detect and quantify myocardial ischemia and estimate the risk of future cardiovascular events.
- • Stress CMR and PET perfusion are preferred modalities when available because of their superior diagnostic and prognostic performance.
- • ICA is indicated in patients with a very high likelihood of CAD (>85%), persistent angina despite optimal medical therapy, or inconclusive or high-risk non-invasive results.
- • Physiological assessment of stenosis severity using FFR (≤0.80) or iFR (≤0.89) is recommended to guide revascularization in intermediate lesions.
- • Coronary function testing, including CFR, IMR, and ACh provocation, is recommended for patients with ANOCA/INOCA to identify microvascular dysfunction or vasospasm.
- • In patients with CCS who have no change in clinical or functional status while receiving optimized guideline-directed medical therapy, routine periodic testing with CCTA or stress testing, with or without imaging, is not recommended to guide therapeutic decision-making.

## GUIDELINE-DIRECTED THERAPY

### Patient education, lifestyle optimization, and risk factor control in chronic coronary syndrome

#### Guideline summary

Despite major advances in pharmacological and interventional therapy, optimal CCS management requires patient education, lifestyle optimization, and multifactorial risk factor control. The 2024 ESC and 2023 AHA/ACC guidelines emphasize that these non-pharmacological strategies are not supplemental measures but essential components of contemporary secondary prevention.

#### Patient education and engagement

Patient education about risk factors and lifestyle modification, together with pharmacological treatment, is essential for effective secondary prevention. Key educational goals include helping patients understand CAD pathophysiology and chronicity, recognize angina symptoms and early warning signs, appreciate the importance of strict adherence to medical therapy, and engage in self-monitoring and lifestyle management. A multidisciplinary team involving physicians, nurses, dietitians, and exercise specialists can improve outcomes, as illustrated by the EUROACTION trial, a nurse-led, family-based preventive cardiology model that improved lifestyle adherence and risk factor control.

#### Lifestyle modification

##### 1. Diet

The PREDIMED trial demonstrated that a Mediterranean diet enriched with extra-virgin olive oil or nuts significantly reduced major cardiovascular events. Current dietary recommendations emphasize high intake of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, legumes, and fish while limiting red and processed meats, refined carbohydrates, and added sugars.

##### 2. Alcohol

The association between alcohol consumption and cardiovascular risk is dose-dependent and complex. Accordingly, the 2024 ESC and 2023 AHA/ACC guidelines advise against initiating alcohol consumption for presumed cardiovascular benefit and recommend minimizing intake among individuals who already drink alcohol. Notably, whereas the ESC guidelines apply a universal threshold regardless of sex, the AHA/ACC guidelines propose sex-specific limits, reflecting differences in guideline methodology and interpretation of the available evidence. If alcohol is consumed, intake should be limited to less than 100 g per week, approximately 15 g per day, because intake above this threshold has been associated with higher all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in large individual-participant meta-analyses. However, this threshold should be interpreted with caution given the heterogeneity of the underlying evidence, including divergent findings from observational studies and Mendelian randomization analyses. A landmark meta-analysis of observational data further supports the conclusion that cardiovascular risk increases progressively with higher alcohol intake, whereas recent reviews highlight ongoing clinical uncertainty regarding safe levels of consumption.

##### 3. Smoking

Smoking remains one of the most potent modifiable risk factors for CAD progression and recurrent ischemic events. In a landmark meta-analysis, smoking cessation was associated with an approximately 36% reduction in all-cause mortality in patients with coronary heart disease. Comprehensive smoking-cessation strategies that integrate behavioral counseling and pharmacotherapy are recommended. First-line pharmacological aids, such as nicotine replacement therapy (NRT), varenicline, and bupropion, are effective and safe even after MI. Evidence on the role of electronic cigarettes, also referred to as e-cigarettes or electronic nicotine delivery systems, in smoking cessation remains mixed. A randomized trial by Jackson et al. found that e-cigarettes were almost twice as effective as NRT for 1-year abstinence when combined with behavioral support. Nevertheless, their long-term safety remains uncertain. Although e-cigarettes expose users to fewer toxicants than combustible cigarettes, nicotine-mediated vascular dysfunction and sympathetic activation may persist. Consequently, both the ESC and AHA/ACC guidelines emphasize that e-cigarettes should be considered only for smokers who are unable to quit using conventional therapies and should never be used by nonsmokers or youth. In the Korean context, a large-scale observational study reported that switching from conventional cigarettes to e-cigarettes was associated with a lower risk of cardiovascular events than continued conventional cigarette smoking in post- patients after percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) patients; however, given the current regulatory framework in Korea, which applies a similar level of caution to e-cigarettes and conventional cigarettes, complete cessation remains the primary recommendation.

##### 4. Exercise

Regular exercise improves vascular function and metabolic balance. For aerobic training, such as walking, jogging, cycling, or swimming, exercise at moderate to moderately high intensity at least 3 days per week is recommended. Exercise is contraindicated in patients with active angina symptoms or other high-risk cardiovascular conditions, such as high-grade arrhythmia, decompensated heart failure, severe aortic dilatation, or active thromboembolic disease.

##### 5. Weight management

Obesity is a major determinant of residual cardiovascular risk and is closely linked to hypertension, dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, and systemic inflammation. Evidence from large prospective cohorts, such as the Global BMI Mortality Collaboration and the Nurses’ Health Study, demonstrates a continuous, graded increase in cardiovascular mortality above a body mass index (BMI) of 25 kg/m2, with the lowest all-cause mortality observed at 22–24 kg/m2. Achieving a sustained 5–10% reduction in body weight through calorie restriction and increased physical activity markedly improves glycemic control, blood pressure, and lipid parameters. Although lifestyle-based weight loss remains the cornerstone of therapy, the availability of pharmacological and procedural interventions has broadened management options. In patients with obesity and established cardiovascular disease or diabetes, newer agents such as glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) and sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors provide additional cardiometabolic benefits.

#### Risk factor control

##### 1. Lipid management

Aggressive LDL-C lowering remains a cornerstone of CCS management, reflecting the “lower is better, earlier is better” principle endorsed by current guidelines. The 2024 ESC guidelines recommend an LDL-C target of <55 mg/dL (1.4 mmol/L), with at least a 50% reduction from baseline, for most patients with CCS and an even lower goal of <40 mg/dL (1.0 mmol/L) for those with recurrent events within 2 years or polyvascular disease, who are classified as being at “very high risk.” The 2023 AHA/ACC guideline adopts a tiered approach: initiate high-intensity statin therapy in all patients with CCS, add ezetimibe if LDL-C remains ≥70 mg/dL, and consider proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) inhibitors, such as evolocumab or alirocumab, for further reduction.

##### 2. Blood pressure control

Elevated blood pressure contributes to endothelial dysfunction, LV hypertrophy, and accelerated atherosclerosis, thereby increasing the risk of MI, stroke, and heart failure. The SPRINT trial provided landmark evidence that intensive systolic blood pressure control (<120 mmHg) significantly reduced major cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality compared with standard targets. Both the 2024 ESC and 2023 AHA/ACC guidelines recommend maintaining blood pressure below 130/80 mmHg in most patients with CCS, provided that this target is well tolerated. Initial therapy should include agents with established cardiovascular benefit, such as angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), especially in patients with prior MI, heart failure, or diabetes; beta-blockers, in patients with prior MI or angina; calcium channel blockers (CCBs); and thiazide-type diuretics. Combination therapy is frequently required, and fixed-dose combinations may improve adherence.

##### 3. Glycemic control

Chronic hyperglycemia accelerates endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and vascular inflammation, contributing to plaque instability and microvascular dysfunction. Effective glycemic control therefore has a pivotal role in secondary prevention. Contemporary guidelines emphasize individualized glycemic targets, generally aiming for hemoglobin A1c <7% while avoiding hypoglycemia in older adults and patients with comorbidities. SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs are first-line glucose-lowering medications in patients with CCS and type 2 diabetes.

##### 4. Prevention of infection (immunization)

Infections such as influenza, pneumococcal pneumonia, and coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) are additional risk factors for MACE, especially in patients with CCS. Respiratory infections may increase cardiovascular events through proinflammatory mediators, sympathetic activation, and stimulation of the coagulation cascade. They may also trigger rupture of vulnerable plaques. Vaccination can improve clinical outcomes and may reduce hospitalizations and health care costs. Evidence from many studies shows the greatest benefit in high-risk groups, including older adults and patients with CCS. Therefore, vaccination should be promoted as an additional preventive strategy in patients with CCS to reduce the risk of infectious complications.

#### Evidence from Korea

Korean data also support aggressive lifestyle modification, particularly smoking cessation, for secondary prevention in CCS. A large analysis of more than 25,000 patients undergoing PCI from a Korean national registry showed that continued smoking after PCI was associated with a markedly higher risk of MI, stroke, and cardiovascular death, whereas smoking cessation after PCI substantially reduced major cardiovascular events. Importantly, even sustained “light” smoking carried significant excess risk, emphasizing that complete cessation, rather than reduction, is required for optimal secondary prevention.

#### Recommendations

- • All patients with CCS should receive structured education covering disease pathophysiology, symptom recognition, medication adherence, and lifestyle management.,,
- • Smoking cessation should be strongly recommended. Behavioral counseling and pharmacotherapy, including NRT, varenicline, and bupropion, should be provided.,
- • A healthy dietary pattern, such as a Mediterranean-style diet, should be recommended for all patients with CCS.,
- • Alcohol intake should be minimized, and alcohol consumption should not be initiated for cardiovascular benefit.,
- • Regular physical activity at moderate to moderately high intensity should be encouraged at least 3 days per week.,
- • Lipid management should target LDL-C <55 mg/dL, or <40 mg/dL in patients at very high risk. Therapy should begin with the maximum tolerated dose of a high-intensity statin, followed by ezetimibe and then a PCSK9 inhibitor; bempedoic acid may be considered in patients with statin intolerance.,,
- • Blood pressure should be maintained at <130/80 mmHg when tolerated.,,
- • For glycemic control, a hemoglobin A1c target of <7% should generally be used. SGLT2 inhibitors or GLP-1 RAs with proven cardiovascular benefit should be used regardless of hemoglobin A1c.,,,,,
- • Vaccination against influenza, pneumococcal disease, and other widespread infections, such as COVID-19, should be encouraged.-

### Antianginal/anti-ischemic medication

#### General principles

For patients with CCS due to obstructive CAD, the primary goal of antianginal medical therapy is to relieve ischemia and angina symptoms, thereby improving quality of life and exercise capacity. Current evidence indicates that antianginal medical therapy provides symptomatic relief but does not improve long-term cardiovascular outcomes in CCS. The key mechanism of antianginal treatment for CCS is reduction of ischemia by decreasing myocardial oxygen demand or increasing coronary blood supply. Beta-blockers, non-dihydropyridine CCBs, and ivabradine reduce myocardial oxygen demand by lowering heart rate and/or contractility, whereas nitrates and dihydropyridine CCBs increase oxygenated arterial blood supply through vasodilation. Although evidence is limited, the stepwise approach to antianginal medical therapy includes first-line agents, namely beta-blockers, CCBs, and long-acting nitrates, and second-line agents, namely nicorandil, ranolazine, ivabradine, and trimetazidine. Short-acting nitroglycerin is recommended for immediate relief of acute angina symptoms across all lines of therapy. Different classes of antianginal drugs may need to be used in combination because the mechanisms underlying symptoms may include obstructive, vasospastic, or microvascular ischemia; therefore, medical therapy should be reassessed and adjusted to achieve adequate symptom control. Although most patients achieve clinically meaningful symptom control, complete freedom from angina is attained in only 40–50% of patients. Accordingly, revascularization may be considered in patients whose angina remains life-limiting despite optimized medical therapy.

#### First-line therapy

##### 1. Beta-blockers

Beta-blockers reduce myocardial oxygen demand by decreasing heart rate and contractility while increasing diastolic perfusion time. The use of beta-blocker therapy should be guided by LVEF and history of prior MI. In patients with LVEF ≤40% or recent MI, beta-blockers not only relieve angina but also reduce mortality. Among patients with LVEF >40% and no recent MI, beta-blockers are used primarily for symptom relief, as evidence of mortality reduction is lacking. When beta-blockers are used, the resting heart rate should be titrated to 55–60 beats per minute to optimize the anti-ischemic effect. Caution is required because of potential adverse effects, including bradyarrhythmias, masking of hypoglycemia in patients with diabetes mellitus, bronchospasm in patients with asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, peripheral vasoconstriction, postural hypotension, and erectile dysfunction.

##### 2. Calcium channel blockers

CCBs comprise 2 subclasses: dihydropyridines, such as amlodipine and nifedipine, and non-dihydropyridines, such as verapamil and diltiazem. Both subclasses relieve angina symptoms by dilating coronary and systemic arteries, thereby reducing afterload; however, only non-dihydropyridines also decrease heart rate and atrioventricular nodal conduction, similar to beta-blockers. Non-dihydropyridine and dihydropyridine CCBs are similarly effective for relieving angina symptoms in patients with CCS. Although CCBs relieve angina symptoms, they have not been shown to improve major cardiovascular outcomes in patients with CCS.

###### 1) Non-dihydropyridine agents

Non-dihydropyridine CCBs, such as diltiazem and verapamil, decrease heart rate and contractility and are similarly effective to beta-blockers for relieving anginal symptoms in patients with CCS. They also have vasodilatory effects in the coronary and peripheral arteries. No head-to-head outcome trials have compared diltiazem and verapamil. When non-dihydropyridine agents are used, the resting heart rate should be titrated to 55–60 beats per minute to optimize the anti-ischemic effect. However, caution is required because of potential adverse effects, including bradyarrhythmias, worsening systolic function in patients with LVEF ≤40% due to reduced myocardial contractility, constipation, and peripheral edema.

###### 2) Dihydropyridine agents

Dihydropyridine CCBs, such as amlodipine and nifedipine, are potent vasodilators that reduce systemic vascular resistance and arterial pressure, thereby lowering afterload, myocardial wall stress, and oxygen demand. These agents also increase coronary blood flow through coronary artery vasodilation and do not directly slow heart rate. They are similarly effective to beta-blockers and non-dihydropyridine CCBs for relieving anginal symptoms in patients with CCS. No head-to-head outcome trials have compared amlodipine and nifedipine. When dihydropyridine agents are used, caution is required because of potential adverse effects, including headache, flushing, dose-dependent ankle edema, reflex tachycardia, and gingival hyperplasia. However, these agents do not worsen heart failure with reduced ejection fraction.

##### 3. Nitrates

Nitrates dilate epicardial arteries and arterioles, but they reduce myocardial oxygen demand mainly through systemic vasodilation and preload reduction. Short-acting nitrates provide rapid relief of acute angina symptoms. All patients with CCS should have access to short-acting nitrates, such as sublingual nitroglycerin or nitroglycerin spray, for immediate symptom relief or prophylaxis. Long-acting nitrates have traditionally been considered effective for relieving angina symptoms in patients with CCS. Isosorbide dinitrate and isosorbide mononitrate are similarly effective for relieving angina symptoms in these patients. Continuous exposure to long-acting nitrates can lead to tolerance and attenuation of effect; therefore, a daily nitrate-free interval, typically 8–10 hours, is recommended to preserve efficacy. Caution is required because of potential adverse effects, including hypotension, headache, and flushing. These agents are contraindicated in patients with hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy or severe aortic stenosis and in patients receiving phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors.

#### Second-line therapy

##### 1. Nicorandil

Nicorandil donates nitric oxide and activates adenosine triphosphate-sensitive potassium channels, resulting in dilation of both arteries and veins, including the epicardial arteries and microvasculature. Nicorandil is effective for relieving angina symptoms but has no demonstrated effect on mortality in patients with CCS. When nicorandil is used, caution is required because of potential adverse effects, including headache, tachycardia, facial flushing, hypotension, and, rarely, serious skin, mucosal, or ocular ulcerations, as well as an increased risk of gastrointestinal ulcers when it is combined with aspirin.

##### 2. Trimetazidine

Trimetazidine is a metabolic modulator that shifts myocardial energy metabolism from fatty acid oxidation to glucose oxidation, thereby helping the heart use oxygen more efficiently and reducing ischemia at the cellular level. Although trimetazidine has been shown to relieve anginal symptoms in some studies, its overall efficacy remains uncertain because results across clinical trials have been inconsistent. When trimetazidine is used, caution is required because of potential adverse effects, including nausea, dizziness, mild gastrointestinal upset, and Parkinsonian-like symptoms, such as tremor and rigidity, especially in patients with impaired renal function.

##### 3. Ivabradine

Ivabradine is a sinus node inhibitor that selectively slows the mixed sodium–potassium inward current (If ) in the sinoatrial node, thereby reducing heart rate without affecting contractility or blood pressure. Ivabradine has a role in patients with CCS and LVEF ≤40% who are receiving tolerated beta-blocker therapy or cannot tolerate beta-blockers; in this setting, it can relieve angina symptoms and reduce cardiovascular events. In patients with CCS and LVEF >40%, ivabradine improved angina symptoms but may have been associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular events. Based on these findings, ivabradine should be considered in patients with LVEF ≤40% and a resting heart rate ≥70 beats per minute despite beta-blocker therapy. When ivabradine is used, caution is required regarding bradyarrhythmias, phosphenes, atrial fibrillation, and coadministration with CYP3A4 inhibitors.

##### 4. Combination therapy

In patients with CCS and persistent angina symptoms, tailored combination therapy using antianginal agents with different and complementary mechanisms can improve symptoms. Initiation of a first-line agent or combination therapy is recommended in patients with CCS. If angina persists, a second-line agent or combination therapy can be added.

For patients with high blood pressure, dihydropyridine CCBs are effective because they relieve ischemia while controlling hypertension. Beta-blockers can also help reduce blood pressure. If a patient is hypotensive, agents with minimal hemodynamic effects, such as trimetazidine, may be considered. For patients with LVEF <40%, beta-blockers, such as carvedilol or bisoprolol, are indicated for both outcome improvement and symptom relief. Ivabradine has a role in patients with CCS and LVEF <40%. Conversely, non-dihydropyridine CCBs should not be routinely used in patients with LVEF ≤40% because of their negative inotropic effects. For patients with variant angina, beta-blockers should be avoided, whereas CCBs and nitrates are the cornerstone of therapy. For patients with atrial fibrillation, beta-blockers or non-dihydropyridine CCBs can treat both conditions by controlling heart rate. For patients with peripheral arterial disease or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cardioselective beta-blockers, such as bisoprolol, can be considered, but non-selective beta-blockers, such as carvedilol, should be avoided. For patients with erectile dysfunction who routinely receive phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors, long-acting nitrates or nicorandil must be avoided.

#### Evidence from Korea

In a registry of patients with CCS undergoing PCI, beta-blocker therapy showed no benefit for the composite outcome of all-cause death and MI (hazard ratio [HR], 0.88; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.63–1.24). In a single-center cohort of patients with chronic total occlusion, beta-blocker therapy was not associated with a lower risk of all-cause mortality than no beta-blocker therapy (HR, 0.81; 95% CI, 0.61–1.08; p=0.15). In patients with LVEF >40%, the benefit of beta-blocker therapy remains a topic of debate. In a nationwide cohort of patients with LVEF ≥40%, beta-blocker therapy was associated with reduced all-cause mortality (HR, 0.80; 95% CI, 0.69–0.94), but another study did not show such a benefit, indicating conflicting evidence in this setting. Among stabilized patients who received beta-blocker therapy beyond the first year after MI, discontinuation of beta-blocker therapy was noninferior to continuation with respect to the composite of death from any cause, recurrent MI, or hospitalization for heart failure. In patients with variant angina, CCBs relieved coronary spasm but did not improve clinical outcomes compared with beta-blockers or nitrates. Nitrates, nicorandil, and trimetazidine have not been shown to improve long-term clinical outcomes in patients with CCS.

#### Recommendations

- • In patients with CCS, initial therapy with beta-blockers and/or CCBs is recommended to relieve angina symptoms.
- • In patients with CCS and LVEF ≤40%, beta-blockers should be used to reduce mortality unless they are contraindicated or not tolerated.
- • In patients with CCS and persistent symptoms after initial therapy, add-on second-line therapy with nicorandil, trimetazidine, ivabradine, or a combination of these agents can be considered to relieve angina symptoms.
- • In patients with CCS, short-acting nitrates are recommended for immediate relief of angina symptoms.

### Medical therapy for event prevention

#### General principles and guideline summary

Medical treatment is essential for reducing the risk of cardiovascular events in patients with CCS and should be provided alongside lifestyle modification, regardless of revascularization strategy. Standard medical therapy for event prevention generally includes antithrombotic drugs, lipid-lowering drugs, and renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibitors. SGLT2 inhibitors or GLP-1 RAs are recommended for selected patients. Evidence supporting the role of anti-inflammatory drugs in patients with CCS is increasing but remains limited.

#### Antithrombotic drugs

Single antiplatelet therapy (SAPT) with aspirin, or clopidogrel as an alternative to aspirin, is the standard antithrombotic treatment for patients with CCS during the chronic maintenance phase. Because the role of clopidogrel monotherapy or dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) after coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) has not been established, lifelong low-dose aspirin is recommended after CABG. DAPT with aspirin and a P2Y12 inhibitor, usually clopidogrel, is generally recommended for 6 months after PCI. Routine anticoagulant use in patients with CCS without an indication for anticoagulation is not recommended.

##### 1. Antiplatelet drugs

In a meta-analysis of individual data from randomized trials, aspirin, compared with no aspirin, reduced the risk of non-fatal MI, non-fatal ischemic stroke, or death from vascular causes. In the secondary prevention setting, the benefit of aspirin outweighed the bleeding risk, and no significant differences in efficacy or safety were observed between low-dose and high-dose aspirin. Thus, low-dose aspirin is a first-line antiplatelet therapy in patients with CCS, whereas clopidogrel has been recommended as an alternative to aspirin. No randomized data specifically compare aspirin and clopidogrel monotherapy in patients with CCS without a history of PCI or CABG.

DAPT, typically aspirin with clopidogrel, is generally recommended for 6 months after PCI. The appropriate duration of DAPT after PCI should be individualized according to ischemic and bleeding risk. Various characteristics, including older age, renal insufficiency, and previous stroke, increase both ischemic and bleeding risk. Recently, multiple randomized controlled trials showed that early discontinuation of DAPT followed by SAPT was more effective and safer than a conventional DAPT strategy, or at least noninferior to it, for reducing the risk of ischemic events, bleeding events, or both. Thus, early discontinuation of DAPT should be considered in patients undergoing PCI who are at high bleeding risk (HBR) without high ischemic risk. Conversely, an intensified antiplatelet strategy, such as prolonged DAPT beyond 6 months, DAPT with aspirin and a potent P2Y12 inhibitor, or the addition of low-dose rivaroxaban to aspirin, can be considered for patients undergoing PCI who are at high ischemic risk without HBR. Of note, the net clinical benefit of an intensified DAPT strategy over standard DAPT in patients with high ischemic risk was partially attenuated by increased bleeding events. In contrast, the benefit of abbreviated DAPT versus standard DAPT in patients with HBR was generally maintained because this strategy did not significantly increase ischemic events in most studies. Evidence supporting the use of potent P2Y12 inhibitors, such as ticagrelor or prasugrel, with or without aspirin, in patients with CCS is limited because their benefits have not been clearly established or are offset by increased bleeding events.

In patients requiring SAPT after completing DAPT for PCI, either aspirin or clopidogrel can be given for long-term secondary prevention. Growing evidence suggests that clopidogrel is not only a promising alternative to aspirin but may also be more effective and safer than aspirin after DAPT for PCI, especially in the Korean population.

##### 2. Anticoagulants

Anticoagulants are not recommended in patients with CCS unless there is an established indication for anticoagulation, regardless of revascularization status. The use of direct oral anticoagulants or vitamin K antagonists, and the choice between them, should be based on clinical guidelines for atrial fibrillation, venous thromboembolism, or cardiac surgery. In contemporary practice, the standard antithrombotic regimen for patients undergoing PCI who have an indication for anticoagulation is triple therapy with aspirin, clopidogrel, and an anticoagulant for up to 7 days, followed by double antithrombotic therapy with clopidogrel and an anticoagulant for 6–12 months. Lifelong anticoagulant monotherapy is recommended thereafter. The duration of these antithrombotic strategies should be individualized according to ischemic and bleeding risk.

#### Evidence from Korea

The HOST-EXAM trial evaluated the optimal single-antiplatelet strategy in patients undergoing PCI who remained free of cardiovascular events during the DAPT period. Clopidogrel monotherapy significantly reduced the risk of the composite of all-cause death, non-fatal MI, stroke, readmission due to ACS, and Bleeding Academic Research Consortium (BARC) type 3 or greater bleeding compared with aspirin monotherapy (5.7% vs. 7.7%; HR, 0.73; 95% CI, 0.59–0.90; p=0.0035). The results were consistent for both thrombotic and bleeding events and were maintained at 5.8 years in the extended follow-up study and at 10 years in the trial population. The SMART-CHOICE 3 trial compared the efficacy and safety of clopidogrel versus aspirin monotherapy in patients undergoing PCI who completed the standard duration of DAPT and were at high ischemic risk. The primary outcome was a composite hard endpoint that included all-cause death, MI, and stroke. In this study, clopidogrel monotherapy significantly reduced the primary outcome compared with aspirin monotherapy, without increasing bleeding events (4.4% vs. 6.6%; HR, 0.71; 95% CI, 0.54–0.93; p=0.013), providing additional evidence. Recently, the TAILORED-CHIP trial reported that a tailored antiplatelet strategy with early escalation before 6 months (ticagrelor 60 mg twice daily plus aspirin) and late de-escalation thereafter (clopidogrel monotherapy), compared with aspirin plus clopidogrel for 12 months, did not decrease the risk of the composite of death from any cause, MI, stroke, stent thrombosis, unplanned urgent revascularization, and BARC type 2, 3, or 5 bleeding among high-risk patients undergoing complex PCI (10.5% vs. 8.8%; HR, 1.19; 95% CI, 0.90–1.58; p=0.21).

The EPIC-CAD and ADAPT AF-DES trials compared anticoagulation monotherapy with dual antithrombotic therapy, consisting of an anticoagulant plus an antiplatelet drug, in patients with atrial fibrillation and CCS and in those with atrial fibrillation and previous PCI, respectively. In these trials, anticoagulation monotherapy showed favorable clinical outcomes in terms of efficacy, safety, or both compared with dual antithrombotic therapy.

#### Recommendations

- • SAPT with aspirin, or clopidogrel as an alternative to aspirin, is recommended in patients with CCS to reduce the risk of cardiovascular events.
- • Aspirin is recommended after CABG for secondary prevention.
- • DAPT with aspirin and clopidogrel is generally recommended for 6 months after PCI. This duration should be individualized according to ischemic and bleeding risk.
- • After completion of DAPT for PCI, SAPT with either aspirin or clopidogrel is recommended for secondary prevention. Although aspirin has long been recommended worldwide as standard SAPT after DAPT, recent randomized controlled trial data demonstrated the superiority of clopidogrel over aspirin in the Korean population.
- • In patients with CCS and an indication for anticoagulation, the standard antithrombotic treatment is triple therapy comprising aspirin, clopidogrel, and an anticoagulant for up to 7 days, followed by double therapy with clopidogrel and an anticoagulant for 6–12 months and lifelong anticoagulant monotherapy thereafter.
- • Routine anticoagulation is not recommended in patients with CCS unless there is an established indication for anticoagulation.
- • The addition of low-dose rivaroxaban, at the vascular dose of 2.5 mg twice daily, to aspirin is reasonable for long-term reduction of cardiovascular events in patients with CCS who do not have an indication for anticoagulation or DAPT and who are at high risk of recurrent ischemic events but at low-to-moderate bleeding risk.

### Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol-lowering drugs

#### General principles and guideline summary

Statin therapy, preferably high-intensity statin therapy, is a cornerstone of LDL-C-lowering treatment to reduce the risk of cardiovascular events in patients with CCS. However, statin therapy alone is frequently insufficient to achieve LDL-C targets, and the use of high-intensity statins remains suboptimal, potentially because of dose-dependent side effects. To overcome these limitations, non-statin LDL-C-lowering drugs, such as ezetimibe, PCSK9 inhibitors, or bempedoic acid, are recommended for patients who do not achieve LDL-C targets despite statin therapy or who cannot tolerate statin therapy.

Ezetimibe is a representative non-statin LDL-C-lowering drug that lowers LDL-C by 10–20%. Although the absolute risk reduction is modest, combination therapy with ezetimibe and statins has been shown in randomized controlled trials to improve cardiovascular outcomes. Nonetheless, because the benefit of combination therapy was observed in studies that showed greater LDL-C reduction, the role of ezetimibe should not be fixed but should instead be considered part of a comprehensive LDL-C-lowering strategy for secondary prevention. The 2 monoclonal antibodies used for PCSK9 inhibition, evolocumab and alirocumab, effectively lower LDL-C levels, and their clinical effectiveness has been demonstrated in cardiovascular outcome trials. Inclisiran, a small interfering ribonucleic acid used for PCSK9 inhibition, also lowers LDL-C by 25–60%; however, cardiovascular outcomes data are still lacking. High cost and injectable administration are major barriers to the use of PCSK9 inhibitors. Bempedoic acid significantly lowers LDL-C by 8–23% and subsequently reduces cardiovascular events. These non-statin agents remain second-line therapies because of limitations in the evidence base, administration route, cost, or efficacy. Single or combined use of these drugs should be considered in addition to statin therapy when indicated.

#### Evidence from Korea

In the RACING trial, moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe combination therapy (rosuvastatin 10 mg with ezetimibe 10 mg) was noninferior to high-intensity statin monotherapy (rosuvastatin 20 mg) in patients with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease for reducing cardiovascular events, defined as the composite of cardiac death, coronary or peripheral revascularization, hospitalization for cardiovascular events, or non-fatal stroke, during 3-year follow-up (9.1% vs. 9.9%; absolute difference: −0.78%; prespecified noninferiority margin: 2.0%). The LODESTAR trial showed that a treat-to-target strategy, with an LDL-C target of 50–70 mg/dL, was noninferior to standard high-intensity statin therapy, consisting of rosuvastatin 20 mg or atorvastatin 40 mg, in patients with CAD at 3-year follow-up for ischemic events, including death, MI, stroke, or coronary revascularization (8.1% vs. 8.7%; p<0.001 for noninferiority).

#### Recommendations

- • Maximally tolerated statin therapy is recommended in patients with CCS regardless of revascularization strategy.
- • Non-statin LDL-C-lowering drugs, including ezetimibe, PCSK9 inhibitors, or bempedoic acid, are recommended for patients who do not achieve LDL-C targets despite maximally tolerated statin therapy.

### Renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibitors/angiotensin receptor neprilysin inhibitors

#### General principles and guideline summary

The use of an ACE inhibitor, or an ARB as an alternative to an ACE inhibitor, reduces cardiovascular events among patients with hypertension, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, or an ejection fraction of 40% or less. The benefit of an ACE inhibitor or ARB in patients with CCS without hypertension, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, or reduced ejection fraction has not been established. Sacubitril/valsartan is more effective than an ACE inhibitor in reducing the risk of hospitalization for heart failure and cardiovascular death in patients with an ejection fraction of 35% or less. The cardiovascular benefit of sacubitril/valsartan in patients with CCS and an ejection fraction greater than 35% remains unclear.

#### Recommendations

- • An ACE inhibitor, or an ARB as an alternative, is recommended in patients with CCS who have hypertension, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, or an ejection fraction of 40% or less.
- • Sacubitril/valsartan is recommended over an ACE inhibitor or ARB in patients with CCS and an ejection fraction of 40% or less.

### Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists

#### General principles and guideline summary

SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs are preferred glucose-lowering medications in patients with CCS and type 2 diabetes because their cardiovascular benefits appear to be independent of their glucose-lowering effects. Because SGLT2 inhibitors also reduce the risk of worsening heart failure or cardiovascular death in patients with heart failure across the ejection fraction spectrum, they are a drug class of choice in patients with CCS who have heart failure, type 2 diabetes, or both. GLP-1 RAs significantly reduce major cardiovascular events in patients with obesity or overweight without type 2 diabetes; however, injectable administration limits broad implementation.

#### Recommendations

- • SGLT2 inhibitors are recommended in patients with CCS who have heart failure, type 2 diabetes, or both.
- • GLP-1 RAs are recommended in patients with CCS and type 2 diabetes.
- • GLP-1 RAs should be considered in patients with CCS who have obesity or overweight.

### Anti-inflammatory agents

#### General principles and guideline summary

High-dose anti-interleukin-1 beta monoclonal antibody therapy was effective in reducing the risk of cardiovascular events, but infection risk and cost remain barriers to its use. Methotrexate did not show cardiovascular benefit. Although early randomized trials, such as COLCOT and LoDoCo2, suggested that low-dose colchicine may reduce cardiovascular events, subsequent trials have yielded neutral results, raising uncertainty regarding its overall efficacy in cardiovascular prevention.

#### Recommendations

- • Low-dose colchicine (0.5 mg daily in Korea) can be considered to reduce the risk of MI, stroke, and need for revascularization.
