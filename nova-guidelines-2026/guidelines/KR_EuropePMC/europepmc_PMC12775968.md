# Clinical Practice Guidelines of the Korean Society for Vascular Surgery for the Management of Abdominal Aortic Aneurysms: Focused on 18 Key Clinical Questions

## Abstract

These clinical practice guidelines (CPG) were developed by the Korean Society for Vascular Surgery to provide evidence-based recommendations for the management and post-treatment surveillance of abdominal aortic aneurysms (AAAs). The guidelines were developed by a multidisciplinary guideline working committee of 19 members, including vascular surgeons and methodological experts. A total of 18 key clinical questions focusing on AAA management and surveillance after treatment were selected. Based on systematic reviews and meta-analyses of the available literature, literature searches were performed in MEDLINE (via PubMed), EMBASE, Cochrane Central, and KoreaMed up to February 3, 2022. Additional searches for two questions were conducted in September 2022 and December 2023. Recommendations were formulated through structured evidence review and consensus, following a Cochrane-based grading system with modifications in terminology and structure. Eighteen recommendations were made, covering surgical indications, comparison of open surgical repair and endovascular aneurysm repair, postoperative complication management, and surveillance strategies. Each recommendation was assigned a strength (strong or conditional) and direction (do or do not), along with an evidence level (high, moderate, low, or very low). These CPGs provide a structured, evidence-based framework to guide clinical decision-making in the management of AAAs.

## INTRODUCTION AND GENERAL ASPECTS

### 1) Introduction and methods

The members of the Guideline Writing Committee (GWC) were appointed by the Korean Society for Vascular Surgery (KSVS), the Korean Society for Thoracic and Cardiovascular Surgery (KTCVS), and the Korean Society of Cardiology (KSC) to represent clinicians involved in the management of individuals with abdominal aortic aneurysms (AAAs).

Prior to initiating the literature review, GWC members submitted disclosure statements outlining any relationships that could be construed as actual or potential conflicts of interest. These statements are maintained at KSVS headquarters. The final document was approved by all GWC members and the external review committee. It underwent formal external peer review and was subsequently endorsed by the KSVS.

#### ① The purpose of these guidelines

The KSVS developed clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) for the management of individuals with AAAs to support clinicians in selecting optimal therapeutic strategies. This document represents the first clinical guideline of AAAs issued by the KSVS and focuses on 18 key clinical questions.

In consideration of existing international AAA guidelines, redundant content was minimized, and emphasis was placed on issues particularly relevant to domestic contexts or insufficiently addressed in global recommendations. Authored and endorsed by the 16 GWC members, all of whom are KSVS members, these guidelines integrate the best available scientific evidence with clinical expertise. Through evidence synthesis and critical appraisal, recommendations were formulated for the evaluation and treatment of individuals with AAAs.

The primary goal of the KSVS in developing these guidelines is to standardize care and enhance clinical decision-making by presenting the most current and reliable evidence. However, individualized care must still be guided by a range of factors, including symptomatology, comorbidities, age, functional status, care setting, procedural availability, and other clinical considerations.

#### ② Methodology

(1) Strategy

The GWC convened on November 8, 2021, to formulate key questions for inclusion in the guidelines. In June 2023, GWC members reviewed and refined the phrasing and strength of each recommendation for the initial draft. When unanimous agreement could not be achieved, structured discussions were held to reach consensus. If consensus remained unattainable, decisions regarding phrasing, grading, and evidence level were resolved through a majority vote among GWC members. A final hearing with KSVS members was held in December 2023, after which the finalized guidelines were submitted in March 2025. These guidelines will be subject to ongoing updates.

(2) Literature search and selection

Committee members, with support from clinical librarians, conducted systematic literature searches in MEDLINE (via PubMed), EMBASE, Cochrane Central, and KoreaMed up to February 3, 2022. Reference tracking and hand-searching by GWC members supplemented the search for additional relevant sources. A second search, focused on questions 17 and 18, was conducted in September 2022 and December 2023. Literature selection was based on information in study titles and abstracts.

The selection criteria were as follows: (1) Language: no restrictions; (2) Level of evidence: eligible studies included randomized controlled trials (RCTs), observational studies, case series, and case reports. The level of evidence assigned in each section corresponded to the strength of available data on the given topic; (3) Sample size: greater emphasis was placed on larger studies when available.

(3) Weighing the evidence

To develop the current guidelines, members of the GWC reviewed and synthesized the selected literature. Conclusions were drawn based on the underlying scientific evidence. The recommendations provided in this document follow the Cochrane grading system. For each recommendation, both the direction and strength of the recommendation are indicated (Table 1). The quality of evidence supporting each recommendation is classified as high, moderate, low, or very low (Table 2).

## SURGICAL INDICATION

### 1) Diameter

The decision regarding the appropriate diameter for aneurysm repair is based on weighing the risk of rupture against the operative mortality associated with repair procedures. The most recent guidelines advise against elective repair of AAAs smaller than 55 mm in men and 50 mm in women, because of the very low rupture risk in this subgroup. The lower threshold in women reflects the approximately fourfold higher rupture risk for small AAAs compared with men, which is partly attributed to smaller baseline aortic diameters in women. Given that the aortic diameter in Korean individuals is generally smaller than in Western populations, racial differences should also be considered.

The GWC conducted a systematic review to synthesize current evidence objectively. The literature search on AAA treatment outcomes revealed no direct comparative evidence identifying a specific diameter threshold for intervention. Therefore, indirect conclusions were drawn from studies involving individuals with AAAs smaller than the conventional intervention threshold. Of 261 studies, plus 5 additional studies identified through reference tracking, 4 RCTs were found to directly address the relevant key questions (Table 3). Among the included studies, three applied an inclusion criterion of an aneurysm diameter less than 5.5 cm, while one used a threshold of 5.0 cm or less. Two studies evaluating open surgical repair (OSR) included only individuals with AAAs smaller than 5.5 cm. The remaining two studies assessed endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR), with one study enrolling individuals with AAAs measuring 5 cm or less.

When overall survival was compared between the early intervention and surveillance groups, no significant difference was observed across all four studies. The meta-analysis showed that the risk ratios (RRs) for overall survival in the early intervention group compared with the surveillance group were 0.98 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.97-0.99) at 1 year, 0.99 (95% CI, 0.98-1.01) at 2 years, 0.99 (95% CI, 0.97-1.02) at 3 years, and 1.01 (95% CI, 0.93-1.11) at 5 years (Fig. 1). Overall, these findings suggest no clinically meaningful long-term survival advantage with early repair compared with surveillance for small AAAs.

The studies reviewed were RCTs published between 1998 and 2011, all of which had a low risk of bias and were rated as high-level evidence. To date, in individuals with non-ruptured degenerative AAAs measuring less than 5.5 cm, neither OSR nor EVAR has demonstrated an improvement in overall survival, supporting a recommendation of selective application (Do, Conditional).

### 2) Saccular aneurysm

Saccular AAAs are classified as a distinct subtype, characterized by focal, spherical dilations involving only a segment of the aortic circumference. A widely accepted view holds that saccular AAAs may warrant earlier intervention due to their asymmetrical morphology, which is believed to increase rupture risk. However, this remains debated, as the natural history and rupture risk of saccular aneurysms are poorly defined. The most recent ESVS guidelines suggest that early intervention via open or endovascular repair may be considered for saccular AAAs. Although these guidelines imply a lower size threshold for elective repair compared to fusiform AAAs, a specific diameter cutoff is not defined.

Given this uncertainty, the GWC sought to assess prognosis and treatment outcomes in individuals with saccular AAAs treated with EVAR or OSR. A literature search examining outcomes in these individuals yielded 261 studies. After full-text review, studies relevant to this question were identified, including 2 retrospective observational studies, 3 case series reporting clinical characteristics and/or outcomes (Table 4), and 17 case reports detailing treatment outcomes (Supplementary Table 1).

Karthaus et al. analyzed 7,659 patients undergoing primary repair of degenerative AAAs in the Netherlands (2016-2018), among whom 471 (6.1%) had saccular AAAs. Saccular AAAs were treated at smaller diameters than fusiform AAAs in both elective and acute settings. Notably, 25.2% of acute saccular AAAs presented with a maximum diameter <55 mm and 8.4% with a maximum diameter <45 mm, supporting the concept that saccular AAAs may become acute at smaller diameters. The authors suggested that 45 mm may be an acceptable threshold for elective treatment.

Shang et al. identified 322 saccular aortic aneurysms in 284 patients (2003-2011), including AAAs in 24.2%. Overall, 113 patients (39.8%) underwent repair (97 after the initial CTA and 16 during observation). Among 50 patients with CT follow-up at ≥3 months, 15 (30.0%) ultimately underwent intervention. Although the growth rate was not higher than that reported for fusiform aneurysms, the authors emphasized the need for clinical and radiologic surveillance because a substantial proportion ultimately required intervention. Although this study included thoracic aortic aneurysms (TAAs) as well as AAAs, it was considered an important source for this key question, as it remains the only study describing the clinical progression of saccular aortic aneurysms. It has been cited as supporting evidence in both the SVS and ESVS guidelines.

Kristmundsson et al. analyzed 206 individuals with ruptured AAAs diagnosed by CT between 2006 and 2013 and identified 9 cases of saccular AAAs. Notably, saccular AAAs were significantly more common among ruptures with a maximum diameter <55 mm than among those ≥55 mm (5/12 vs. 4/194, P<0.01).

Regarding post-treatment outcomes, the evidence is largely limited to small case series and case reports. Mizoguchi et al. reported no mortality among six patients treated with EVAR for distal saccular AAAs. Yoshida et al. reported one mortality due to acute myocardial infarction among nine individuals who received EVAR for saccular AAAs between 2010 and 2020.

Overall, no comparative studies have demonstrated the superiority of early intervention via surgery or EVAR over conservative management for saccular AAAs. The level of evidence remains low, as available studies only represent observational data from patient groups with this condition. Nevertheless, published case-based reports suggest low perioperative complication and mortality rates after intervention, while also highlighting instances of rapid aneurysm enlargement or rupture without treatment. These findings support early consideration of elective repair. Given the uncertainty surrounding rupture potential, intervention may be justified at a lower diameter threshold compared to fusiform AAAs.

### 3) Symptomatic non-ruptured AAA: emergent repair vs. deferred repair

The optimal timing for managing symptomatic but non-ruptured AAAs remains a subject of ongoing debate, as the balance between prompt intervention and deferred elective repair is still not clearly defined. Reflecting current literature, the ESVS guidelines recommend deferred urgent repair, ideally performed under elective conditions, to reduce operative risk and optimize outcomes.

To clarify this issue and generate objective evidence, the GWC conducted a systematic review. A literature search examining outcomes of emergency versus elective surgery for symptomatic, non-ruptured AAAs yielded 7,332 studies and 35 additional studies through reference screening. Eight relevant observational studies were identified through full-text review (Table 5).

Despite decades of investigation, the timing of intervention for symptomatic, non-ruptured AAAs remains clinically controversial. For symptomatic but non-ruptured AAAs, emergency surgery is often performed with limited preoperative optimization and is associated with a higher risk of intraoperative and postoperative complications, even though the rupture risk exceeds that of asymptomatic aneurysms. A meta-analysis of eight studies demonstrated an odds ratio (OR) for mortality of 3.65 (95% CI, 2.17-6.13) for emergent repair compared with deferred treatment (Fig. 2).

Clinical studies to date have generally shown lower mortality with deferred treatment performed under elective conditions. One single-center study reported a hospital mortality rate of 9% in the emergency surgery group and 12% in the group undergoing surgery after 24 hours, with no statistically significant difference (P=0.694); no significant differences were noted in postoperative complications or total hospital stay. Overall, most included studies were consistent with contemporary US and European clinical guidelines, which recommend semi-elective or preferably elective surgery for symptomatic but non-ruptured AAAs, following completion of appropriate diagnostic tests and patient optimization. However, most studies were retrospective and conducted at a single center. Among the eight studies included, four were published prior to 2000 and focused specifically on AAA treatment. Additionally, seven studies involved fewer than 100 participants in either the intervention or control group, indicating a high risk of bias. As a result, the overall level of evidence was rated as very low.

### 4) Treatment method: EVAR vs. OSR

In Korea, the use of EVAR for AAAs has steadily increased since its introduction, while OSR has been performed at a relatively consistent annual rate. To determine the preferred treatment method in anatomically suitable individuals, a literature search examining treatment outcomes in individuals with AAAs meeting anatomical criteria identified 1,484 studies from PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Central, and KoreaMed. Following full-text review, 12 studies derived from 5 RCTs were deemed relevant (Table 6).

Meta-analysis results showed that the risk ratio (RR) for 30-day mortality after EVAR was 0.40 (95% CI, 0.22-0.74) (Fig. 3A). The RRs for mortality at 6 months, 4 years, and 8 years post-treatment were 0.60 (95% CI, 0.41-0.87), 0.93 (95% CI, 0.80-1.08), and 0.99 (95% CI, 0.91-1.09), respectively, indicating that the early survival advantage associated with EVAR diminished after 4 years (Fig. 3B-D).

In contrast, the RR for reintervention showed no significant difference at 4 years post-treatment (1.67; 95% CI, 0.62-4.50), but was significantly higher at 8 years after EVAR (2.13; 95% CI, 1.71-2.66) (Fig. 4).

The included studies had relatively large sample sizes, and the risk of bias was low in all but one trial. Therefore, the level of evidence was rated as high. Taking into account the short-term mortality benefit of EVAR, the lack of long-term survival advantage beyond 4 years, and the increased reintervention rate, the recommendation was graded as conditional (Do, Conditional).

| Recommendation 4. Consider EVAR over OSR in individuals with AAAs who meet the anatomical criteria for EVAR. Grade of recommendation: Do, Conditional Level of evidence: High |

### 5) Younger patients with long life expectancy: EVAR vs. OSR

A recent study on the long-term outcomes of AAA treatment demonstrated that the early survival benefit associated with EVAR diminishes during mid-term follow-up, with higher rates of reintervention and secondary rupture compared with OSR. Although EVAR is generally preferred in many individuals, these findings raise concerns about long-term complications and the burden of lifelong surveillance when applying EVAR in younger individuals with extended life expectancy.

The GWC sought to compare outcomes after EVAR and OSR in relation to life expectancy through a structured literature review. A search across PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Central, and KoreaMed yielded 1,484 studies, of which 28 observational studies were deemed relevant after full-text screening. As no studies directly compared outcomes by life expectancy, direct evidence on this question could not be obtained. Therefore, the mean age of the study population was used as an approximate surrogate for life expectancy. Based on this assumption and the average life expectancy in Korea, studies including participants with a mean age <70 years were considered to reflect a life expectancy of more than 10 years. Ultimately, six studies with a mean patient age <70 years and nine studies with a mean patient age ≥70 years, all reporting outcomes over a follow-up period of at least 10 years, were included (Table 7).

In the cohort with a mean age <70 years, the pooled 10-year survival rate was 79.6% (95% CI, 63.9%-91.8%) for OSR and 63.2% (95% CI, 44.4%-80.2%) for EVAR, with no statistically significant difference between the groups (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Similarly, in studies with a mean patient age ≥70 years, the pooled 10-year survival rate was 46.9% (95% CI, 31.8%-62.2%) for OSR and 40.7% (95% CI, 30.3%-51.5%) for EVAR, also without statistically significant difference (Supplementary Fig. 1B).

The pooled RRs for 10-year survival (OSR vs. EVAR) suggested numerically better outcomes with OSR in both the under-70 group (RR, 1.13; 95% CI, 0.95-1.34) (Fig. 5A) and the 70-and-older group (RR, 1.34; 95% CI, 0.98-1.82) (Fig. 5B). However, reintervention rates could not be pooled for meta-analysis because of heterogeneity in the observation periods across the included studies.

Although no statistically significant difference was observed in this systematic review, a previous meta-analysis that did not stratify by age reported that EVAR was associated with higher long-term mortality (OR, 1.19; 95% CI, 1.06-1.33), higher reintervention rates (OR, 2.12; 95% CI, 1.67-2.69), and an increased risk of post-treatment rupture (OR, 4.84; 95% CI, 2.63-8.89) compared to OSR. Therefore, despite the absence of direct evidence specifically addressing individuals with long life expectancy, the available data raise concerns regarding the long-term durability of EVAR in this population.

Because few studies tracked individuals with a mean age <70 years for more than 10 years following EVAR, and most included studies were retrospective observational designs with a high risk of bias, the level of evidence was downgraded accordingly. The final level of evidence for this recommendation was rated as very low.

### 6) Ruptured AAA: EVAR vs. OSR

Treatment outcomes for ruptured AAAs remain mixed, with some studies reporting similar long-term outcomes between OSR and EVAR, while others indicate lower mortality with EVAR. A literature search on mortality and complication outcomes after repair of ruptured AAAs, either endovascular or open, yielded 1,484 papers from PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Central, and KoreaMed, of which 47 were deemed relevant after full-text review. Among these, 22 retrospective studies and four RCTs directly compared EVAR and OSR, and three RCTs along with 10 observational comparative studies were ultimately included in the meta-analysis (Table 8). Four studies reported reintervention rates; however, due to inconsistent study designs and differing follow-up durations, these were not included in the meta-analysis.

The studies selected for meta-analysis reported complications, sequelae, and mortality among individuals who underwent EVAR or OSR for ruptured AAAs. Three RCTs provided data on 30-day mortality; two publications were derived from the Immediate Management of the Patient with Rupture: Open Versus Endovascular Repair (IMPROVE) trial. Retrospective studies comparing 30-day mortality generally favored EVAR, although these findings did not consistently reach statistical significance. The meta-analysis showed an RR for 30-day mortality of 0.87 (95% CI, 0.74-1.02), suggesting a possible benefit of EVAR.

Comparative studies of intraoperative blood transfusion volume, operative time, and hospital length of stay also favored EVAR. Because randomized trials did not provide consistent data for postoperative morbidity, the meta-analysis for morbidity relied largely on retrospective data. Although follow-up durations varied and most studies reflected mid-term outcomes, the meta-analysis showed an RR for 30-day morbidity of 0.83 (95% CI, 0.78-0.88) and an RR for follow-up mortality of 0.66 (95% CI, 0.62-0.70). These findings suggest that EVAR may offer advantages in early morbidity and overall survival.

In retrospective studies, OSR tended to be selected for individuals with short aneurysm necks, where securing a stent graft in the proximal aortic neck is technically challenging, introducing potential selection bias. However, the impact of neck length on post-EVAR prognosis remains debated. Prognosis in patients with ruptured AAAs may also vary depending on hemodynamic stability and the use of proximal aortic balloon occlusion.

Given the relatively consistent 30-day mortality findings from randomized trials and their lower risk of bias, the level of evidence was rated as moderate. For 30-day morbidity and overall mortality, despite variability in morbidity definitions and follow-up periods, consistency across studies and a relatively low risk of bias supported a moderate-to-high level of evidence. However, as most mortality outcomes were limited to less than one year, further data on long-term prognosis are needed. Based on this, the final level of evidence was classified as moderate. Although statistical significance varied, most studies favored EVAR, justifying a conditional recommendation.

### 7) Infective native AAA: EVAR vs. OSR

Infective native AAAs arise from septic emboli infiltrating the vasa vasorum, hematogenous dissemination during bacteremia, or contiguous spread from adjacent infections, leading to infectious arterial wall degeneration and aneurysm formation. The term “mycotic aneurysm,&quot; originally introduced by Osler in 1885 to describe fungus-like vegetations in endocarditis-associated aneurysms, has recently been reconsidered. The proposed term “infective native aortic aneurysm&quot; is now favored, as “mycotic&quot; misleadingly implies a fungal etiology.

Historically, OSR followed by either extra-anatomic bypass or in-situ reconstruction has been regarded as the standard treatment. However, growing evidence supports EVAR as a viable alternative, with successful outcomes increasingly reported. The GWC conducted a comprehensive literature search on outcomes following EVAR or OSR for infective native AAA. This search retrieved 1,484 articles from PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Central, and KoreaMed, with an additional 42 papers identified through reference screening. Thirteen retrospective studies were deemed relevant upon full-text review (Table 9). Among these, four studies directly comparing EVAR and OSR were included in the meta-analysis. Although follow-up periods for mortality and survival varied, the meta-analysis included studies reporting 1-year mortality rates. The 1-year mortality rate was 17.3% (19/110) in the EVAR group and 26.9% (39/145) in the OSR group, yielding an OR of 0.52 (95% CI, 0.27-0.99), suggesting lower 1-year mortality after EVAR (Fig. 6).

Reinfection and reintervention were reported in individual studies; however, studies directly comparing EVAR and OSR were limited. Even among studies reporting outcomes, inconsistent data formats rendered most data unsuitable for meta-analysis. Although higher reintervention frequencies were reported after EVAR in some reports, interpretation is limited, in part due to planned OSR following EVAR in selected cases, precluding definitive conclusions regarding the inferiority of EVAR.

Traditionally, early-stage infective native aortic aneurysms were managed by complete excision of infected arterial segments with extra-anatomical reconstruction to avoid the infected field. Subsequent studies suggested that in-situ graft reconstruction may offer outcomes comparable or superior to extra-anatomic bypass, especially when combined with prolonged antibiotic therapy and adjunctive measures such as omental wrapping. Initially, EVAR use in infective native AAAs was constrained by concerns regarding persistent infection and the potential need for prolonged, and sometimes lifelong, antibiotic therapy due to retained infected tissue. However, accumulating reports have suggested that EVAR may reduce early mortality in selected high-risk or emergent presentations. Moreover, EVAR has been proposed as a bridging strategy to stabilize patients and reduce perioperative risk before definitive surgical debridement and reconstruction, when required.

In this systematic review, multiple reports demonstrated favorable initial outcomes after EVAR. Recent systematic reviews, including those addressing infective thoracic aortic aneurysms, have also indicated that endovascular treatment may not be demonstrably inferior to OSR. Although all studies included in the meta-analysis were retrospective and carried a high risk of bias, current evidence suggests that EVAR is not inferior to OSR. Moreover, given its association with improved 1-year mortality rates, EVAR may be considered as a treatment option for infective native AAAs when anatomically feasible, particularly in patients with high risk of early mortality.

## DIAGNOSIS OF POSTOPERATIVE COMPLICATIONS

### 1) Regular imaging surveillance after EVAR

The risk of graft-related complications necessitates imaging follow-up after EVAR. However, recent guidelines do not provide a single standardized protocol applicable to all patients and recommend individualized surveillance strategies. The GWC conducted a systematic review to evaluate the association between regular imaging follow-up and clinical outcomes after EVAR. A literature search on imaging surveillance after EVAR in patients with AAA yielded 1,090 papers from PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Central, and KoreaMed, with seven additional studies identified through reference screening. Of these, eight observational cohort studies directly comparing regular versus irregular follow-up were included and analyzed (Table 10). Meta-analysis demonstrated that the RR for all-cause mortality in the regular follow-up group after EVAR was 1.57 (95% CI, 1.06-2.31), and the RR for reintervention was 1.32 (95% CI, 1.14-1.52) (Fig. 7).

These observational findings should not be interpreted as evidence that irregular follow-up is beneficial. The studies included in the meta-analysis did not consistently report adherence to EVAR device instructions for use, and early post-EVAR outcomes such as endoleak were not accounted for. As retrospective cohort studies, variations existed in follow-up protocols, and the rationale for irregular follow-up in the control groups remained unclear. In addition, confounding and selection bias are likely, as higher-risk patients may undergo more intensive surveillance and undergo more reinterventions because complications are detected more frequently. Given the presence of confounding variables, selection biases, and variation in outcome assessments, the recommendation grade was assigned as conditional, and the level of evidence was rated very low.

| Recommendation 8. Consider selective application of post-EVAR imaging intervals based on the operator’s experience and the individual’s risk of complications. Grade of recommendation: Do not, Conditional Level of evidence: Very low |
|---|

### 2) Sigmoidoscopy

Colon ischemia is a severe early complication following EVAR or OSR and is associated with high morbidity and mortality. Reported prevalence varies widely and ranges from 0.5% to nearly 40% depending on rupture status and detection strategy. Because delayed detection may worsen outcomes, the GWC performed a literature review to evaluate whether routine sigmoidoscopy could reduce related morbidity or mortality. The literature search on complications after AAA repair, including colon ischemia, yielded 1,031 papers from PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Central, and KoreaMed, with 15 additional articles identified through reference screening. Among these, eight observational studies were deemed relevant based on full-text review (Table 11).

Three of the included studies were prospective. In each study, endoscopic evaluation was performed in all patients who survived to the scheduled assessment time (100/107, 105/105, and 41/41, respectively). The reported prevalence of colon ischemia was 9.0% (9 cases), 11.4% (12 cases), and 22.0% (9 cases). Surgical intervention was required in 3, 0, and 2 patients, respectively. However, none of the studies reported data on overall or disease-specific mortality.

The remaining five studies were retrospective. Nonetheless, endoscopic examinations were performed in all eligible patients at predefined time points, based on institutional surveillance protocols. The timing varied: four studies performed endoscopy within 24 hours; one study performed endoscopy within 72 hours; one study performed repeat examinations every 12 hours within 2 days; one study performed examinations on postoperative days 2 and 4; and one study performed endoscopy at 3-6 days postoperatively. Repeat examinations were conducted in 2 studies.

No studies directly compared routine versus symptom-triggered sigmoidoscopy. The reported prevalence of colon ischemia was 13.0% in studies evaluating only OSR (excluding ruptured AAA), 9.0% in studies including some ruptured AAA with OSR only, and 11.4% in studies involving EVAR with some ruptured cases. Among studies restricted to ruptured AAA, the prevalence of ischemia varied: 22.0%, 32.8%, and 38.7% in OSR-only studies; 22.2% in EVAR-only cohorts; and 32.3% in studies primarily using EVAR with some OSR cases.

The meta-analysis estimated an overall prevalence of colon ischemia after AAA repair of 21.3% (95% CI, 13.6-30.2%), with 30.7% (95% CI, 25.1-36.6%) in ruptured AAA and 13.0% in non-ruptured AAA (Supplementary Fig. 2). The overall prevalence of ischemia requiring open surgery was 5.5% (95% CI, 1.6-11.1%), including 11.0% (95% CI, 7.3-15.3%) in ruptured AAA and 1.0% in non-ruptured cases.

Given the absence of controlled comparisons, there is insufficient evidence that routine sigmoidoscopy improves overall morbidity or mortality. Therefore, selective sigmoidoscopy guided by the individual's clinical course and symptoms is reasonable. In ruptured AAA, because the risk of colon ischemia is substantially higher, vigilant monitoring and a lower threshold for endoscopic evaluation are warranted.

The level of evidence was rated very low due to the absence of controlled or randomized studies and the potential for bias related to rupture status and baseline patient characteristics.

| Recommendation 9. Consider selective use of sigmoidoscopy to diagnose colon ischemia following OSR of AAAs, guided by the individual’s clinical course. Grade of recommendation: Do, Conditional Level of evidence: Very low |

### 3) Regular imaging surveillance after OSR

Imaging surveillance after OSR aims to detect asymptomatic complications such as para-anastomotic aneurysms (PAA), recurrent aneurysm, late synchronous aneurysms, or disease progression. To date, no high-level evidence supports specific time intervals for surveillance.

A literature search on late complications after abdominal aortic surgery yielded 4,618 papers from major databases, of which 21 relevant observational studies were reviewed (Supplementary Table 2). Among these, four retrospective studies reporting the incidence and timing of PAA following OSR were included in the meta-analysis. The estimated cumulative detection rates of PAA through regular imaging surveillance were 0.6% at 5 years (95% CI, 0-2.4%), 2.2% at 10 years (95% CI, 0.1-6.3%), and 4.7% at 15 years (95% CI, 0.6-12.1%) (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Because all included studies were retrospective and generally lacked a comparator group with irregular imaging intervals, they provide limited evidence for evaluating the true utility of routine surveillance. Accordingly, the level of evidence was rated as very low. CT was the most commonly used follow-up imaging modality, which carries risks such as contrast-induced nephropathy and cumulative radiation exposure. Therefore, imaging surveillance after OSR should be selectively applied based on patient-specific risk factors and surgeon judgment.

## TREATMENT OF POSTOPERATIVE COMPLICATIONS

### 1) Prosthetic graft infection

Prosthetic graft infection following OSR or EVAR is a serious postoperative complication associated with poor outcomes, including pseudoaneurysm formation, aorto-enteric fistula, septic shock, life-threatening hemorrhage, and death. A broad spectrum of bacterial and fungal organisms can cause graft infections, although Gram-positive cocci and enterococci are the most frequently isolated pathogens. Highly virulent organisms, such as Staphylococcus aureus or Pseudomonas aeruginosa, are associated with worse prognosis and higher reinfection rates. In contrast, less virulent skin flora, including Staphylococcus epidermidis and corynebacteria, are typically linked to milder clinical courses. Diagnosis should follow the Management of Aortic Graft Infection Collaboration (MAGIC) criteria, and microbiologic confirmation of the causative pathogen should be pursued whenever possible.

Historically, total graft explantation followed by extra-anatomic bypass was considered the standard treatment due to concerns about persistent infection. This approach was supplemented by long-term systemic antibiotic therapy, typically at least 6 weeks but often extended to 3 to 6 months, with some patients requiring lifelong oral antibiotic suppression. However, given the high mortality and amputation rates associated with total graft excision and extra-anatomic bypass, there has been a paradigm shift toward alternative strategies, including in situ revascularization, graft excision without revascularization, and partial graft resection. In individuals with high operative risk, conservative approaches with graft preservation and adjunctive procedures such as percutaneous drainage or omentopexy may be appropriate.

The GWC conducted a systematic review to compare mortality outcomes between graft removal and conservative management with graft preservation. The search identified 6,719 publications from PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Central, and KoreaMed. After full-text screening, 22 relevant observational studies and one case series were included (Supplementary Table 3). Among these, four retrospective studies reporting outcomes for both graft excision and conservative management were included in the meta-analysis (Supplementary Table 3). It should be noted that the included cohorts were heterogeneous, and some studies also included non-AAA indications (e.g., AIOD) and complex presentations (e.g., aorto-enteric fistula), limiting direct generalizability to all patients after AAA repair.

The meta-analysis showed that within 30 days postoperatively, the pooled RR for mortality in patients undergoing graft removal compared with those managed with graft preservation and antibiotic therapy was 0.98 (95% CI, 0.40-2.38). At 1 year, the RR increased to 3.44 (95% CI, 1.60-7.42) (Fig. 8). Due to the small sample sizes across included studies, pooled mortality estimates were also calculated. Within 30 days, the estimated mortality was 17.4% (95% CI, 12.9%-22.3%) in the graft removal group and 7.1% (95% CI, 0%-31.5%) in the graft-preservation with antibiotic therapy group. The estimated cumulative mortality at 1, 2, and 5 years was 28.7%, 36.6%, and 51.8%, respectively, for the graft removal group, and 16.1%, 18.5%, and 50.0%, respectively, for the graft-preservation with antibiotic therapy group (Supplementary Fig. 4). These trends were generally consistent with the direction of effect observed in the meta-analysis, although estimates were imprecise.

Given potential differences in baseline characteristics and center-specific outcomes between treatment strategies, studies were stratified into Group A (reporting outcomes for graft removal only) and Group B (reporting outcomes for both graft removal and graft preservation). The 30-day mortality was 18.1% (95% CI, 14.0%-22.6%) in Group A and 33.5% (95% CI, 12.5%-55.4%) in Group B. One-year mortality was 27.3% (95% CI, 18.4%-37.1%) in Group A and 71.1% (95% CI, 28.1%-99.9%) in Group B. These differences may reflect case-mix severity and/or center-level variation. However, as some studies reported no mortality in either treatment arm and CIs were wide in two studies, it is difficult to determine a consistent effect direction. Therefore, these findings do not provide strong evidence for the superiority of graft removal. Furthermore, due to the small sample sizes and retrospective design of the included studies, the overall quality of evidence was downgraded to very low.

In prosthetic aortic graft infections, the general principle of foreign body infection management—device removal—carries substantial operative risk. As such, a patient-centered strategy is essential, considering clinical status, anatomical factors, and infection extent (e.g., graft-enteric fistula), along with institutional experience. If the graft is retained, prolonged antibiotic therapy is necessary, and adjunctive procedures such as drainage should be considered when indicated. As a result, the recommendation strength was rated as Conditional.

### 2) Type 1 or 3 endoleaks

Endoleaks are defined as persistent blood flow within the aneurysm sac following EVAR. Management should be guided by the endoleak type and its associated risk of sac rupture. Type 1 endoleak represents direct pressurization of the aneurysm sac due to an inadequate seal at the proximal (type 1a) or distal (type 1b) attachment zone of the stent graft. Type 3 endoleak results from component separation or fabric disruption of the endograft. Type 1 and type 3 endoleaks are considered the most dangerous, as they allow systemic pressure to act directly on the aneurysm wall, increasing the risk of secondary rupture.

The GWC aimed to identify direct evidence regarding the need for reintervention or surgical repair in patients with type 1 or type 3 endoleaks. The available evidence consisted predominantly of small retrospective series, most lacking control groups. Several studies reported favorable outcomes following intervention (endovascular or open repair), but no RCTs compared intervention versus conservative follow-up in these cases.

Some retrospective studies described patients who did not undergo intervention, excluding those whose endoleaks resolved spontaneously. However, most individuals with persistent endoleaks eventually required intervention. The rationale for non-intervention was often unclear, limiting interpretability. Reported reasons included patient refusal, clinical judgment by the treating physician, or minimal endoleak volume.

The literature search on type 1 and type 3 endoleaks following EVAR yielded 5,212 articles from PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Central, and KoreaMed. Among these, 27 retrospective studies were deemed relevant (Supplementary Table 4). Of these, 11 studies provided extractable data on rupture, aneurysm sac size, or mortality and were included in the meta-analysis. Most were non-comparative studies without control groups and were used to estimate the pooled event rates among cohorts managed with intervention versus observation.

Pooled estimates showed an all-cause mortality rate of 3.6% (95% CI, 0.5-8.6%) in the intervention group (endovascular or surgical treatment) and 36.7% (95% CI, 4.1-76.2%) in the non-intervention group (Supplementary Fig. 5). Aneurysm-related mortality was 3.6% (95% CI, 0.5-8.6%) in the intervention group and 1.1% (95% CI, 0-23.3%) in the non-intervention group. Aneurysm sac enlargement occurred in 13.2% (95% CI, 3.6-26.4%) of individuals receiving intervention and in 28.2% (95% CI, 8.9-51.2%) of those not treated. Rupture was observed in 3.2% (95% CI, 0-9.1%) of the intervention group and 4.4% (95% CI, 0-45.9%) of the non-intervention group.

According to published reviews and expert consensus, persistent type 1 endoleak increases the risk of aneurysm rupture. Therefore, reintervention (endovascular or surgical) is generally advised. Endovascular options include balloon angioplasty, graft extension, intravascular stapling, and embolization, with conversion to open repair considered when endovascular correction is unsuccessful. Type 3 endoleaks result from defects at modular junctions or fabric disruption, typically caused by deployment errors, inadequate overlap, stent migration, or device fatigue. Similar to type 1 endoleaks, they increase intra-aneurysmal pressure and rupture risk. Prompt endovascular repair is advised, with open conversion considered if endovascular correction fails.

Because no randomized or case-control studies were available and the evidence consisted primarily of retrospective series, the recommendation was rated as conditional. The quality of evidence was downgraded to very low, primarily due to potential biases, including variability in intervention timing and inconsistent reporting of endoleak volume across studies.

### 3) Type 2 endoleak

Type 2 endoleaks, which arise from retrograde flow through collateral vessels, are the most frequently encountered endoleak following EVAR. Although they are generally considered benign compared to other endoleak types, their clinical relevance remains debated. Some practitioners favor a conservative approach, recommending intervention only when there is clear evidence of aneurysm sac expansion, whereas others advocate earlier treatment to prevent potential complications, including rupture. Spontaneous resolution is common: approximately 60% of early postoperative type 2 endoleaks resolve within six months, and many of the remainder regress over time. Although the relationship between sac enlargement and rupture risk in type 2 endoleaks remains inconclusive, sac rupture has been reported. The decision to intervene for persistent type 2 endoleaks is typically based on diameter increase and growth rate (e.g., ≥5 mm per SVS guidelines; ≥10 mm per ESVS guidelines), as well as inflow and outflow vessel size, patency, and symptom presence.

The GWC evaluated whether reintervention or surgery could reduce rupture risk in patients with type 2 endoleaks associated with ≥10 mm of aneurysm sac enlargement. The literature search on type 2 endoleaks following EVAR yielded 5,170 articles from PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Central, and KoreaMed, with an additional 50 articles identified through reference screening. From these, 172 studies were evaluated for eligibility based on predefined criteria. A total of 40 studies met inclusion criteria, all of which were retrospective observational studies (Supplementary Table 5). None of these studies directly compared aneurysm sac growth or rupture risk between patients managed with surveillance alone and those undergoing reintervention, making it difficult to robustly evaluate the RRs and benefits of conservative monitoring versus intervention.

Nonetheless, rupture events associated with sac enlargement due to type 2 endoleaks have been documented. Given the high mortality rate associated with aneurysm rupture, treatment can be considered for type 2 endoleaks accompanied by sac growth. Although not formally included in the analysis, literature reviews examining post-EVAR aortic rupture have reported high mortality (119 deaths among 231 cases, 51.5%).

Evidence regarding the timing of treatment for type 2 endoleaks with aneurysm sac enlargement remains limited. Most studies defined sac enlargement as an increase of ≥5 mm, and data are sparse regarding outcomes using larger thresholds (e.g., ≥10 mm) or absolute sac diameter criteria. Similarly, the optimal treatment strategy remains uncertain. The 40 studies included for analysis were exclusively retrospective, each reporting on different embolization techniques and materials; therefore no single approach could be recommended based on comparative evidence.

Meta-analysis showed that sac enlargement persisted in 22.6% (95% CI, 13.6%-33.0%) after embolization, with additional endovascular procedures required in 20.9% (95% CI, 13.8%-28.8%), surgical reintervention in 6.1% (95% CI, 2.7%-10.4%), rupture in 0.5% (95% CI, 0%-1.7%), and aneurysm-related mortality in 0.3% (95% CI, 0%-1.6%) (Supplementary Fig. 6). These findings underscore the limitations of current procedural treatments and emphasize the importance of long-term imaging surveillance after intervention.

Reports on surgical treatment for type 2 endoleaks were limited to four studies describing vessel ligation without stent graft explantation. The pooled incidence of aneurysm sac enlargement and additional intervention was 3.8% (95% CI, 0%-13.3%) and 0.1% (95% CI, 0%-5.2%), respectively. However, although only two studies reported major surgical complications, the pooled estimate of major complications was relatively high at 18.0% (95% CI, 5.5%-34.4%). Given the rupture risk associated with type 2 endoleaks and the observation that further sac enlargement was not reported in 77.4% of patients after embolization and 96.2% after surgical vessel closure, treatment can be considered for clinically meaningful sac growth, including growth ≥10 mm, after EVAR.

Because no RCTs or comparative studies were available and the evidence consisted only of retrospective data, the recommendation was graded as conditional. The level of evidence was rated as very low due to concerns about bias, including variability in treatment timing, endoleak volume, and baseline aneurysm size across studies.

### 4) Type 5 endoleak

Type 5 endoleak, also known as endotension, refers to progressive aneurysm sac enlargement without any demonstrable endoleak on imaging. The underlying mechanism is poorly understood; one proposed explanation is increased graft permeability, which permits transmission of systemic pressure to the aneurysm sac wall and results in continued sac pressurization and expansion. It is important to exclude other types of endoleak, as the source may not be detectable using conventional imaging modalities. Similar to type 2 endoleaks, a selective treatment strategy is advised for patients with type 5 endoleak exhibiting significant sac enlargement (ESVS guideline: ≥10 mm).

A literature search was conducted to evaluate whether reintervention or surgery is warranted in such cases. The search included PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Central, and KoreaMed and yielded 5,215 articles. An additional 10 articles were identified through reference screening, of which seven observational studies met inclusion criteria (Supplementary Table 6). Four studies were single-center reports, with reported rates of type 5 endoleak ranging from 1% to 12% among patients undergoing EVAR depending on definitions, devices, and follow-up duration. One study investigating ruptured aneurysms identified nine cases of type 5 endoleak among 270 patients (3.3%).

In a large cohort study involving 14,817 patients, individuals without detectable endoleaks but with aneurysm sac enlargement had significantly lower survival compared to those without sac growth (HR, 1.6; 95% CI, 1.3-2.1; P<0.001). Among patients without endoleaks, 5-year and 10-year survival rates were 87% and 78%, respectively, for those with sac regression, 86% and 69% for those with stable sac size, and 79% and 61% for those with sac enlargement. Another large cohort study of 6,337 patients without endoleaks stratified individuals by changes in aneurysm sac size during follow-up compared with baseline preoperative measurements. Freedom from rupture at 7 years was significantly lower in patients with sac enlargement ≥8 mm. While the risk of rupture in this group was <1% during the first 4 years, it increased to 7.5% at 5 years and 13.6% at 7 years.

In a single-institution series of 97 endovascular repairs, 12 cases of type 5 endoleak were reported. Three patients were managed conservatively, and nine underwent relining. Notably, no deaths or aneurysm ruptures occurred in either group during follow-up.

Among the seven identified studies, six did not directly compare outcomes between conservative management and reintervention/surgery for type 5 endoleak. One single-center study divided patients by treatment strategy, but because no deaths or ruptures were observed, meta-analysis was not feasible, and the level of evidence was assessed as very low.

Overall, available evidence suggests that patients with aneurysm sac enlargement without a visible endoleak may have worse survival and a higher risk of rupture than those without sac growth. Therefore, continued imaging surveillance is warranted in patients with sac enlargement without a demonstrable endoleak, and individualized reintervention or surgical repair should be considered when rupture risk is judged to be clinically significant. In a survey of external experts, 96.6% (28/29) supported these CPGs, and 100% (29/29) endorsed the assigned recommendation grade. Nevertheless, objective criteria for determining when to initiate reintervention or surgery in cases of aneurysm sac enlargement remain insufficiently defined.

## MISCELLANEOUS

### 1) Preservation of internal iliac artery (IIA)

The IIA provides critical blood supply to pelvic structures, including the sigmoid colon, rectum, ovaries, uterus, sacrum, and gluteal muscles. It consists of multiple branches, and collateral circulation is maintained via an extensive pelvic arterial network, which includes contributions from the IMA and the deep femoral artery. Depending on the status of this pelvic collateral network, sacrifice of one or both IIAs can result in varying degrees of ischemic complications, such as buttock claudication, colonic ischemia, pelvic necrosis, or erectile dysfunction.

A literature review was performed to determine whether preservation of at least one IIA is necessary during AAA repair. The search identified 1,822 articles across PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Central, and KoreaMed, focusing on outcomes following IIA embolization or ligation during EVAR or OSR. Among these, 12 observational studies reporting complications from unilateral or bilateral IIA occlusion were included in the meta-analysis (Table 12).

The meta-analysis demonstrated that the risk of buttock claudication was higher after bilateral IIA occlusion than after unilateral occlusion (RR, 1.90; 95% CI, 1.52–2.38). The RR for ischemic colitis was markedly elevated (RR, 16.81; 95% CI, 4.31-65.53), and the RR for erectile dysfunction was 2.43 (95% CI, 1.72-3.42) (Fig. 9). Additionally, neurological deficits suggestive of spinal cord ischemia were observed in two patients across the included studies, both of whom had bilateral IIA occlusion.

In the early 2000s, studies primarily focused the safety of IIA occlusion during EVAR or OSR, regardless of laterality. From the late 2000s onward, research increasingly examined outcomes based on occlusion techniques (e.g., Amplatzer plug or coil embolization) and timing, including preoperative versus intraoperative occlusion. One study directly compared unilateral and bilateral IIA occlusion. In that study, no cases of buttock necrosis or ischemic colitis requiring abdominal surgery were reported in either group. However, persistent buttock claudication occurred in 12% of patients with unilateral occlusion and 11% with bilateral occlusion. Erectile dysfunction was reported in 9% of the unilateral group and 13% of the bilateral group. Mild lower limb neurological deficits were observed in 1.5% of patients with unilateral occlusion.

The individual studies included in the meta-analysis were small and predominantly retrospective and observational, introducing a high risk of bias. Significant heterogeneity in the effect sizes for buttock claudication and erectile dysfunction further limited confidence in the pooled estimates. Additionally, the low incidence and wide CIs for ischemic colitis made it difficult to draw strong conclusions. As such, the overall level of evidence was rated as low. Considering that buttock claudication, erectile dysfunction, and ischemic colitis often improve over time and that neurological complications are rare and typically mild, the recommendation grade was set as conditional.

### 2) IMA reimplantation

Although the overall incidence of bowel ischemia following OSR is relatively low (reported between 2.1% and 3.6%) ischemic colitis remains one of the most severe complications is associated with high mortality. One proposed mechanism is impaired colonic perfusion due to IMA ligation. The colon is typically supported by a rich collateral network connecting the celiac axis, superior mesenteric artery (SMA), and IMA. These anastomoses can enlarge and form the marginal artery of Drummond, which may supply the entire colon. As a result, many patients remain asymptomatic even in the presence of significant atherosclerosis affecting one or more of these arterial origins. However, the splenic flexure and sigmoid colon are particularly vulnerable to ischemia. Moreover, anatomical studies have shown that the marginal artery of Drummond is absent in approximately 5% of individuals and underdeveloped in up to 50%. Therefore, patients in these subsets are at increased risk for ischemic colitis following IMA ligation.

The spectrum of ischemic colonic injury ranges from superficial mucosal damage to transmural bowel necrosis. Surgical intervention is required in approximately 20% of cases, with mortality rates reaching as high as 67%. To mitigate this risk, IMA reimplantation has been advocated in selected patients based on intraoperative assessments, including colon viability, Doppler signal evaluation, IMA back-bleeding, and IMA stump pressure measurement. However, despite its theoretical rationale, evidence supporting the efficacy of IMA reimplantation in preventing ischemic colitis remains inconclusive.

A literature search on outcomes following OSR yielded 1,401 articles from PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Central, and KoreaMed. Of these, seven were deemed relevant through full-text review, comprising four retrospective observational studies, one prospectively assessed study with random assignment, and two case reports (Supplementary Table 7).

One study assessing colonic ischemia as a primary outcome reported an adjusted OR of 1.41 (P<0.05) for colonic ischemia when the IMA was reimplanted, identifying advanced age, congestive heart failure, aneurysm rupture, blood transfusion, transperitoneal approach, upper abdominal aortic clamping, and IMA reimplantation itself as independent risk factors. Another study reported a higher incidence of colonic ischemia in the IMA reimplantation group than in the ligation group (8.6% vs. 2.4%, P=0.027). Conversely, some reports suggested that IMA reimplantation may help prevent colonic ischemia in selected settings where collateral supply is inadequate. However, a meta-analysis of five studies evaluating in-hospital colonic ischemia found no statistically significant difference between IMA reimplantation and ligation (RR, 1.77; 95% CI, 0.72-4.40) (Fig. 10).

Regarding short-term survival, two studies reported 30-day mortality. In one study, 30-day mortality was 4.2% with reimplantation and 6.9% with ligation (RR, 0.60; 95% CI, 0.15-2.30; P=0.511). In another study, 30-day mortality was 5.7% with reimplantation and 3.1% with ligation (P=0.320), again showing no statistically significant difference. Operative duration associated with IMA reimplantation was reported in one study and was significantly longer with reimplantation (319.7±117.8 minutes) than with ligation (242.4±109.3 minutes) (P<0.001).

Overall, evidence remains insufficient to conclude that IMA reimplantation independently reduces colonic ischemia or improves survival. Colonic perfusion is primarily supported by both the SMA and IMA, which are interconnected via the arc of Riolan. Moreover, when both IIAs are patent, additional pelvic collaterals may also contribute. In this context, routine IMA reimplantation may prolong operative time without proven benefit. Several studies indicate that postoperative bowel ischemia is more strongly influenced by hemodynamic instability (including cardiogenic shock) than by IMA reimplantation. Other contributing factors include advanced age, congestive heart failure, rupture status, transfusion volume, surgical approach, and duration of supraceliac clamping.

Therefore routine IMA reimplantation is not recommended. However, selective IMA reimplantation may be considered based on preoperative imaging (collateral adequacy and patency of relevant collaterals), hemodynamic status, and intraoperative findings such as IMA back-bleeding and colonic perfusion during aortic clamping.

The level of evidence was assessed as low, given the limited comparative evidence, inclusion of studies with high risk of bias, and the predominance of retrospective designs.

### 3) Retroperitoneal approach for inflammatory AAA or horseshoe kidney (HK)

Surgical access to the aorta is broadly categorized as transperitoneal or retroperitoneal. The transperitoneal approach via a midline laparotomy remains the most commonly employed technique due to its flexibility and ease of access to abdominal organs. However, the retroperitoneal approach offers superior exposure to the juxtarenal and suprarenal aorta and may help avoid technical challenges inherent to the transperitoneal route, particularly in cases involving a hostile abdomen or a HK.

A literature review was conducted to evaluate whether the retroperitoneal approach is more appropriate for individuals with inflammatory AAA or HK. Regarding HK, the search identified 4,137 papers on surgical approaches for AAA after excluding duplicates (PubMed, EMBASE, and Cochrane Central). Of these, 117 case reports specifically described either transabdominal or retroperitoneal approaches were included (Supplementary Table 8).

A total of 220 patients were included, among whom 38 presented with ruptured aneurysms; all underwent transperitoneal repair via midline laparotomy. Among the 182 patients with non-ruptured aneurysms, four procedures were aborted because of extensive adhesions, and two cases involved aneurysm wrapping. The remaining 176 patients underwent aneurysm resection and aortic reconstruction using straight or Y-shaped prosthetic grafts. Of these, 24 procedures employed a retroperitoneal approach, including four that required thoracotomy. Notably, none of the retroperitoneal cases required division of the HK, and no perioperative deaths were reported.

Among the patients managed through the transperitoneal route, two required conversion involving midline laparotomy followed by medial retraction of the left colon and kidney to gain retroperitoneal access. In the transperitoneal group, 46 patients (30.9%, 46/149) required HK separation (excluding five who underwent unilateral nephrectomy due to renal cell carcinoma), and no complications were attributed to HK separation. Complications occurred in 23 patients (14.9%) treated via the transperitoneal approach, including renal dysfunction necessitating hemodialysis (n=6), acute myocardial infarction (n=2), intestinal ischemia (n=3), lower limb or graft occlusion (n=5), ureteral obstruction (n=1), and pneumonia (n=3). Among those who underwent a retroperitoneal approach, three patients (12.5%) experienced complications: renal dysfunction not requiring hemodialysis (n=2), and intestinal ischemia (n=1).

The mortality rate following transperitoneal approach was 3.9% (6/154), with causes including acute myocardial infarction in two patients, renal failure and coagulopathy in one patient, and unspecified causes in three. In contrast, no deaths were reported among patients treated via a retroperitoneal approach; however, the small number of retroperitoneal cases limits firm conclusions regarding comparative mortality.

With respect to surgical management of inflammatory AAAs, 4,114 publications were screened from PubMed, EMBASE, and Cochrane Central after removing duplicates. Two observational studies were eligible for meta-analysis (Supplementary Table 9).

In the study by Fiorani et al., 45 patients underwent OSR for inflammatory AAAs over a 12-year period: 39 underwent transperitoneal midline laparotomy, four underwent left retroperitoneal approach, and two underwent thoracoabdominal approach. In the transperitoneal group, intraoperative injuries occurred in two patients (left renal vein injury and duodenal injury), and postoperative complications were reported in six patients; three (7.7%) died within 1 month of surgery. In the study by Todd and DeRose, 12 patients with inflammatory AAAs underwent OSR by a single surgeon over 7 years. Five underwent transperitoneal repair, six underwent retroperitoneal repair, and one required intraoperative conversion from transperitoneal to retroperitoneal due to anatomical constraints. One duodenal injury occurred in the transperitoneal group, and no deaths were reported in either group.

The meta-analysis demonstrated an RR of 0.41 (95 % CI, 0.06-3.02) for perioperative complications after a retroperitoneal approach and an RR of 1.14 (95 % CI, 0.07-19.05) for mortality, neither reaching statistical significance (Fig. 11).

Current evidence guiding the choice of surgical access in patients with HK is limited to case reports, and no randomized or adequately powered comparative studies exist for inflammatory AAAs. Consequently, selection of a transperitoneal or retroperitoneal approach should be based on patient-specific anatomy and surgeon experience. Owing to substantial risk of bias, the overall quality of evidence was graded very low.

### 4) Permissive hypotension in ruptured AAA

The concept of permissive hypotension was first introduced by Cannon et al. in 1918 as a fluid resuscitation strategy for trauma patients with major hemorrhage, advocating maintenance of subnormotensive blood pressure during initial resuscitation. This principle has since been applied in the management of ruptured AAA (rAAA), primarily to minimize ongoing bleeding and avoid disruption of early hemostasis before proximal control is achieved.

A literature search on preoperative permissive hypotension in rAAA patients yielded 1,039 articles from PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Central, and KoreaMed. No RCTs or meta-analyses specifically addressing rAAA were available, largely due to the absence of comparative groups using normotensive resuscitation or the inability to extract mortality data. Given the logistical and ethical challenges inherent in studying critically unstable patients in prehospital or intraoperative settings, the study group recognized the limitations of conducting such trials. Therefore, trauma evidence was considered alongside the limited rAAA-specific observational data. After screening, eight studies were ultimately included (Table 13) [398–405]. Of these, two evaluated patients with rAAA, whereas the remaining six were trauma studies involving hemorrhagic shock and were used as indirect (surrogate) evidence.

Two studies specifically examined fluid resuscitation strategies in patients with rAAA. Dick et al. found that aggressive fluid resuscitation prior to proximal aortic control was associated with higher postoperative mortality, even when accounting for blood pressure as a confounding factor. In another study, Li et al. observed that both hypertension (systolic blood pressure (SBP) >140 mmHg) and severe hypotension (SBP <70 mmHg) during interhospital transfer were independently associated with increased 30-day mortality, indicating that resuscitation should focus on avoiding these extremes of blood pressure.

In trauma literature, controlled resuscitation strategies have been associated with reduced transfusion requirements and, in some analyses, lower mortality. A meta-analysis of trauma studies demonstrated lower postoperative mortality in the controlled hypotension group compared to standard resuscitation (RR, 0.76; 95% CI 0.62-0.92) (Supplementary Fig. 7). However, extrapolation to rAAA requires caution because trauma cohorts typically include younger individuals with fewer comorbidities than patients with rAAA, and the physiologic context differs substantially.

In summary, direct evidence in rAAA is limited, and available data support avoiding both severe hypotension and hypertension during initial resuscitation and transfer. Given the reliance on indirect evidence from trauma literature and the lack of robust data specifically addressing rAAA, the recommendation was graded as conditional, and the level of evidence was rated as very low.
