# Advances and unmet needs in the management of proliferative lupus nephritis: evidence, guidelines, and emerging therapies

## Abstract

Proliferative lupus nephritis is a major cause of kidney failure and premature mortality in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus. Although mycophenolate-based regimens and cyclophosphamide remain standard induction therapies, up to one-third of patients have refractory disease, and many fail to achieve a complete renal response, as demonstrated in the ASPREVA, AURORA, and BLISS-LN trials. Treatment-related toxicity—particularly from prolonged glucocorticoid exposure—contributes to cumulative organ damage. Recent guideline updates emphasize early assessment of treatment response, glucocorticoid minimization, and the selective addition of targeted therapies. Belimumab improves composite and complete renal response rates when added to standard therapy and reduces renal events, particularly in patients with proliferative histology. Voclosporin, a novel calcineurin inhibitor with predictable pharmacokinetics, increases complete renal response rates and rapidly reduces proteinuria in patients with preserved kidney function. Despite these advances, refractory disease and relapse remain common, prompting investigation of additional therapeutic pathways. Obinutuzumab, a type II anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody, demonstrated higher complete renal response rates than placebo in a phase 3 trial, albeit with increased serious infections. Anifrolumab, which targets type I interferon signaling, has shown favorable trends in reducing proteinuria and systemic disease activity in early lupus nephritis studies and is undergoing phase 3 evaluation. Newer agents, such as iscalimab, a CD40 pathway inhibitor, have reduced proteinuria in phase 2 trials. This review summarizes the epidemiology, prognostic markers, and current and emerging therapies, and proposes a practical, response-guided framework aligned with contemporary guidelines to improve kidney outcomes while limiting toxicity.

## Introduction

Proliferative lupus nephritis (LN)—defined as International Society of Nephrology/Renal Pathology Society class III or IV, with or without a membranous component—is the principal driver of kidney-related morbidity and mortality in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). LN develops in a substantial proportion of patients with SLE across diverse cohorts, and a clinically significant subset progresses to end-stage kidney disease despite treatment. The therapeutic goal is no longer limited to controlling acute inflammation but has expanded to preserving nephron mass and preventing irreversible chronic kidney damage while minimizing treatment-related harm. This paradigm shift is reflected in contemporary guideline updates, which emphasize early, measurable treatment targets and glucocorticoid-sparing strategies, and incorporate targeted therapies—such as belimumab and voclosporin—into initial treatment options for active proliferative LN.

This review focuses on the following: (1) the epidemiology and long-term prognosis of proliferative LN; (2) response-guided treatment goals and the rationale for early proteinuria reduction as a surrogate marker of outcome; (3) evidence supporting conventional induction and maintenance regimens, along with their limitations; (4) the practical implications of updated Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) and European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) recommendations; and (5) emerging therapies, including obinutuzumab, anifrolumab, and iscalimab, that aim to address persistent nonresponse and relapse. The overarching theme is that proliferative LN requires individualized, mechanism-informed combination therapy anchored by objective response assessment and strict toxicity mitigation.

## Epidemiology and disease burden

Among patients with SLE, the reported lifetime incidence of LN ranges from approximately 20% to 60%, reflecting differences in ancestry, access to care, and biopsy practices. Kidney involvement is a major predictor of SLE-related mortality and contributes disproportionately to healthcare utilization and long-term disability. Despite advances in supportive care and immunosuppressive therapy, approximately 10% of patients with LN progress to kidney failure, and many develop chronic kidney disease, with cumulative loss of renal function over time.

An important clinical observation is that substantial inflammatory kidney pathology may be present even when proteinuria is modest. Studies of patients with SLE and low-level proteinuria have shown that active urinary sediment and low protein excretion do not reliably exclude proliferative lesions on biopsy. These findings support a low threshold for kidney biopsy in patients with SLE who have urinary abnormalities, particularly when hematuria, cellular casts, or declining kidney function are accompanied by proteinuria.

## Prognosis and response-guided treatment targets

In proliferative LN, long-term outcomes are influenced by baseline kidney function, biopsy chronicity, treatment adherence, and therapy-related toxicity. However, baseline clinical and serological parameters alone are often insufficient to predict durable renal outcomes. An important advancement has been the recognition that early improvement in proteinuria is a strong predictor of favorable long-term renal prognosis. In the 10-year follow-up of the MAINTAIN Nephritis Trial, baseline variables—including serum creatinine, proteinuria, albumin, complement levels, and global disease activity indices—did not reliably distinguish between patients with favorable and unfavorable renal outcomes, whereas an early reduction in proteinuria demonstrated a high positive predictive value for preserved kidney function over a decade. These findings have clear clinical implications. Clinicians can use early proteinuria kinetics as an objective “response signal” to guide therapy continuation, intensification, or switching.

Consistent with this evidence, the EULAR recommendations define response targets that emphasize progressive reductions in proteinuria: at least a 25% reduction by 3 months, 50% reduction by 6 months, and urine protein-to-creatinine ratio (UPCR) below 500–700 mg/g by 12 months as a practical threshold for complete clinical response. Because improvements in proteinuria and kidney function occur gradually and vary among patients, response assessments should be individualized rather than strictly anchored to fixed time points. When proteinuria demonstrates a sustained downward trend and kidney function remains stable, allowing additional time for response before modifying therapy is reasonable. Together, these targets provide a standardized framework for treatment-to-target monitoring in routine practice, thereby aligning clinical decisions with established prognostic markers.

## Supportive care and background therapies

Supportive care is fundamental in all stages of LN and should be implemented alongside immunosuppressive therapy. Hydroxychloroquine is recommended for most patients with SLE and LN in the absence of contraindications, with evidence supporting improved survival, reduced damage accrual, and favorable effects on cardiovascular and thrombotic risk profiles. Mechanistically, hydroxychloroquine interferes with antigen processing within lysosomes, inhibits toll-like receptor activation by nucleic acid ligands, and attenuates the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase–stimulator of interferon genes pathway (cGAS–STING pathway), thereby reducing type I interferon production. This mechanism plausibly translates into reduced systemic immune activation.

Blood pressure control, renin-angiotensin system blockade for proteinuria, lipid management, vaccination, infection prophylaxis where appropriate, and fertility counseling are critical adjunctive measures. These interventions are not optional; they directly influence long-term kidney survival and patients’ ability to tolerate immunosuppressive therapy. A coordinated approach using rheumatology, nephrology, and—when needed—maternal-fetal medicine improves outcomes and aligns with modern, multidisciplinary care models.

## Conventional induction therapies: evidence and limitations

Glucocorticoids remain an important component of induction therapy for organ-threatening SLE because of their rapid anti-inflammatory effects. However, these benefits are counterbalanced by dose-dependent toxicity. Observational data from the Hopkins Lupus Cohort demonstrated that cumulative prednisone exposure predicts irreversible organ damage, including osteoporotic fractures, coronary artery disease, and cataracts. The 2024 KDIGO recommendations endorse 0.25–0.5 g of intravenous methylprednisolone daily for 1–3 days, followed by 0.35–1.0 mg/kg/day (maximum 80 mg/day) of oral prednisone. When a reduced-dose approach is appropriate, 0.3–0.5 mg/kg/day of prednisone for up to 4 weeks is reasonable, with tapering to ≤7.5 mg/day by 3–6 months, provided disease control is maintained.

Cyclophosphamide- and mycophenolate-based regimens remain standard options. The Euro-Lupus Nephritis Trial established that low-dose intravenous cyclophosphamide (500 mg every 2 weeks for six doses; cumulative dose approximately 3 g) is as effective as historical high-dose regimens for induction therapy, with the potential for reduced toxicity. Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) has shown an efficacy comparable to that of intravenous cyclophosphamide in large trials. In the ALMS induction study, response rates at 24 weeks were approximately 56% with MMF and 53% with intravenous cyclophosphamide, with similar secondary endpoints and safety signals. Nonetheless, these results highlight a persistent therapeutic ceiling; nearly half of the patients did not achieve an adequate response during induction, and relapses remain common during maintenance therapy.

These limitations are clinically consequential. Incomplete remission is associated with subsequent flares, progressive biopsy chronicity, and declining renal function. Additionally, conventional agents carry distinct risks, including gonadotoxicity and malignancy risks with cyclophosphamide and infectious and hematologic complications with all potent immunosuppressants. Thus, the current therapeutic challenge is to improve response depth and durability while minimizing cumulative toxicity—an impetus for targeted add-on therapies and response-guided treatment intensification.

## Maintenance therapy and relapse prevention

Maintenance therapy consolidates the induction response, reduces renal flares, and limits chronic damage. Consistent with the KDIGO and EULAR guidelines, mycophenolate-based maintenance is generally preferred after MMF induction, with azathioprine reserved for selected situations (e.g., pregnancy planning or intolerance). The 2024 KDIGO guideline recommends an initial maintenance dose of 750–1,000 mg of MMF twice daily (or 540–720 mg of mycophenolic acid twice daily) and specifies that the total duration of induction plus maintenance immunosuppression for proliferative LN should be ≥36 months. This recommendation is supported by the ALMS maintenance trial, in which MMF was superior to azathioprine for the primary endpoint of time to treatment failure over 36 months (hazard ratio, 0.44; 95% confidence interval, 0.25–0.77).

Low-dose glucocorticoids may be continued in the short term, but should be minimized; prolonged exposure above approximately 5–7.5 mg/day increases the risk of damage accrual. The optimal duration of maintenance therapy has not been precisely defined; however, continuation for several years is typical, particularly in patients with prior relapse risk factors. Importantly, relapse prevention is not solely pharmacologic; adherence support, patient education, and structured monitoring are essential.

Definitions of renal flare vary among trials but commonly include increasing proteinuria, worsening kidney function, and/or active urinary sediment. Serial monitoring of UPCR, serum creatinine (or estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR]), complement levels, and anti-double-stranded DNA titers remains standard, as relapses can occur even after apparent clinical remission. The clinical implication of guideline-endorsed response targets is that maintenance plans should be anchored to objective response milestones and adjusted when trajectories deviate (e.g., plateauing proteinuria reduction or recurrent serologic activity).

## Guideline updates: the 2024 KDIGO and 2023 EULAR implications for practice

The 2024 KDIGO update for LN represents a key shift from a “two-regimen” induction model toward multiple evidence-based combination strategies. For patients with active class III or IV LN (with or without class V), KDIGO recommends glucocorticoids in combination with one of the following: i) mycophenolic acid analogs; ii) low-dose intravenous cyclophosphamide; iii) belimumab combined with mycophenolic acid analogs or low-dose cyclophosphamide; or iv) mycophenolic acid analogs plus a calcineurin inhibitor provided kidney function is not severely impaired (suggested eGFR >45 mL/min/1.73 m2). This framework positions belimumab- and calcineurin inhibitor-based combination therapy as first-line options in appropriately selected patients.

The EULAR recommendations reflect similar principles, emphasizing treat-to-target proteinuria milestones, rapid tapering, and individualized selection of induction and maintenance strategies. From a practical standpoint, these documents support early combination therapy for patients at high risk of nonresponse (e.g., those with high disease activity, severe proteinuria, or unfavorable early trajectory), while preserving conventional regimens for lower-risk patients who show rapid early improvement. Importantly, successful implementation requires a structured monitoring strategy and early identification of patients who do not meet proteinuria targets to minimize prolonged exposure to ineffective therapy.

## Belimumab (anti–B-cell activating factor): evidence, patient selection, and limitations

Belimumab is a human IgG1λ monoclonal antibody that inhibits soluble B-lymphocyte stimulator (B-cell activating factor, BAFF), thereby reducing B-cell survival and differentiation into antibody-producing plasma cells. In BLISS-LN, a phase 3 randomized trial enrolling patients with class III–V LN and proteinuria above predefined thresholds, intravenous belimumab (10 mg/kg every 4 weeks after a loading regimen), when added to standard therapy, improved renal outcomes at 104 weeks. Compared with placebo, belimumab increased both primary efficacy renal response and complete renal response rates and reduced the risk of renal-related events or death, with an overall safety profile comparable to that of standard therapy alone.

Post hoc analyses provided additional clinically relevant insights. The overall treatment effect appeared to be driven largely by patients with a proliferative histologic component, whereas those with pure class V LN demonstrated a less consistent benefit across composite response endpoints. Baseline proteinuria also modified the treatment benefit; improvements were most evident among patients with a UPCR <3 g/g at baseline, with limited differences observed in higher proteinuria strata. Additionally, a subgroup analysis of East Asian patients demonstrated improved renal response rates and reduced renal event risk, supporting generalizability across ancestries, although individual patient factors remain critical.

In practice, belimumab is best conceptualized as an adjunctive therapy that incrementally increases the probability of durable response and reduces flare risk, rather than as a rapid “rescue” intervention. Its benefit may be greatest when initiated early in the treatment course in patients with proliferative pathology who require sustained immunologic control, particularly when steroid tapering is a priority. Limitations include cost, access, and the fact that even with belimumab, complete response rates remain suboptimal, underscoring the need for additional therapeutic strategies.

## Voclosporin (novel calcineurin inhibitor): evidence, practical considerations, and safety

Voclosporin is a structural analog of cyclosporine A with stronger binding to cyclophilin A and more potent calcineurin inhibition. A key practical advantage is its more predictable dose-concentration relationship, which reduces the need for therapeutic drug monitoring. Beyond its immunomodulatory effects through T-cell suppression, calcineurin inhibition may stabilize podocytes and contribute to rapid reductions in proteinuria. This effect is clinically meaningful given the prognostic importance of early proteinuria improvement.

In AURORA 1, a phase 3 randomized, double-blind trial, voclosporin added to MMF (typically 1 g twice daily) and low-dose oral glucocorticoids improved complete renal response rates at 52 weeks compared with placebo. Response definitions included stringent proteinuria thresholds, stable kidney function, and avoidance of rescue therapy. The rapid proteinuria-lowering effect of voclosporin may be particularly advantageous for patients with preserved kidney function and significant proteinuria who require early trajectory improvement to meet treat-to-target milestones.

Calcineurin inhibitor therapy requires careful patient selection and close monitoring. KDIGO recommends this approach in patients without severe kidney impairment (e.g., eGFR >45 mL/min/1.73 m2), reflecting concerns about nephrotoxicity and hemodynamic effects in advanced kidney dysfunction. Blood pressure, kidney function, and drug–drug interactions must be closely monitored. Although long-term safety and durability data continue to accumulate, clinicians should balance the benefits of rapid proteinuria reduction against potential risks, particularly in patients with cardiovascular comorbidities or increased susceptibility to infection.

## Rituximab and intensified B-cell depletion strategies: lessons for next-generation anti-CD20 therapy

Rituximab, a type I anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody, has long been used off-label for refractory LN despite mixed trial evidence. In the LUNAR trial, rituximab added to MMF and corticosteroids did not significantly increase overall renal response rates compared with placebo, although numerical improvements and serologic benefits were observed, and subgroup signals suggested potential heterogeneity of treatment effect. These findings highlight the complexity of B-cell biology in LN and suggest that the depth and persistence of B-cell depletion may be important determinants of clinical response.

Interest in intensified B-cell depletion therapy has increased. In prospective cohort studies combining rituximab with low-dose cyclophosphamide and methylprednisolone pulses, high complete response rates were reported in the absence of maintenance therapy. Although not definitive, these data support the concept that more profound B-cell depletion may translate into improved renal outcomes and inform the development of next-generation anti-CD20 agents designed for more potent and sustained depletion.

## Obinutuzumab (type II anti-CD20): phase 3 evidence and positioning

Obinutuzumab is a glycoengineered type II anti-CD20 antibody that enhances direct cell killing and antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity compared with type I antibodies. In a recent phase 3 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in adults with active proliferative LN receiving standard therapy with MMF and glucocorticoids, obinutuzumab improved complete renal response at week 76 compared with placebo. According to published results, a complete renal response occurred in 46.4% of patients receiving obinutuzumab versus 33.1% of those receiving placebo, representing a clinically meaningful absolute difference. Additionally, obinutuzumab produced greater serologic improvements and higher rates of complete CD19+ B-cell depletion.

Safety considerations remain central to clinical positioning. Overall adverse event rates were similar between groups; however, serious adverse events—driven largely by infections—were more frequent in the obinutuzumab arm, and grade ≥3 infections and neutropenia occurred more commonly. These findings underscore the need for careful infection risk mitigation, vaccination optimization, and potentially biomarker-informed patient selection. If ongoing data confirm a favorable benefit–risk profile in appropriate populations, type II anti-CD20 therapy may become a key option for patients with high-risk proliferative diseases or inadequate responses to standard therapy.

## Anifrolumab (type I interferon receptor blockade): renal signals and ongoing evaluation

Type I interferons amplify dendritic cell activation and antigen presentation and promote autoreactive T- and B-cell responses, thereby contributing to renal inflammation in LN. Anifrolumab is a monoclonal antibody that targets the type I interferon receptor, thereby inhibiting downstream interferon signaling. Although anifrolumab has been approved for moderate-to-severe non-renal SLE, its role in LN remains to be defined.

In the TULIP-LN phase 2 program, anifrolumab was evaluated as an add-on to standard therapy (MMF plus glucocorticoids) using two dosing strategies. In the 104-week extension, the intensified regimen showed numerically higher complete renal response rates than the placebo and was associated with lower cumulative proteinuria and improvements in systemic disease activity. Safety was generally acceptable; however, herpes zoster and influenza were reported more frequently in anifrolumab-treated patients, consistent with interferon pathway inhibition. These results support continued phase 3 development to clarify the magnitude of renal benefit, identify subgroups most likely to respond, and define optimal combination strategies.

## Iscalimab (CD40 pathway inhibition): emerging phase 2 data

The CD40–CD154 co-stimulatory pathway is central to T-cell-dependent B-cell activation and germinal center responses, making it a biologically plausible target in LN. Iscalimab (CFZ533) is a monoclonal antibody targeting CD40. In a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 2 trial in patients with active LN, iscalimab met the primary endpoint of proteinuria reduction at week 24. Reported results demonstrated a relative reduction in UPCR from baseline of 63.1% with iscalimab compared with 36.3% with placebo, representing a statistically significant greater proteinuria reduction versus placebo. Adverse events were mostly mild to moderate; however, long-term safety and durability remain to be established.

These findings are noteworthy for two reasons. First, they provided a proof-of-concept that upstream co-stimulatory blockade can yield measurable renal signals over a relatively short timeframe. Second, if confirmed in larger trials, CD40 pathway inhibition could complement existing regimens by targeting adaptive immune activation, while potentially allowing further glucocorticoid reduction. Key unanswered questions include optimal patient selection (e.g., immunologic phenotypes with strong T-cell–B-cell interaction signatures), appropriate combination partners, and infection risk mitigation.

## Practical, response-guided framework for contemporary management

Incorporating guideline updates into clinical practice requires a structured framework centered on early response assessment, toxicity mitigation, and individualized selection of adjunctive therapies. First, background therapy with hydroxychloroquine and supportive care should be optimized in all patients. Second, induction therapy should be selected based on disease severity, kidney function, patient-specific risks (e.g., fertility concerns), and anticipated adherence. MMF-based regimens are often preferred when fertility preservation is a priority, whereas low-dose cyclophosphamide is a validated option in appropriately selected patients.

Third, clinicians should explicitly monitor treat-to-target milestones, particularly the proteinuria trajectory at 3 and 6 months, and reassess therapy when targets are not achieved. In patients with preserved kidney function and substantial proteinuria who require rapid reduction, combining MMF with a calcineurin inhibitor, such as voclosporin, can be considered, acknowledging the need for monitoring. For patients in whom long-term immunological control and flare prevention are priorities, adding belimumab to standard therapy is supported by phase 3 evidence and guideline recommendations. For patients with persistent disease activity or inadequate response—particularly when B-cell signatures or serologic activity remain prominent—next-generation B-cell depletion (e.g., obinutuzumab) may represent an emerging therapeutic option, although infection risk must be weighed carefully. Interferon pathway inhibition and co-stimulatory blockade represent additional investigational strategies that may be particularly relevant for selected immunologic phenotypes.

Finally, glucocorticoid minimization should be treated as a parallel therapeutic goal rather than a secondary consideration. Achieving low-dose prednisone thresholds by 3–6 months, when clinically feasible, may reduce damage accrual and aligns with guideline recommendations. Long-term vision is a precision-based approach involving early identification of nonresponse, mechanism-guided therapy selection, and durable remission with the least toxic regimen.

## Discussion

This review highlights that the management of proliferative LN is transitioning from empiric steroid-heavy regimens to response-guided combination strategies that incorporate targeted therapies. However, major unmet needs persist. First, complete renal response remains difficult to achieve for many patients. Even in contemporary trials incorporating add-on targeted agents, absolute complete response rates frequently remain below 50%, and a substantial proportion of patients fail to reach proteinuria targets within recommended timeframes. Second, refractory disease and relapse remain common. Observational data suggest that between 20% and 30% of patients with proliferative LN exhibit refractory disease despite standard induction therapy, and renal flares continue to occur during follow-up, contributing to chronic progression and irreversible nephron loss.

Several themes have emerged in clinical translation. Early proteinuria reduction is the most actionable prognostic marker in routine practice and should be operationalized as a prospective decision-making tool rather than as a retrospective observation. A “trajectory mindset” that evaluates the slope of proteinuria decline and stability of kidney function over the first 3–6 months enables timely treatment intensification for patients who are not meeting targets. Importantly, this approach can prevent prolonged exposure to ineffective regimens and reduce cumulative toxicity. Another theme is the central importance of glucocorticoid stewardship. The association between cumulative prednisone exposure and irreversible organ damage is well established, and glucocorticoid minimization is not only desirable but also necessary for long-term health. Targeted therapies may provide the immunological control required to facilitate safer tapering in high-risk patients.

The expanding therapeutic armamentarium presents new challenges. Incremental gains in efficacy must be weighed against additional immunosuppressive exposure, cost, and adherence burden. For example, belimumab improves composite and complete responses and reduces renal events; however, it requires sustained administration and may be less beneficial in patients with very high baseline proteinuria or pure membranous disease. voclosporin offers rapid antiproteinuric benefits but requires careful selection and monitoring to mitigate the risk of nephrotoxicity, particularly in patients with reduced baseline kidney function. Obinutuzumab provides strong evidence for an improved complete response; however, infection and neutropenia risks may limit its broad use unless risk mitigation strategies and patient selection improve. Emerging agents, such as anifrolumab and iscalimab, highlight the promise of targeting interferon signaling and co-stimulation; however, confirmatory trials are needed to define optimal combinations, long-term safety, and patient subgroups most likely to benefit.

Looking ahead, precision medicine approaches will likely determine whether the next decade will produce transformational rather than incremental improvement. Incorporating biomarkers—including serological, transcriptomic, and potentially intrarenal molecular signatures—could refine the selection of B-cell–targeted versus interferon-targeted or T cell co-stimulation-targeted strategies. Repeat biopsy and noninvasive biomarkers may also indicate whether residual proteinuria reflects ongoing inflammation or chronic structural damage, guiding escalation decisions. Ultimately, the goal is durable renal remission with minimal toxicity—a target that requires not only better drugs but also better treatment personalization, structured monitoring, and adherence support.

## Conclusion

Proliferative LN remains a high-burden condition associated with substantial risks of chronic kidney disease, kidney failure, and premature mortality. Early reduction in proteinuria is a strong predictor of long-term kidney outcomes and should guide treat-to-target monitoring. Conventional regimens with mycophenolate or cyclophosphamide remain foundational but are limited by incomplete response rates and glucocorticoid toxicity. The updated KDIGO and EULAR recommendations now endorse belimumab and calcineurin inhibitor-based combination strategies in appropriately selected patients, reflecting trial evidence for improved renal responses and flare prevention. Emerging therapies, such as obinutuzumab, anifrolumab, and iscalimab, expand the options for refractory disease and support a future direction toward mechanism-based, individualized combination therapy with rigorous toxicity mitigation.
