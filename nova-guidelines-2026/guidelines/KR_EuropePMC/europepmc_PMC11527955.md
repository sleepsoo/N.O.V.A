# Delphi consensus recommendations on treatment for advanced-stage marginal zone lymphoma in South Korea

## Abstract

Due to the lack of treatment guidelines for the management of advanced-stage marginal zone lymphoma (MZL), only one chemoimmunotherapy—cyclophosphamide, vincristine, and prednisone plus rituximab (R-CVP)—is reimbursed in the first-line setting in South Korea. The aim of this study was to develop a consensus-based recommendation for the treatment of patients with advanced-stage MZL. Twelve hematologist oncologists participated in a two-round Delphi process to identify consensus on the management of patients with advanced-stage MZL in South Korea. Physicians rated their level of agreement with each statement on a four-point Likert scale. Statements were divided into two sections: definitions used in clinical practice and clinical management of patients with advanced-stage MZL. Consensus was reached for 23 of 33 (69.7%) and 5 of 13 statements (38.5%) in rounds 1 and 2, respectively. There was strong consensus (91.7%) that advanced-stage MZL subtypes are defined according to the Lugano staging system. First-line systemic treatment should be prescribed for patients with symptomatic advanced-stage MZL. Although there was unanimous agreement that R-CVP is the standard first-line treatment for advanced-stage MZL, physicians also agreed that bendamustine with rituximab (BR) has greater efficacy than R-CVP as first-line treatment (91.7%). For the treatment of relapsed/refractory advanced-stage MZL, BR and R-CVP can be repeated in patients with short (< 24 months) and long remission periods (≥ 24 months), respectively. This study provides insights on the management of patients with advanced-stage MZL in South Korea. This may enhance clinical decision-making, thus improving patient outcomes.

## Introduction

Non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) is a group of heterogenous neoplasms that has diverse clinical presentations and disease evolution. NHL that develops from B cells is known as the B-cell lymphoma (BCL) subtype and can be further classified into high and low grade. Marginal zone lymphoma (MZL) is an indolent BCL and rare disease that is slow-growing in patients receiving long-term treatment. In South Korea, 35–45% of NHLs are indolent, with MZL being the most common indolent lymphoma (21%). Between 2011 and 2015, the incidence of MZL increased by 32%, and the all-cause mortality rate increased by 77% in patients with MZL. More than 50% of patients with MZL will experience relapse within 10 years.

There are three subtypes of MZL: (1) extranodal MZL (EMZL) of mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue, which can be further categorized into gastric and non-gastric types; (2) nodal MZL (NMZL); and (3) splenic MZL (SMZL). According to National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines for MZL, current treatment options vary depending on the subtype and stage of disease and patient and tumor characteristics. The NCCN recommends bendamustine and rituximab (BR); cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone (CHOP); cyclophosphamide, vincristine, and prednisone (CVP); and rituximab monotherapy for first-line treatment. Bendamustine and obinutuzumab; BR; covalent BTKi including acalabrutinib and zanubrutinib; non-covalent BTKi including pirtobrutinib; CHOP; CVP; and lenalidomide and rituximab have been recommended for second-line and subsequent treatments. Patients who receive systemic treatments experience lower quality of life; hence, guidance on selection and sequencing of treatment options is needed.

In South Korea, there is a lack of guidelines for treatment of patients with advanced-stage MZL, and clinicians’ prescribing decisions are influenced by the reimbursement status of cancer therapies. Currently, only R-CVP is reimbursed in the first-line setting, and there is no reimbursed second-line therapy for advanced-stage MZL. Hence, developing a consensus for the treatment of advanced-stage MZL may improve the quality of clinical decisions and consistency of care for patients with advanced-stage MZL. Given the scarcity of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in rare diseases, Delphi studies are suitable and valuable to establish consensus and develop clinical guidelines. To develop a consensus-based recommendation for the treatment of patients with advanced-stage MZL, we performed a Delphi survey to examine expert opinions regarding the treatment approach in this setting.

## Methods

### Study design

The Delphi method is a widely accepted scientific and systematic technique to establish consensus among experts in the healthcare setting when limited evidence is available. It uses an iterative multi-stage process to elicit and collate controlled, individual, and anonymous feedback from a group of experts through structured questionnaires. Ethics approval for this study was provided by the Samsung Medical Center Institutional Review Board, and informed consent forms were collected from the experts.

### Delphi experts

Delphi panelists were hematologist oncologists specializing in the management of patients with advanced-stage MZL in South Korea. The target panel size in our study was 12, as 10 to 15 people are adequate when a Delphi panel is homogenous. Hematologist oncologists with ≥ 5 years of experience (post-specialty training) who spent ≥ 4 days per week directly involved in patient care and treated ≥ 10 patients with MZL per year were recruited to the expert panel.

### Delphi questionnaire domains/statements

A targeted literature search regarding relevant topics in the clinical management of advanced-stage MZL, focusing on first-line treatment for advanced-stage MZL and treatment for relapsed/refractory (R/R) advanced-stage MZL, was conducted. The Delphi questionnaire included two main sections: (1) definitions used in clinical practice and (2) clinical management of patients with advanced-stage MZL in six major domains. The six domains were (1) definitions for staging of MZL; (2) clinical indications that warrant treatment switch; (3) frontline therapy for advanced-stage MZL; (4) frontline therapy for advanced-stage EMZL; (5) frontline therapy for advanced-stage SMZL; and (6) treatment for R/R advanced-stage MZL. In this study, two structured rounds of online questionnaires in Korean were used to obtain consensus from a group of experts.

Statements included in the Delphi questionnaire were reviewed by two hematologist oncologists (study investigators and authors) with recognized expertise in MZL management and research in South Korea. Both study investigators were involved in the generation of questionnaire domains and statements and the interpretation and discussion of questionnaire results.

### Two-round Delphi process

Panelists received an electronic link providing personalized access to the online platform for each round of data collection. In round 1, physicians were asked to independently rate their level of agreement on a four-point Likert scale (0 = “no,” 1 = “minimal,” 2 = “moderate,” 3 = “strong”) as a four-point scale has been shown to produce stable findings in Delphi studies. Physicians individually and anonymously provided feedback for each statement based on current clinical evidence and routine clinical practice. For each statement, a free-text response was available to allow physicians the opportunity to elaborate on or explain their responses. Statements that did not reach consensus were modified for round 2. Results from round 1 were discussed with the study investigators.

In round 2, the Delphi questionnaire was updated to include modified versions of statements that did not reach consensus in round 1. Statements that reached consensus in round 1 were not tested in round 2; however, we tested a few new statements in round 2 to further validate physicians’ comments in round 1. Physicians were asked to rate the statements using the same method described for round 1. For statements that did not reach consensus, the reasons for non-consensus were analyzed and summarized by the study team.

### Statistical analysis

The level of agreement with each statement was categorized according to the scores on a four-point scale. Consensus agreement and disagreement were achieved when ≥ 70% of physicians and ≤ 30% of physicians gave a score of 2–3, respectively. Non-consensus was defined as 31–69% of physicians giving a score of 2–3. Descriptive statistics were used to describe physicians’ background characteristics and to group responses to each statement in both rounds. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 17 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas 77845, USA).

## Results

### Subjects

Of the 27 physicians invited to participate in this Delphi study, 12 agreed to participate and completed round 1 (44.4%), and all 12 completed round 2 (100%). Table 1 summarizes the background characteristics of physicians. Physicians had a median of 19.5 years of post-specialty training. ### Rating of agreement in round 1 Delphi survey

Table 2 shows the number of statements by clinical domain in both rounds. In round 1 (Table 3), consensus was reached for 23 of 33 statements (69.7%); 10 statements (30.3%) did not reach consensus. A high degree of consensus (≥ 91.7%) was achieved for all statements regarding the definitions for staging of different MZL subtypes, including EMZL, NMZL, and SMZL (statements 1–4). Physicians reached consensus on the statements for the frontline therapy for advanced-stage MZL domain (12/14 [85.7%]). Unanimous consensus was reached regarding the use of R-CVP as the standard first-line treatment for patients with advanced-stage MZL (statement 13) and use of first-line systemic treatment in patients with asymptomatic advanced-stage MZL with steady or rapid disease progression (statement 12.3). A total of 12 of 14 statements (85.7%) on frontline therapy for advanced-stage MZL reached consensus; statements that only patient preference should be used as an indication for first-line systemic treatment for asymptomatic advanced-stage MZL (statement 12.6) and that rituximab monotherapy is recommended in elderly patients (aged ≥ 70 years) (statement 15.1) did not reach consensus. One reason for non-consensus was that shared decision-making is more suitable as it enables clinicians and patients to participate jointly in deciding on the next suitable treatment. Physicians also mentioned that R-CVP use is feasible in elderly patients and that it is challenging to determine treatment based on age.

Consensus on frontline therapy for advanced-stage EMZL was reached in 2 of 3 statements (66.7%), while there was no consensus on BR as the treatment of choice for patients with advanced-stage EMZL with chromosome translocation t(11;18) (statement 18). A reason for non-consensus was that treatment is not determined based on chromosome translocation, and further evidence is needed to identify the most suitable treatment for this group of patients. Physicians reached consensus on rituximab-based therapy as the preferred first-line systemic therapy over splenectomy in patients with advanced-stage SMZL (statement 21). On the contrary, physicians did not reach consensus on rituximab monotherapy as the preferred first-line therapy in patients with advanced-stage SMZL. The reason for non-consensus is that rituximab monotherapy is not reimbursed in South Korea; thus, R-CVP therapy is more suitable and currently reimbursed in South Korea. Regarding treatment for R/R advanced-stage MZL, there was consensus on watch and wait as an acceptable initial approach for patients with asymptomatic R/R advanced-stage MZL (statement 23); furthermore, BR should be recommended in patients with R/R advanced-stage MZL with a short remission period of < 24 months (statement 25), or R-CVP can be repeated with a long initial remission period of ≥ 24 months (statement 24). Additionally, BR should be recommended in patients with R/R advanced-stage MZL who are unable to tolerate the side effects of R-CVP (statement 26). No consensus was reached on recommending zanubrutinib in patients with R/R advanced-stage MZL who are at risk of cardiovascular conditions (statement 27); zanubrutinib is not reimbursed in South Korea, and further evidence is needed before recommending it.

### Rating of agreement in round 2 Delphi survey

Table 3 summarizes the level of agreement with statements in round 2. Consensus was reached for two clinical indications that warrant switching systemic treatments (2/5 [40%]), including a 50% increase in the size of a previously identified node (statement 2) and the occurrence of a clinically significant adverse event (statement 5). Other statements, including the size of a new lesion measuring > 1.5 cm (statement 1), the appearance of positron emission tomography–positive lesions (statement 3), and a < 50% reduction in tumor size (statement 4), did not reach consensus. For statements that did not reach consensus, physicians mentioned that treatment switch is recommended if patients experience new recurrent symptoms and based on tumor characteristics such as the location, size, and extent of the new lesion. With regard to frontline therapy for advanced-stage MZL, physicians reached consensus that patients with asymptomatic advanced-stage MZL should be treated with first-line systemic treatment based on shared decision-making among clinicians, patients, and their caregivers (statement 6); they also agreed that BR is the preferred first-line systemic treatment in patients with advanced-stage MZL regardless of reimbursement status (statement 7). Additionally, there was consensus on reducing the R-CVP dose in patients with Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status ≥ 2 (statement 8.2) but not in patients aged ≥ 70 years (statement 8.1). The reasons for non-consensus include the ability to use R-CVP in patients aged ≤ 80 years and that dose reduction should be based not on age but on the patient’s performance status and comorbidities.

No consensus was reached regarding R-CVP or BR as the treatment choice for patients with advanced-stage EMZL with chromosome translocation t(11;18) (statements 9 and 10) or R-CVP as the preferred first-line treatment of choice for patients with advanced-stage SMZL (statement 11). The reasons for non-consensus include treatment not being determined based on the chromosome translocation, as there is a lack of large randomized controlled trials demonstrating the effectiveness of systemic treatment in patients with EMZL with chromosome translocation t(11;18). In addition, physicians commented that R-CVP is appropriate but not the preferred treatment for advanced-stage SMZL, which can be managed by other types of treatment, including BR, rituximab monotherapy, or radiotherapy. Regarding treatment for patients with R/R advanced-stage MZL, no consensus was reached for zanubrutinib as preferred over rituximab-based therapy in patients who are at risk of cardiovascular conditions, regardless of reimbursement status (statement 12). In general, physicians do not have experience in prescribing zanubrutinib and want further evidence on the effectiveness of zanubrutinib before prescribing it.

## Discussion

Advanced-stage MZL is an indolent disease, and patients with asymptomatic advanced-stage disease with a low tumor burden are suitable for a watch-and-wait approach. Systemic chemotherapy and/or immunotherapy are prescribed in patients with symptomatic, systemic disease. Current clinical guidelines and evidence-based research on management of MZL are mainly available for Western populations, and limited evidence is available in Asia. Clinical guidance specific to South Korea will improve the consistency of patient care and outcomes. To our knowledge, this is the first Delphi panel study to provide insights on clinical practice among hematologist oncologists, and it reaffirms the need to research areas that did not reach consensus, particularly related to the clinical indications that warrant switching patients’ systemic treatments.

In general, there was consensus on the definitions for staging different MZL subtypes and aspects of management of patients with advanced-stage MZL, particularly on the type of clinical indications observed in patients with asymptomatic advanced-stage MZL before first-line systemic treatment, frontline therapy for advanced-stage MZL, and treatment for R/R advanced-stage MZL. No consensus was reached for some clinical indications that warrant switching patients’ systemic treatments. A possible explanation is that clinical indications should be considered collectively instead of individually to determine whether treatment switch is required, because disease progression may differ due to patient or disease characteristics, number and type of prior treatments, and duration of previous remissions. Another possible explanation is the lack of MZL-specific treatment guidelines in South Korea. MZL is a rare disease with a heterogeneous clinical presentation, and the number of symptomatic patients requiring treatment is low; thus, variation in the management of patients with MZL among hematologist oncologists is expected. Additionally, there is a lack of evidence available for the management of advanced-stage MZL due to challenges in recruiting sufficient patients to conduct clinical trials specially designed for patients with MZL. Most clinical trial data on the treatment of MZL have been extrapolated from umbrella trials including patients with indolent B-cell lymphoma or from retrospective reviews. By conducting real-world studies in patients with MZL, we may be able to better understand the impact of patient or disease characteristics and treatment patterns on patient outcomes, thus preventing disease progression and improving quality of care.

Physicians unanimously agreed that R-CVP is the standard first-line treatment for patients with advanced-stage MZL in South Korea. This is expected as the prescribing patterns of systemic treatments for advanced-stage MZL in South Korea are largely influenced by reimbursement status, and the R-CVP regimen is currently reimbursed for patients with advanced-stage MZL. R-CVP is effective and well tolerated in patients with advanced-stage MZL in South Korea. Although it is currently the standard first-line treatment, there was strong consensus (91.7%) among physicians that BR has greater efficacy than R-CVP as first-line systemic treatment for patients with advanced-stage MZL. This may be explained by the clinical effectiveness of BR observed in a phase II trial and a large international consortium. BR was also well tolerated, demonstrating a better toxicity profile than R-CHOP or R-CVP. Furthermore, the BR regimen is recommended as a first-line treatment for MZL by NCCN and European Society for Medical Oncology guidelines. Considering the effectiveness and safety profile of BR, including BR as a treatment option in South Korea may provide clinicians with an alternative treatment regimen to improve patient outcomes and safety.

Advanced-stage MZL is generally incurable, and most patients on first-line treatment experience a continuing pattern of relapse and remission. Patients with R/R MZL require a personalized care approach, considering patient or disease characteristics, number and type of prior treatments, and duration of previous remissions. Furthermore, toxicities accumulated from previous therapies can limit treatment options. Therefore, future management of patients with advanced-stage MZL requires newer therapies with improved efficacy and tolerability to improve outcomes in patients with R/R MZL. NCCN guidelines support the use of Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitors as second-line or subsequent treatment for patients with advanced-stage MZL. In the MAGNOLIA trial, the second-generation Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitor zanubrutinib demonstrated a high overall response rate of 68%, a complete response rate of 25%, and a favorable safety profile in patients with R/R MZL.

The Delphi method is used to develop expert-based opinions and provide insights in areas where limited evidence-based literature is available, such as in the diagnosis and management of guidelines for rare disease. This study has several strengths. First, the Delphi method allows for the development of consensus guidance for the treatment of patients with MZL in South Korea, where a national treatment guideline is currently not available. Second, the anonymized data collection process limits interaction and prevents bias from influential or vehement panelists. Third, physicians in this study had a median of 19.5 years of post-specialty training, suggesting that they have a considerable level of experience in clinical practice and increasing the likelihood that the consensus guidance reflects the opinions of experts well-versed in the field of MZL management.

Limitations of the study include the absence of a face-to-face meeting as physicians may not have had the opportunity to exchange important information, such as to clarify reasons for disagreements or to seek clarifications to reach consensus. Delphi statements are structured concisely to improve their readability and understandability; hence, it may be challenging to construct a statement that addresses the complex nature of managing MZL. This is evident in the lack of consensus in round 2 for statements on clinical indications that warrant treatment switch; choosing the most suitable treatment option for patients is a challenge faced by clinicians selecting the most appropriate treatment for different MZL subtypes, and the different factors that may influence treatment choice must be evaluated. It is unlikely that statements that did not initially meet consensus will reach consensus given the lack of MZL-specific treatment guidelines, clinicians’ limited exposure in prescribing different systemic treatments in various MZL subtypes due to the local reimbursement landscape for MZL, and the variability in clinical experience among hematologist oncologists. Another limitation is the limit on the length of the Delphi survey to reduce respondent burden. As we could not exhaustively include all topics related to MZL management, we carefully formulated each iteration to include the most necessary statement to gauge consensus while reducing respondent fatigue and maximizing response rate.

## Conclusions

This Delphi study provides valuable insights in the management of patients with advanced-stage MZL. The expert consensus emphasizes that patients with symptomatic advanced-stage MZL are predominantly prescribed R-CVP as first-line treatment, and other treatment options may be considered in some patient settings. Additionally, this Delphi study identified other specific issues, such as the clinical indications that warrant switching patients’ systemic treatments, that remain unresolved in the setting of advanced-stage MZL. In view of the findings from this study and the changing treatment landscape for MZL in Western populations, alternative treatment options for the general population are vital. Efforts should be made to establish an MZL-specific treatment guideline to support clinicians and enhance clinical decision-making, thus improving the consistency and quality of health care.
