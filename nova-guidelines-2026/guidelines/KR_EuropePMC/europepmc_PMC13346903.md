# Clinical practice recommendations for the management of Alport syndrome: a joint statement of the Korean Society of Nephrology and the Korean Society of Pediatric Nephrology

## Abstract

### Background

Alport syndrome (AS) is a hereditary kidney disorder characterized by progressive hematuria, proteinuria, and kidney failure, frequently accompanied by hearing loss and ocular abnormalities. Although blockade of the renin-angiotensin system (RAS) is established as the first-line therapy, additional pharmacological interventions have been explored. To formulate evidence-based recommendations tailored to the Korean healthcare setting, the Genetic Kidney Disease Study Group of the Korean Society of Nephrology conducted a systematic review evaluating four therapeutic strategies: RAS blockade, cyclosporine, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs), and sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i).

### Methods

The quality of evidence and strength of recommendations were assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation framework.

### Results

The review confirmed that early initiation of RAS blockade delays progression to kidney failure and improves long-term survival, with the greatest benefit when initiated at the stage of microscopic hematuria or microalbuminuria. In contrast, evidence supporting cyclosporine, MRAs, and SGLT2i remains limited, primarily derived from small observational studies with short follow-up and heterogeneous populations. Although cyclosporine and MRAs have demonstrated short-term reductions in proteinuria, neither has shown sustained renoprotective effects, and cyclosporine is associated with a substantial risk of nephrotoxicity. Evidence regarding SGLT2i is inconsistent, with insufficient data on long-term efficacy in AS.

### Conclusion

Accordingly, these guidelines strongly recommend early RAS blockade as the cornerstone of AS, while no recommendations are made for routine use of cyclosporine, MRAs, or SGLT2i. Well-designed clinical trials are needed to expand therapeutic options and improve long-term outcomes in AS.

## Introduction

Alport syndrome (AS) is a common inherited kidney disorder characterized by persistent hematuria that progressively leads to kidney failure and is frequently accompanied by sensorineural hearing loss and, less commonly, ocular abnormalities. The clinical course and prognosis of AS vary according to the mode of inheritance and patient sex. Males with X-linked AS (XLAS) and individuals with autosomal recessive AS (ARAS) typically experience progressive disease culminating in kidney failure, whereas females with XLAS and patients with autosomal dominant AS (ADAS) generally demonstrate a more favorable clinical course.

Although no curative therapy currently exists for AS, blockade of the renin-angiotensin system (RAS) is well established as the standard of care and has been consistently shown to delay disease progression, particularly when initiated at an early stage of the disease. Accordingly, international clinical practice guidelines strongly recommend early initiation of RAS blockade to improve kidney outcomes in patients with AS. Despite these recommendations, implementation in routine clinical practice in Korea has been inconsistent, and a nationally unified consensus on optimal management strategies remains lacking.

In parallel, several additional pharmacological agents, including cyclosporine, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRA), and sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), have been explored as potential therapeutic options for AS. However, the available evidence supporting their efficacy and safety remains limited and inconclusive. To address these unmet needs and provide guidance tailored to the Korean healthcare system, the Genetic Kidney Disease Study Group of the Korean Society of Nephrology (KSN) developed evidence-based clinical practice recommendations. This guideline synthesizes current evidence on available therapeutic strategies and offers practical recommendations to assist clinicians in optimizing disease management and improving outcomes for patients with AS.

## Materials and methods

The Genetic Kidney Disease Study Group of the KSN convened a multidisciplinary panel, comprising adult and pediatric nephrologists, with methodological support from an evidence-based medicine expert. The development of these guidelines followed the Korean version of the AGREE II (Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation II) framework to ensure methodological rigor and transparency. Key domains of the AGREE II framework, including scope and purpose, stakeholder involvement, rigor of development, and applicability, were considered throughout the process.

Clinical questions were identified through a review of international guidelines and prioritized according to their relevance to the Korean healthcare context.

Four therapeutic strategies were selected for evaluation: RAS blockade, cyclosporine, MRAs, and SGLT2i. A systematic literature search was conducted on July 6, 2023, in MEDLINE (1946 to present), EMBASE (1974 to present), the Cochrane Library (from inception), and KoreaMed (from inception), and supplemented by manual screening of reference lists.

Evidence was organized using the PICO (Population, Intervention, Comparator, and Outcome) framework and appraised using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) methodology. Study quality was assessed with validated tools appropriate to study design, including AMSTAR 2 for systematic reviews and the RoBANS (Risk of Bias Assessment tool for Non-randomized Studies) for observational studies.

Draft recommendations were formulated by topic-specific subgroups and refined through structured consensus meetings until full agreement was achieved. The final guideline was reviewed and approved by the KSN guideline committees and formally endorsed by the Korean Society of Pediatric Nephrology.

## Key Questions and Recommendations

All key clinical questions and corresponding recommendations for the management of AS are summarized in Table 1. Detailed rationales and supporting evidence for each recommendation are provided in the sections that follow.

### Renin-angiotensin system blockade

Key Question 1: Does RAS blockade delay the progression to kidney failure in patients with AS?

#### Background

AS often initially presents with isolated hematuria, followed by the gradual development of proteinuria as the disease progresses. Most affected individuals eventually develop kidney failure with advancing age. Although no curative therapy currently exists, multiple clinical studies have demonstrated that RAS blockade can slow disease progression, consistent with its established benefits in other forms of chronic kidney disease (CKD).

This guideline evaluates the efficacy of RAS blockade in delaying progression to kidney failure, considering the mode of inheritance and disease severity at treatment initiation. Recommendations are provided based on a systematic review of the available evidence to guide the optimal use of RAS blockade in patients with AS.

Recommendation 1.1

RAS blockade is strongly recommended to delay the progression to kidney failure in patients with AS.

Grade of Recommendation: Strong

Level of Evidence: Moderate

Recommendation 1.2

Initiation of RAS blockade is recommended at the onset of microalbuminuria in patients with AS. In genetically confirmed males with XLAS and in males or females with ARAS, RAS blockade is also advised even in the presence of isolated hematuria without overt proteinuria.

Grade of Recommendation: Expert consensus

#### Summary of evidence

This guideline includes eight studies: seven cohort studies and one randomized controlled trial. Until the late 1990s, AS was considered incurable; however, accumulating evidence indicates that angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEi) reduce proteinuria and slow kidney function decline in AS.

In an early case series, enalapril slowed the decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) over more than 4 years in three adults with AS and baseline serum creatinine levels of 1.7–2.0 mg/dL. Additionally, enalapril reduced proteinuria by up to 77% in seven children with XLAS or ARAS over 2 years. These observations were subsequently supported by small controlled clinical studies and, later, by large-scale cohort studies facilitated by patient registries in the 2010s.

Retrospective and prospective cohort studies primarily derived from the European registry—and supplemented by recent reports from Japan and China—assessed disease progression and kidney failure. In a 2012 study of 283 patients, Gross et al. demonstrated that ACEi or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) delayed progression to kidney failure, with the greatest benefit observed when treatment was initiated early, at the stage of hematuria or microalbuminuria. Similarly, Temme et al. reported that RAS blockade delayed kidney failure in patients carrying heterozygous pathogenic variants in COL4A3–COL4A5.

#### Preferred timing of renin-angiotensin system blockade initiation

Preclinical and clinical evidence consistently supports early initiation of RAS blockade in AS. In a knock-out mouse model of ARAS, ramipril started at 4 weeks of age doubled lifespan and reduced proteinuria and kidney fibrosis, whereas treatment initiated at 7 weeks provided no survival benefit. Clinically, early initiation at the stage of hematuria or microalbuminuria confers the greatest kidney protection.

Although efficacy may decrease over time due to aldosterone escape or limited response in patients with severe variants affecting collagen function and glomerular basement membrane integrity, RAS blockade has been shown to delay kidney failure by 3–12 years, even in patients with truncating variants. In a prospective cohort study of 114 patients carrying the hypomorphic COL4A5 p.Gly624Asp variant, none who initiated RAS blockade with normal kidney function or early CKD stages progressed to kidney failure. In contrast, untreated patients or those treated at advanced CKD stages (GFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2) developed kidney failure at 48.9 and 51.7 years, respectively.

A systematic review and meta-analysis reported a 67% reduction in kidney failure risk (hazard ratio, 0.33; 95% CI, 0.24–0.45), with reductions of 68% in males with XLAS, 75% in ARAS, and 60% in females with XLAS or ADAS (Supplementary materials, available online). The EARLY PRO-TECT study further confirmed that early ramipril therapy reduced disease progression by approximately 50% in both randomized and real-world observational cohorts. Early RAS blockade may even halt disease progression in females with XLAS and in ADAS patients with albuminuria.

Taken together, early initiation of RAS blockade is strongly recommended. The 2024 international guideline recommends initiation at the onset of microalbuminuria regardless of genetic subgroup and earlier—at the stage of microscopic hematuria after 2 years of age—for males with XLAS and both sexes with ARAS. In contrast, for females with XLAS and patients with ADAS, treatment is not advised for isolated microscopic hematuria, as the benefit at this stage has not been clearly demonstrated and potential risks—including hypotension, acute kidney injury, and teratogenicity in females of childbearing age—may outweigh the uncertain therapeutic benefits. These groups generally have a milder and more variable disease course. Therefore, treatment is deferred until the onset of microalbuminuria, with close monitoring to ensure timely initiation if disease progression occurs.

#### Practical considerations: choice of renin-angiotensin system blockade

Ramipril has the most extensive evidence supporting both safety and efficacy among ACEi and ARBs, as demonstrated by numerous preclinical and clinical studies. Notably, two major European trials in children with CKD—the ESCAPE (Effect of Strict Blood Pressure Control and ACE Inhibition on the Progression of Chronic Renal Failure in Pediatric Patients) trial and the EARLY PRO-TECT Alport (Early Prospective Therapy European Community Trial in Alport) trial in children with AS aged ≥2 years —demonstrated significant clinical benefits.

A recent nationwide survey of adult and pediatric nephrologists in South Korea reported a unanimous consensus that RAS blockade is the treatment of choice for AS, although adult nephrologists showed a stronger preference for ARBs compared with pediatric nephrologists. This preference is likely driven by tolerability considerations, including the higher incidence of ACEi-induced cough in East Asian populations.

In an ARAS mouse model, ramipril improved survival substantially more than candesartan (111% versus 38%), consistent with more pronounced antifibrotic and anti-inflammatory effects. In humans, no studies have directly compared ramipril with other agents in AS. One small pediatric study found no significant difference in antiproteinuric effects between enalapril and losartan, and evidence from other clinical settings, such as primary hypertension, suggests comparable antiproteinuric effects between ACEi and ARBs.

While ACEi—particularly ramipril—are recommended as first-line therapy, ARBs may be used at the physician’s discretion, particularly in patients who are intolerant of ACEi. This approach aligns with recent international guidelines.

#### Titration of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors

In addition to early initiation, the 2024 guideline recommends titrating RAS blockade to the maximum tolerated dose once microalbuminuria is detected, regardless of genetic subgroup. For children, ramipril is recommended as first-line therapy, starting at 1–2 mg/m2/day and titrated up to 6 mg/m2/day, with dose adjustment according to growth.

Regarding the pace of dose escalation, the 2020 guidelines (expert opinion) recommended gradual up-titration over the first 3–4 months if tolerated, irrespective of proteinuria severity, although the 2024 guideline does not specify an exact timeframe. No pediatric dosing recommendations are currently available for ACEi other than ramipril.

As a second-line option, losartan may be initiated at 12.5 mg/m2/day, with a maximum dose of 50 mg/m2/day. Response to RAS blockade is best assessed by reduction in albuminuria, with higher urine albumin-to-creatinine ratios (UPCRs) predicting faster disease progression. Accordingly, additional therapies in combination with RAS blockade may be considered in selected cases.

### Cyclosporine

Key Question 2: Does cyclosporine treatment reduce proteinuria and delay the progression of CKD in patients with AS?

#### Background

At present, no curative therapy exists for AS. Nevertheless, several pharmacologic strategies have been investigated to attenuate disease progression, among which RAS blockade remains the most well-established and evidence-based intervention (see Key Question 1).

Calcineurin inhibitors (CNIs), including cyclosporine and tacrolimus, have been reported to reduce proteinuria in various glomerular diseases. This antiproteinuric effect is thought to result, at least in part, from direct stabilization of the podocyte actin cytoskeleton. However, the long-term use of CNIs is limited by concerns regarding nephrotoxicity. Experimental and clinical data indicate that calcineurin inhibition may promote the expression of profibrotic mediators, such as transforming growth factor-β, thereby contributing to interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy.

This guideline critically appraises the available evidence on the efficacy and safety of Cyclosporine in patients with AS and provides recommendations regarding its clinical use. Given the heightened susceptibility to CNI-related nephrotoxicity, patients with CKD defined by an eGFR <90 mL/min/1.73 m2 are considered a high-risk subgroup and are evaluated separately.

Recommendation

No recommendation can be made regarding the use of cyclosporine in patients with AS owing to insufficient and low-quality evidence.

Grade of Recommendation: No recommendation (insufficient evidence)

Level of Evidence: Very low

#### Summary of evidence

A total of eight studies were included in this review: four prospective observational studies, three retrospective observational studies, and one case report. No randomized controlled trials or systematic reviews were identified. The included studies were conducted in Spain (n = 2), Turkey (n = 2), France (n = 1), Italy (n = 1), and Iceland (n = 1), along with one abstract-only report from Russia.

Study designs varied based on background therapy, with cyclosporine evaluated either as monotherapy or in combination with RAS blockade. Additional methodological details are provided in the Supplementary materials (available online).

#### Effect on proteinuria

Seven of the eight studies, including one abstract, evaluated changes in proteinuria and consistently reported reductions following cyclosporine therapy. Early reports by Callís et al. demonstrated a notable decline in proteinuria within 3 weeks in five of eight patients, which was sustained over 8.4 years of follow-up. Charbit et al. reported that in nine patients with baseline eGFR >50 mL/min/1.73 m2, 24-hour urine protein excretion decreased significantly from 2.0 ± 1.06 g/day to 0.65 ± 0.73 g/day after 6 months of treatment. In four additional patients with CKD, proteinuria declined from 2.1 ± 1.6 g/day to 1.4 ± 1.7 g/day. Massella et al. observed that in 11 patients (UPCR, ≥1 mg/mg; creatinine clearance, >40 mL/min/1.73 m2), proteinuria decreased from a median UPCR of 1.25 mg/mg (range, 0.55–6.55 mg/mg) to 0.48 mg/mg (range, 0.21–2.41 mg/mg) over 6–30 months.

#### Sustainability of proteinuria reduction

All three studies reported an increase in proteinuria after discontinuation of cyclosporine. Callís et al. observed a return to baseline within 2 weeks, whereas Özdemir et al. reported recurrence to or above baseline values 6 months after discontinuation. Massella et al. found that the initial reduction in proteinuria (from 1.25 to 0.48 mg/mg) was not sustained beyond 36–42 months, with levels rising to 0.99 mg/mg (range, 0.26–6.49 mg/mg). In an abstract-only report, Shebalkina et al. noted that although proteinuria increased after cyclosporine withdrawal, it remained significantly lower than pretreatment levels.

#### Effect on kidney function

Kidney outcomes were reported inconsistently across studies. Callís et al. found that kidney function remained stable over 8.4 years of follow-up, including in three patients with CKD (eGFR: baseline, 57.1 ± 21.6 mL/min/1.73 m2; 8 months, 57.4 ± 21.1 mL/min/1.73 m2; and 8.4 years, 51.0 ± 18.1 mL/min/1.73 m2). In contrast, Charbit et al. observed a >30% decline in eGFR after 6 months among patients with initially normal kidney function. Massella et al. reported that four of 15 patients progressed to kidney failure, primarily those with baseline CKD. Ozdemir et al. found that eGFR declined more rapidly in the cyclosporine group (–62.7 ± 57.6 mL/min/1.73 m2 vs. –4.8 ± 53.7 mL/min/1.73 m2 in controls, p < 0.001), although the difference in kidney survival between cyclosporine-treated and untreated groups was not statistically significant. In an abstract-only report, Shebalkina et al. noted no significant change in kidney function during 2 years of cyclosporine therapy.

#### Risks

Nephrotoxicity is the primary safety concern associated with cyclosporine therapy. Although Callís et al. reported no histologic evidence of nephrotoxicity at 5 years in eight patients, subsequent studies have documented cyclosporine-induced kidney injury. Charbit et al. reported biopsy-confirmed cyclosporine-related nephrotoxicity in three of five patients after 14–42 months of treatment. Similarly, Massella et al. observed early and rapid eGFR decline in five patients with CKD, three of whom progressed to kidney failure within 1 year; biopsy-confirmed nephrotoxicity was noted in two cases after prolonged exposure. In a Japanese cohort study, Sugimoto et al. reported early GFR decline in three of five patients with normal baseline kidney function within 2 weeks of cyclosporine initiation, despite maintaining trough levels below 100 ng/mL. Kidney biopsy revealed immature glomeruli, suggesting increased susceptibility to cyclosporine-induced injury. No major infections were reported in any of these studies.

In summary, cyclosporine may induce a transient reduction in proteinuria during the early phase of treatment in AS; however, there is no evidence supporting a sustained renoprotective effect. The risk of nephrotoxicity is considerable, particularly in patients with CKD. Given the limited number of studies, the small sample sizes, the lack of high-quality evidence such as randomized controlled trials or systematic reviews, and the scarcity of data from Asian populations, no formal recommendations can be made regarding the use of cyclosporine in AS. Some experts advise against its use due to concerns about nephrotoxic potential. These findings support a cautious approach, even when cyclosporine is administered at low doses or for short treatment durations.

### Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist

Key Question 3: Does an MRA reduce proteinuria and slow the progression of CKD in patients with AS?

#### Background

Although there is no curative treatment for AS, several therapeutic approaches have been explored to slow disease progression. RAS blockade remains the cornerstone of management (see Key Question 1). In patients with persistent proteinuria despite maximal RAS blockade, concerns regarding accelerated decline in kidney function have prompted interest in adjunctive therapies. MRAs have demonstrated antiproteinuric effects in other glomerular diseases and have been investigated for their potential to attenuate GFR decline in AS. However, when combined with RAS blockade, MRAs may increase the risk of adverse effects, including worsening kidney function and hyperkalemia. This guideline summarizes the available evidence and provides recommendations on the use of MRAs in AS to reduce proteinuria and slow the progression of kidney disease.

Recommendation

No recommendation can be made regarding the use of MRAs in patients with AS owing to insufficient evidence.

Grade of Recommendation: No recommendation (insufficient evidence)

Level of Evidence: Very low

#### Summary of evidence

Two small prospective observational studies were identified, conducted in Japan and Italy. Both studies evaluated the addition of MRAs to ongoing RAS blockade in patients with AS. The sample sizes were limited, including five and ten participants, respectively. Additional details are provided in the Supplementary materials (available online).

#### Effect on proteinuria

Both included studies demonstrated significant reductions in proteinuria following the addition of spironolactone (SP), a steroidal MRAs, to ongoing RAS blockade therapy.

Kaito et al. administered SP at a dose of 25 mg/day for 18 months in five patients with AS. All patients exhibited statistically significant reductions in proteinuria at 3, 6, 12, and 18 months, with levels decreasing from 0.61 ± 0.10 to 0.13 ± 0.06 g/g at 18 months (p < 0.05).

Giani et al. treated 10 patients with AS with SP 25 mg/day for 6 months, with monthly assessments. Eight patients showed a significant reduction within the first month of treatment, from 1.77 ± 0.8 to 0.86 ± 0.6 g/g (p < 0.001), which was sustained throughout the treatment period. Of the two non-responders, one had documented poor adherence, and the other was suspected of low compliance. The eight responders continued SP treatment for up to 12 months, maintaining low levels of proteinuria (0.82 ± 0.48 g/g).

#### Sustainability of proteinuria reduction

Neither study assessed the long-term sustainability of proteinuria reduction; therefore, it remains uncertain.

#### Effect on kidney function

Both studies reported no statistically significant changes in kidney function following SP treatment. Kaito et al. observed a decrease in eGFR rate from 129.2 ± 21.4 to 110.1 ± 30.1 mL/min/1.73 m2 over 18 months (p = 0.115). Giani et al. reported stable kidney function over 6 months, with creatinine clearance changing minimally from 120 ± 30 to 118 ± 35 mL/min/1.73 m2.

#### Risks

When combined with RAS blockade, MRAs such as SP may cause adverse effects, including impaired kidney function, hypotension, hyperkalemia, and gynecomastia. No significant decline in kidney function was observed in either study.

Kaito et al. reported significant reductions in systolic and diastolic blood pressure after 3 months of SP treatment (systolic: 110.8 ± 4.99 to 91.2 ± 6.55 mmHg, p = 0.016; diastolic: 58.2 ± 2.65 to 51.0 ± 2.97 mmHg, p = 0.009), without symptoms of orthostatic hypotension or dizziness. Serum potassium increased slightly (4.10 ± 0.12 to 4.42 ± 0.10 mEq/L, p = 0.045), but no value exceeded 5.0 mEq/L. Giani et al. observed no significant changes in kidney function, blood pressure, or serum potassium levels. One obese male developed gynecomastia, which led to the discontinuation of SP.

In summary, SP added to RAS blockade provided additional proteinuria reduction in patients with AS without major safety concerns. However, the evidence is limited by small sample sizes, short treatment durations (6 and 18 months), and the absence of randomized controlled trials. Consequently, the long-term effects of MRAs on kidney outcomes, including sustained proteinuria reduction and preservation of GFR, remain uncertain.

Some experts suggest that MRAs may be cautiously considered as a secondary agent for patients with persistent proteinuria (UPCR >1.0 mg/mg) despite maximally tolerated ACEi therapy. Nonsteroidal MRAs, such as finerenone, have been shown to reduce proteinuria and improve kidney outcomes in adults with diabetic CKD, with emerging evidence indicating similar proteinuria-lowering and potential renoprotective effects in non-diabetic CKD. These agents appear to offer a more favorable benefit–risk profile than steroidal MRAs, demonstrating comparable or superior efficacy and lower rates of hyperkalemia and treatment discontinuation in available studies. In the future, patients with AS and overt proteinuria may be candidates for clinical trials, particularly those with CKD.

### Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors

Key Question 4: Do SGLT2i reduce proteinuria and slow the progression of CKD in patients with AS?

#### Background

Early RAS blockade remains the standard of care for AS (see Key Question 1). However, in patients with persistent proteinuria despite maximal RAS blockade, additional therapies may be considered due to concerns about accelerated kidney function decline. SGLT2i have demonstrated renoprotective and antiproteinuric effects in adults with diabetic and non-diabetic CKD, but their efficacy in AS has not yet been established. These agents may reduce proteinuria and preserve kidney function—particularly in patients with AS and coexisting CKD—yet potential adverse effects remain a concern when combined with RAS blockade. Reported risks include acute kidney injury, urinary tract infections, and hypovolemia. This guideline summarizes the current evidence on the use of SGLT2i in AS and provides recommendations regarding their efficacy and safety for proteinuria reduction and kidney protection.

Recommendation

No recommendation can be made regarding the use of SGLT2i in patients with AS owing to insufficient evidence.

Grade of Recommendation: No recommendation (insufficient evidence)

Level of Evidence: Very low

#### Summary of evidence

Four small prospective observational studies were identified, conducted in China (n = 2), Germany (n = 1), and Spain (n = 1; abstract only). All studies evaluated the use of SGLT2i in patients with AS, most of whom were receiving stable RAS blockade. Sample sizes were small (4, 4, 3, and 12 patients, respectively), and treatment durations were less than 1 year in all studies. For studies including mixed diagnoses, only AS-specific data were analyzed. No randomized controlled trials or systematic reviews were identified, and none of the studies included Korean patients. Additional details are provided in the Supplementary materials (available online).

#### Effect on proteinuria

All studies assessed proteinuria or albuminuria following treatment with SGLT2i, with inconsistent results. In an abstract by Galan Carrillo et al., 10 of 12 adults with ADAS and CKD (mean eGFR, 50 ± 22 mL/min/1.73 m2) demonstrated significant reductions in both proteinuria and albuminuria after a mean treatment duration of 7 ± 4 months; improvement in albuminuria was observed in all 12 patients.

Similarly, Song et al. reported that three patients with ARAS and CKD (mean eGFR, 51.8 ± 25.6 mL/min/1.73 m2) experienced reductions in 24-hour proteinuria (from 1.59 ± 0.30 to 1.10 ± 0.58 g/m2) and albuminuria (from 1,002.5 ± 575.7 to 612.4 ± 463.5 mg/g) after 4–6 months of treatment.

In a pediatric study including five children with AS (mean age 9.3 ± 3.4 years; one excluded due to missing data), 24-hour proteinuria decreased among the four children with normal kidney function after 12 weeks of treatment (from 2.92 ± 2.22 to 2.20 ± 1.40 g/m2), although the change was not statistically significant (p = 0.125).

In contrast, Boeckhaus and Gross reported that three male patients with AS and CKD (mean eGFR, 45.3 ± 4.0 mL/min/1.73 m2) and one female with preserved kidney function received treatment for a mean of 3.3 ± 0.5 months, with no significant change in albuminuria (from 1,382 ± 947 to 1,442 ± 1,000 mg/g, p = 0.87).

#### Sustainability of proteinuria reduction

The durability of proteinuria reduction with SGLT2i was not assessed in any of the four studies and therefore remains uncertain.

#### Effect on kidney function

All four studies assessed eGFR as a kidney function outcome measure. Small declines in eGFR were observed in some cases, but changes were generally mild and clinically acceptable. In the study by Boeckhaus and Gross, eGFR decreased slightly over 3.3 months (from 63.8 ± 37.0 to 59.8 ± 36.2 mL/min/1.73 m2, p = 0.022), including three male patients with CKD (from 45.3 ± 4.0 to 41.7 ± 2.3 mL/min/1.73 m2). Similarly, Galan Carrillo et al. reported a mild reduction over 7 ± 4 months in 12 adults with ADAS and CKD (from 50 ± 22 to 45 ± 19 mL/min/1.73 m2, p = 0.008). Liu and Fang et al. observed minimal change in four pediatric patients after 12 weeks of treatment (from 127.2 ± 26.5 to 121.9 ± 21.3 mL/min/1.73 m2; p = 1.000), while Song et al. reported stable kidney function in three patients with ARAS and CKD over 4–6 months (from 51.8 ± 25.6 to 47.0 ± 23.5 mL/min/1.73 m2).

In large, double-blind randomized controlled trials in adults with CKD (DAPA-CKD and EMPA-KIDNEY), an initial eGFR dip after SGLT2i initiation was transient and followed by long-term preservation of kidney function. According to the 2024 KDIGO (Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes) CKD guideline, a 10%–20% acute eGFR decline within the first 3 months after SGLT2i initiation is typical. The changes observed in the four AS studies fall within this range; however, recovery beyond the initial decline could not be demonstrated. Given the limited data, short treatment durations, and absence of high-quality study designs in AS, the long-term kidney benefits of SGLT2i remain uncertain. Well-designed, large-scale studies are warranted to determine whether SGLT2i can delay GFR decline in this population.

#### Risks

Potential adverse effects of SGLT2i when added to RAS blockade include kidney function decline, hypotension, hypovolemia, urinary tract infections, and ketoacidosis. None of the four studies reported treatment discontinuation due to adverse events. Although detailed safety outcomes were inconsistently described, Boeckhaus and Gross reported no cases of acute kidney injury, hypovolemia, or urinary tract infections, and Galan Carrillo et al. noted no significant issues with blood pressure, urinary tract infections, dehydration, or potassium levels.

In conclusion, current evidence is insufficient to support the efficacy of SGLT2i in reducing proteinuria or albuminuria when added to RAS blockade in patients with AS. Mild declines in eGFR were observed but were generally clinically acceptable, and no serious adverse events were reported. All studies were limited by small sample sizes and short treatment durations (<1 year). In the absence of robust clinical data, a formal recommendation cannot be established. Nevertheless, interest remains in their use for patients with persistent proteinuria despite RAS blockade. Renoprotective benefits of SGLT2i have been confirmed in adults with diabetic and non-diabetic CKD in large randomized controlled trials (DAPA-CKD and EMPA-KIDNEY). According to the 2024 international CKD guideline, SGLT2i may be considered as a second-line therapy, added to RAS blockade, in adults with AS and proteinuric CKD.

## Conclusions

The current management of AS prioritizes early RAS blockade, which remains the first-line therapy supported by the strongest evidence. Cyclosporine demonstrates antiproteinuric effects in small studies; however, its use is limited by nephrotoxicity, transient efficacy, and the absence of long-term data, particularly in patients with CKD. MRAs, specifically SP—the only agent evaluated—may provide additional proteinuria reduction when added to RAS blockade without major adverse effects, although the evidence is limited. SGLT2i have shown inconsistent effects on proteinuria in AS; nevertheless, recent international guidelines suggest their consideration as a second-line option in adults with proteinuric CKD, based on expert consensus. Given the overall limited evidence, no definitive recommendations can be made for cyclosporine, MRAs, or SGLT2i, and further well-designed studies are warranted to clarify their efficacy and safety in AS. Accordingly, future updates of these guidelines should aim to enhance applicability in real-world clinical practice and incorporate broader stakeholder participation, including patients.
