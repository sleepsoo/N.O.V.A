# Korean Medication Algorithm Project for Depressive Disorder 2025: Comparisons with Other Treatment Guidelines

## Abstract

The sixth edition of the Korean Medication Algorithm Project for Depressive Disorder (KMAP-DD) was published in 2025. This review compared KMAP-DD 2025 with four major international clinical practice guidelines: Canadian Network for Mood and Anxiety Treatments Clinical Guidelines for the Management of Major Depressive Disorders, National Institute for Health and Care Excellence Depression Guideline, Royal Australian and New Zealand College of Psychiatrists Clinical Practice Guidelines for Mood Disorders, and British Association for Psychopharmacology Guideline. While KMAP-DD is based on expert consensus, and others on evidence-based methods, overall treatment strategies for depressive episodes were fairly consistent. Especially, KMAP-DD 2025 offers more structured recommendations in areas lacking strong evidence, such as premenstrual dysphoric disorder, perinatal depression, and depression with medical comorbidities. KMAP-DD 2025 also reflected Korean clinical practice patterns emphasizing rapid symptom relief and early use of combination strategies. Despite limitations as a consensus-based guideline, KMAP-DD 2025 complements evidence-based approaches and provides practical, situation-specific guidance for real-world clinical decision-making in Korea.

## INTRODUCTION

Depression is a chronic disease that causes a significant economic and social burden. Globally, the number of diagnoses is increasing, and the associated burden is also expanding. For this reason, there is a high level of international interest in the efficient and safe treatment of depression, with pharmacotherapy being considered the most representative treatment modality. Despite advances in pharmacotherapy for depression, only about 40% of patients achieve full remission with primary medication therapy, and treatment response rates vary depending on the types of medication used, severity of symptoms, comorbid diseases, and patient characteristics. Despite such circumstances, given the rapid expansion of evidence, clinicians often struggle to stay current and select optimal treatments for diverse situations, underscoring the necessity of updated clinical guidelines.

Accordingly, many international clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) for the pharmacological treatment of depression continue to be published and updated to reflect the latest treatment options. Most CPGs are developed based on evidence obtained from well-designed clinical trials, which has the advantage of giving a relatively reliable assessment of the priority of treatment and the risk-benefit balance. However, due to various culturally specific factors – such as how depression presents, patient characteristics, and differences in clinical settings including healthcare policies and insurance systems – these guidelines may not be fully applicable in real-world clinical practice. To address this, the Korean Medication Algorithm Project for Depressive Disorder (KMAP-DD) was launched in 2002 based on expert consensus among experienced Korean psychiatrists. Thereafter, the KMAP-DD was revised five times in 2006, 2012, 2017, 2021 and 2025. Along with continuous updates, we have compared KMAP-DD with other international CPGs to identify differences and similarities. In this review, we aim to compare the updated KMAP-DD 2025 with other CPGs published over the past decade.

## TREATMENT GUIDELINES FOR COMPARISON

### Evidence-based Guidelines for Treating Depressive Disorders with Antidepressants: A Revision of the 2008 British Association for Psychopharmacology Guidelines

The British Association for Psychopharmacology (BAP) has released a series of evidence-based guidelines on the pharmacological treatment of psychiatric disorders, aiming to provide concise and practical recommendations grounded in current research in 2015. Initially published in 1993, the guidelines were updated in 2000 and 2008, with the latest revision in 2015 reflecting new evidence and reinforcing a strict evidence-based approach.

### Canadian Network for Mood and Anxiety Treatments Clinical Guidelines for the Management of Major Depressive Disorder in Adults

The Canadian Network for Mood and Anxiety Treatments (CANMAT) published its updated clinical guidelines for the management of major depressive disorder in 2023. This update was prompted by significant advances in research and the emergence of new treatment options since the previous edition in 2016. The CANMAT guidelines have published earlier editions in 2001 and 2009, providing structured, evidence-based recommendations for clinicians. The CANMAT 2023 guideline was developed through a systematic review of recent high-quality studies and expert consensus, with input from clinicians and patient partners. Treatments were categorized by strength of evidence and clinical feasibility.

### National Institute for Health and Care Excellence Depression Guideline in Adults

The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) published its updated guideline on the treatment of depression in adults in 2022, replacing the previous edition from 2009. The guideline was developed by an independent multidisciplinary committee using NICE’s standardized methodology. This involved a systematic review of recent clinical evidence, including randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses, along with health economic modeling. The recommendations were formed through structured consensus processes, considering treatment effectiveness, safety, patient preferences, and cost-effectiveness.

### The 2020 Royal Australian and New Zealand College of Psychiatrists Clinical Practice Guidelines for Mood Disorders

The Royal Australian and New Zealand College of Psychiatrists (RANZCP) published its updated guideline for mood disorders in 2020, building on earlier version at 2015. The update aimed to reflect new clinical evidence and provide practical, evidence-based recommendations suitable for both primary care and psychiatric settings. The guideline was developed through systematic literature reviews and expert consensus. Treatments were evaluated based on evidence quality, clinical relevance, and applicability. ## COMPARISONS OF TREATMENT OPTIONS ACROSS TREATMENT GUIDELINES

### Initial Treatment for Depressive Episode

In KMAP-DD 2025, for mild to moderate major depressive episodes (MDE), antidepressant (AD) monotherapy is the primary recommendation. For moderate to severe non-psychotic MDE, either AD monotherapy or combination therapy with an atypical antipsychotics (AAP) may be considered. For psychotic MDE, combination therapy with AD and AAP is recommended as the first-line treatment strategy (Table 2). Among ADs, escitalopram was the treatment of choice (TOC) not only for mild and moderate MDEs but also for severe MDE, whether with psychotic or non-psychotic features. Most selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) are recommended as first-line medications in all cases of MDE, but among SNRIs, milnacipran is considered a second-line medication. Mirtazapine is a first-line medication in all types of MDEs, while bupropion, agomelatine, tianeptine, and tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) were second-line medications in all types of MDEs. Vortioxetine is considered a first-line medication in mild to moderate in all types of MDEs but is considered a second choice in other conditions. The use of esketamine nasal spray is included in second-line options for severe MDE without psychotic features, although there was no expert consensus on this. When choosing AAP for first-line treatment, aripiprazole and quetiapine are recommended as first-line drugs in MDE without psychotic symptoms, while olanzapine, risperidone, and ziprasidone can be considered as second-line AAPs. In episodes with psychotic MDEs, aripiprazole and quetiapine are recommended as TOC, and olanzapine is recommended as the first-line medication. Risperidone, paliperidone, ziprasidone, amisulpride, and blonanserin may be considered as second-line AAPs.

Except for KMAP-DD 2025, the other four CPGs recommended non-pharmacological treatment as the first choice for mild MDE, including psychotherapy, exercise, cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT), and interpersonal therapy (IPT). CANMAT 2023 additionally recommended complementary and alternative medicine and digital health intervention as first-line treatments. However, in specific cases, such as when the patient prefers AD pharmacotherapy or has a history of moderate to severe recurrent depression, AD monotherapy may be considered. For moderate MDE, all five CPGs recommended AD monotherapy in combination with non-pharmacologic treatment as the first-line treatment strategy. For severe MDE without psychotic features, KMAP-DD 2025 was the only CPG to recommend an AAP augmentation with AD as first-line treatment strategy, while other CPGs recommended AD monotherapy as in moderate MDE. This was also prominent in the previous KMAP-DD 2021, which relatively preferred the combined therapy of AAP and AD with or without psychotic features for severe MDE compared with other CPGs. Considering that the combination therapy of two ADs was also selected as the first-line strategy in KMAP-DD 2025 for severe MDE with psychotic features, it can be interpreted that active combination therapy is generally preferred. This is thought to reflect the unique medical environment in Korea, where national health insurance provides relatively high economic and clinical accessibility to medical services, and quick symptom relief is prioritized in psychiatric care. On the other hand, for severe MDE with psychotic features, KMAP-DD 2025, CANMAT 2023, and BAP 2015 recommend AAP augmentation with AD as the first-line treatment strategy. The discrepancies between these five CPGs may indicate that, as in KMAP-DD 2021, Korean practitioners’ preference for the use of AAPs in severe depression continues to persist also in KMAP-DD 2025. Furthermore, considering one Korean study showing that aripiprazole is effective in MDE accompanied by anxious distress, it can be expected that Korean practitioners will keep preferring AAP for MDE with complicated symptoms.

### Second-step Treatment in Cases of Inadequate Response to Initial Treatment

For cases of MDE with insufficient response to initial treatment, the KMAP-DD 2025 guideline presents two pathways depending on the initial treatment strategy. In non-responders treated with AD monotherapy, the next steps include adding another AD or an AAP, or switching the AD. In those initially treated with AAP augmentation with AD, the guideline recommends either switching the AD or AAP, or adding an AD. For partial responders, the same structure applies: following AD monotherapy, augmentation with an AD or AAP is recommended, whereas for those already on AAP augmentation with AD, switching an AD or AAP or adding another AD or AAP is recommended (Table 3).

In CANMAT 2023, the diagnosis was prioritized for reassessment if the patient’s response to treatment is insufficient. For pharmacologic treatments, non-responders are advised to optimize the AD dose. After optimizing dosage, switching AD to another AD with distinct mechanism and superior treatment efficacy is recommended. In partial responders, augmentation of AAP including aripiprazole and brexpiprazole is recommended. These AAPs are serotonin and dopamine activity modulators and have been recommended due to their rapid therapeutic response and abundant evidence. Switching ADs that are not serotonin or norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors like bupropion, mirtazapine is also recommended. And esketamine or other augmentation agents like lithium, modafinil or triiodothyronine (T3) can be augmented if adding AAP is not effective (Table 3).

In the NICE 2022 guideline, for non-responders, clinicians are advised to switch the AD, either within the same class or to a different class, offer psychological therapy if not previously provided, consider augmentation with lithium or AAP, and refer to a specialist if multiple treatments fail. This is because NICE 2022 is a CPG that is primarily targeted for healthcare professionals at primary care and people with depression and their families. For partial responders, the guideline recommends adding an AD, lithium, or an AAP, as well as adding psychological therapy (Table 3).

In the RANZCP 2020 guideline, for non-responders, it is recommended to augment lithium or aripiprazole or switch the AD only if the previous augmentation fails. In cases of partial response, the preferred strategy is augmentation with lithium, aripiprazole, or quetiapine, and if this is unsuccessful, switching the AD may be considered (Table 3).

In the BAP 2015, for non-responders, switching AD with different mechanisms or augmenting quetiapine, aripiprazole, or lithium is recommended. For partial responders, augmentation with quetiapine, aripiprazole, or lithium is recommended, and other options such as risperidone, olanzapine, T3, mirtazapine, or bupropion may also be considered (Table 3).

When comparing KMAP-DD 2025 with four other CPGs, KMAP-DD showed a lower preference for augmenting agents such as T3 and lithium as a second treatment strategy. However, it was more recommended to combine or change AD or AAP, which is thought to be due to the Korean national health insurance coverage only allowing psychotropic medications to be used for depression. In the case of T3 and lithium, continuous serum level or thyroid function checks may be necessary, which could increase the cost burden on patients.

### Third-step Treatment in Cases of Inadequate Response to Second-step Treatment

The KMAP-DD 2025, in cases where the second-step treatment resulted in an inadequate response, clinical recommendations generally included either adding another AD or AAP, or changing AAP or AD. For non-psychotic MDEs, first-line augmentation strategies included buspirone, mood stabilizers including lithium, valproate, lamotrigine, psychostimulants, and thyroid hormone, particularly in cases of inadequate response following the addition of an AD to an existing AAP augmentation with AD regimen or a switch to AD and AAP augmentation therapy. Similarly, for psychotic MDEs, these agents were also recommended as first-line options when response remained inadequate after either adding an AD to an AD and AAP combination (i.e., 2ADs with AAP) or modifying AAPs within ongoing AD augmentation with AAP treatment (Table 3). When selecting specific augmentation agents, lithium was consistently recommended as a first-line option irrespective of the presence of psychotic features. Meanwhile, lamotrigine, buspirone, and valproate were classified as second-line options across both psychotic and non-psychotic presentations. Along the same lines, buspirone is also thought to be an effective augmenting agent for depression with anxiety, according to a recent study published in Korea. Methylphenidate was considered a second-line option for non-psychotic severe episodes, whereas carbamazepine was designated as a second-line agent for psychotic severe episodes. The third-step strategy for MDE without psychotic features was broadly similar to KMAP-DD 2021 excepting switching AD to another AD has changed from second-line option to first-line option. In cases with psychotic features, the recommended treatment strategy was the same as in KMAP-DD 2021.

The difference between KMAP-DD 2025 and other CPGs is that it considers cases that do not respond to two or more ADs as a third step treatment strategy rather than treatment-resistant depression (TRD). This classification was made because, in KMAP-DD 2025, clinicians separately defined TRD as cases where treatment was ineffective even after using two or more ADs and one or more AAPs, rather than using two or more ADs like in other CPGs. Also this reflects the view that Korean clinicians believe that treatment responses can be elicited in severe and complicated depression through combination therapy involving two or more classes of medications and suggests a preference for active treatment rather than defining patients as treatment-resistant.

CANMAT 2023 recommends considering electro convulsive therapy (ECT) or repeated transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) after multiple unsuccessful augmentation strategies with AAPs, ADs, lithium, or T3, or after AD switching in TRD patients. Notably, CANMAT 2023 recommended the use of TCAs and monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs) in TRD patients. This was due to the availability of serum levels of TCAs during treatment, unlike SSRIs and SNRIs, and the fact that they may be more effective in subpopulations of patients like with obsessive-compulsive disorder or chronic pain (Table 3). However, the use of these drugs should be monitored for safety, such as side effects and dietary adjustments.

In cases of treatment resistance, the NICE 2022 recommended next steps include referral to a specialist, high-intensity CBT, and consideration of AD switching, pharmacologic augmentation of AAPs including aripiprazole, olanzapine, quetiapine and risperidone or ECT. Augmentation agents like lithium, lamotrigine and T3 were also recommend, however, the use of these drugs is off-label and should be used with caution (Table 3). Interestingly, NICE 2022 does not recommend esketamine nasal spray for use in patients who have not responded to two AD as commonly defined in other guidelines, but rather recommends it when three ADs has been used. This reflects the nature of NICE guidelines as outcomes based on cost-effectiveness.

For patients reaching the third step or showing treatment resistance, the RANZCP 2020 recommends switching AD after augmentation was trialed. They also noted that switching ADs should generally involve a change to a different pharmacological class. However, switching within the same class is considered acceptable when the primary reason is inadequate adherence or discontinuation due to side effects of the initial agent. Also, ECT is recommended for severe, melancholic, or psychotic depression (Table 3). TCAs and MAOIs as broad-spectrum ADs can also be augmented after second treatment strategy show inadequate efficacy.

BAP 2015, in the third treatment step or TRD, the guideline suggests combining ADs with different mechanism or augmenting with an AAP and either lithium or T3. ECT is recommended for severe or psychotic cases (Table 3). Interestingly, adding CBT is also recommended with ongoing AD treatment.

To summarize the above, most CPGs have not organized their third-step treatment strategies in the way that KMAP-DD 2025 does as mentioned above. Various treatment strategies provided can be tried, even if the level of evidence is low, and it is acknowledged that response to treatment may be limited. Nevertheless, similar to KMAP-DD 2025, there was a consensus in other four CPGs that switching to an AD with different mechanism or using augmentation agents like lithium or T3 could be considered. Furthermore, in KMAP-DD 2025, since the definition of treatment resistance was defined as cases that did not respond to treatment even after adding AAP to AD combination therapy, it can be considered that the preference for non-pharmacological biological treatments such as ECT and rTMS was lower in the third treatment strategy compared to other CPGs.

### Pharmacological Treatment for Persistent Depressive Disorder and Each Subtype of Depression

According to KMAP-DD 2025, first-line pharmacological strategies for persistent depressive disorder (PDD) include AD monotherapy or AD with AAP combination therapy. Second-line options consist of two AD combinations, AD with mood stabilizer combinations, or AAP monotherapy. Escitalopram is most strongly recommended as a first-line agent, while sertraline, desvenlafaxine, fluoxetine, venlafaxine, duloxetine, paroxetine, mirtazapine, vortioxetine, bupropion, agomelatine, and milnacipran are also considered appropriate first-line agents. Tianeptine and TCAs are reserved for second-line use. In NICE 2022, treatment for chronic depressive symptoms (encompassing PDD) includes CBT, AD monotherapy with an SSRI, SNRI, or TCA, or combination of CBT and ADs. NICE 2022 cautions against TCAs due to overdose risk, recommending lofepramine as a relatively safer option when a TCA is selected. Also, BAP 2015 provides pharmacologic evidence for PDD, citing studies supporting the efficacy of fluoxetine, sertraline, moclobemide, amisulpride, and others. Meta-analytic data suggest that moclobemide and amisulpride may be more effective than fluoxetine. For chronic major depression, IPT combined with AD treatment is recommended over pharmacotherapy alone. CANMAT 2023 and RANZCP 2020 do not provide specific pharmacological recommendations for PDD. CANMAT 2023 conceptually addressed within the broader framework of difficult-to-treat depression (DTD), but no distinct treatment algorithms are proposed. RANZCP 2020 also recognized DTD, and the treatment was the same as the treatment strategy for depression mentioned above.

The KMAP-DD 2025 recommends escitalopram, desvenlafaxine, venlafaxine, sertraline, duloxetine, fluoxetine, vortioxetine, and bupropion for melancholic depression without psychotic features. TCAs are not recommended due to concerns regarding tolerability and safety. The RANZCP 2020 suggests that ECT may be considered as a first-line treatment in severe melancholic depression, particularly in the presence of psychomotor retardation, suicidal ideation, or refusal to eat or drink. TCAs or MAOIs may be considered when pharmacological treatment is chosen, although no specific agents are prioritized. BAP 2015 notes some evidence suggesting that TCAs may be more effective than SSRIs in melancholic depression. However, this is based on retrospective and non-randomized studies, and no definitive recommendations are made. CANMAT 2023 and NICE 2022 do not differentiate AD selection based on melancholic features.

Most CPGs, including KMAP-DD 2025, have not provided treatment strategies for atypical depression. In contrast, the BAP 2015 highlights modest evidence that phenelzine may be superior to imipramine in atypical depression, though no consistent advantage is found between MAOIs, SSRIs, and TCAs. Some evidence also supports the use of fluoxetine and reboxetine. For seasonal affective disorder, sertraline, fluoxetine, moclobemide, and bupropion are recommended as having potential treatment benefit.

### Pharmacological Treatment for Child and Adolescent Depression

According to the KMAP-DD 2025 guideline, AD monotherapy is the recommended TOC for both children and adolescents with mild to moderate MDEs. In children, fluoxetine is recommended as TOC, with escitalopram and sertraline also included as first-line options. In adolescents, escitalopram and fluoxetine are both considered TOC, while sertraline is also a recommended first-line AD. In cases of non-psychotic severe depression, both AD monotherapy and AD with AAP combination therapy are recommended as TOC for children and adolescents. Among ADs, fluoxetine, escitalopram, and sertraline are consistently endorsed across age groups. For AAPs, aripiprazole is the most strongly recommended and serves as the TOC when augmentation is required. For psychotic depression, AD with AAP combination therapy is recommended TOC in both children and adolescents. The preferred ADs in these strategies are also fluoxetine, escitalopram, and sertraline. Among AAPs, aripiprazole is the TOC, with risperidone also considered a first-line option.

For disruptive mood dysregulation disorder (DMDD), there is no recommended first-line strategy in KMAP-DD 2025. Several second-line strategies are suggested, including AD monotherapy, AAP monotherapy, AD with AAP combination, mood stabilizer monotherapy, mood stabilizer with AAP, and mood stabilizer with AD. Clonidine is also considered a potential option in DMDD. Among ADs, escitalopram, fluoxetine, and sertraline are recommended as first-line agents. For AAPs, aripiprazole is considered TOC, with risperidone also recommended as a first-line option. Regarding mood stabilizers, valproate is TOC for males, while for females, lithium, valproate, and lamotrigine are considered second-line options.

NICE 2022 does not include treatment strategies for children and adolescents. However, we can provide a brief overview of the NICE guidelines for children and adolescents published in 2019. For mild depression, ADs are not recommended; psychological therapies (e.g. CBT, IPT) are preferred. For moderate to severe depression, fluoxetine is the TOC, but only in combination with psychological therapy and after inadequate response to therapy alone. Sertraline or citalopram may be considered if fluoxetine is ineffective. AAP augmentation may be considered for psychotic depression.

RANZCP 2020 offers a structured approach to managing depression in children and adolescents. For mild depression, the recommendation is active monitoring without immediate pharmacological intervention. In cases of moderate to severe depression, the TOC is fluoxetine, either alone or in combination with CBT or IPT. Close monitoring is strongly advised for emergent suicidality, hostility, agitation, mania, and behavioral changes during pharmacotherapy.

BAP 2015 guideline notes that the use of ADs in children and adolescents remains controversial, primarily due to the risk–benefit balance and variability in efficacy across drug types. Meta-analytic data indicates a significant overall benefit of ADs over placebo in youth populations, though the effect size varies by drug. Among ADs, fluoxetine is recommended as TOC due to its superior efficacy and consistent evidence across age groups. Citalopram, sertraline, and venlafaxine showed smaller but positive effects. Paroxetine and TCAs are not recommended due to low efficacy and increased risk. However, there is also a risk of an increase in suicides, which emphasizes close monitoring.

To summarize pharmacotherapy in children and adolescents, except for KMAP-DD 2025, none of the guidelines recommend pharmacotherapy as first-line treatment. However, for medication use, KMAP-DD 2025 and the other CPGs were consistent in selecting fluoxetine as the TOC.

### Pharmacological Treatment for Late-life Depression

According to KMAP-DD 2025, the TOC for non-psychotic mild to moderate late-life depression is AD monotherapy, with escitalopram most strongly recommended. Other first-line ADs include sertraline, duloxetine, fluoxetine, desvenlafaxine, mirtazapine, venlafaxine, and vortioxetine. For non-psychotic severe depression, AD monotherapy, AAP augmentation with AD, and two ADs combination are all considered first-line. In psychotic depression, the TOC is AAP augmentation with AD, with aripiprazole as the preferred AAP, and quetiapine and olanzapine also first-line options.

CANMAT 2023 recommended using any adult first-line AD in late-life depression, particularly SSRIs and SNRIs for their safety profiles. In treatment-resistant or severe cases, switching ADs, augmentation with lithium or AAPs, or biological therapies such as ECT and rTMS are suggested. Vortioxetine is highlighted for its cognitive benefits in older adults.

RANZCP 2020 recommended SSRIs in older adults due to better tolerability but advises avoiding paroxetine and using citalopram/escitalopram with caution due to QTc prolongation. This differs from KMAP-DD 2025, which recommended escitalopram as the first-line treatment. Duloxetine and vortioxetine are suitable alternatives, especially when cognitive symptoms are present. Lithium may be used cautiously for augmentation. Psychological therapies like CBT, IPT, and problem-solving therapy are also recommended.

In BAP 2015, SSRIs remain the preferred first-line agents, though citalopram requires electrocardiogram (ECG) monitoring and paroxetine is avoided. Mirtazapine is favored when insomnia or appetite loss is present. Venlafaxine, duloxetine, and vortioxetine are also suitable options. For resistant depression, lithium, AAPs, or psychostimulants may be used with caution. ECT is endorsed as safe and effective in severe or psychotic cases.

In summary, KMAP-DD 2025 prioritizes the therapeutic effects of depression and recommends the use of SSRIs such as escitalopram and sertraline, as well as combination therapy for severe depression. On the other hand, other CPGs recommend drugs based on cognitive effects, such as vortioxetine and duloxetine, considering their cognitive benefits, and recommended non-pharmacological biological treatments like ECT and rTMS or psychologic interventions.

### Pharmacological Treatment for Depression in Women

Among the five CPGs, KMAP-DD 2025 is the only one that provides specific pharmacological recommendations for premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD), identifying escitalopram, fluoxetine, sertraline, paroxetine, and desvenlafaxine as first-line ADs. This focus on PMDD is not addressed in CANMAT 2023, NICE 2022, RANZCP 2020, or BAP 2015.

For antenatal depression, all five CPGs emphasize caution with pharmacological treatment and recommend psychological therapy (e.g., CBT or IPT) as the preferred first-line approach in mild to moderate cases. KMAP-DD 2025 stands out by recommending AD monotherapy or rTMS as first-line treatment even during pregnancy, and lists AAP augmentation with AD or ECT as first-line treatment for psychotic antenatal depression. In contrast, CANMAT 2023 and RANZCP 2020 support the use of ADs in severe or refractory cases but recommend avoiding paroxetine, fluoxetine, and venlafaxine due to safety concerns.

Regarding postpartum depression, KMAP-DD 2025 recommends both AD monotherapy and AD + AAP combination as first-line options for mild to moderate cases and designates AD + AAP as the TOC in severe cases. This is consistent with RANZCP 2020 and CANMAT 2023, which support pharmacological treatment or ECT in severe or psychotic cases, while favoring psychotherapy for non-severe presentations.

In summary, KMAP-DD 2025 is distinguished by its diagnosis-specific pharmacological strategies for PMDD, antenatal, and postpartum depression, including the use of AD, AAP, rTMS, and ECT where appropriate. Other guidelines provide more general approaches or defer to external perinatal guidance but share a common emphasis on risk–benefit analysis, specialist collaboration, and psychological therapies as first-line in perinatal patients.

### Choosing Antidepressants for Specific Situations

Among the major clinical guidelines, KMAP-DD 2025 offers the most structured and detailed framework for selecting ADs based on side effects and safety profiles. It recommends first-line ADs tailored to specific tolerability concerns: bupropion is preferred for patients with sexual dysfunction, sedation, weight gain, risk of falls, and orthostatic hypotension, while mirtazapine is favored in cases of insomnia, gastrointestinal symptoms, or suicidal ideation. The guideline also provides nuanced recommendations for minimizing risks such as serotonin syndrome and anticholinergic burden, offering a clear, symptom-specific approach to AD selection.

CANMAT 2023 similarly emphasizes tolerability, although in a less prescriptive format. It highlights bupropion, vortioxetine, and agomelatine as favorable choices for patients at risk of sexual dysfunction or weight gain, while noting citalopram as a QTc-prolonging agent requiring ECG monitoring in certain cases. It also addresses hepatic risks, advising caution with agomelatine, bupropion, duloxetine, and nefazodone in patients with liver disease.

BAP 2015 also places side effects considerations at the center of its pharmacological strategy. It also recommends bupropion, vortioxetine, agomelatine, and mirtazapine in patients experiencing sexual dysfunction or emotional blunting on SSRIs, and warns against the use of citalopram in those with cardiovascular risk. The guideline additionally advises avoiding TCAs and MAOIs in overdose-prone individuals and encourages dose adjustments or switching agents when intolerable side effects arise.

In summary, KMAP-DD 2025 distinguishes itself by offering a symptom-targeted algorithm for AD selection, while CANMAT 2023 and BAP 2015 contribute practical guidance based on tolerability, age, and clinical judgment. Across all guidelines, there is strong convergence around the safer and more tolerable profiles of bupropion, vortioxetine, and agomelatine, and consistent caution toward citalopram, paroxetine, and TCAs in vulnerable patients.

In case of depressive patients with chronic illnesses, among the five guidelines, KMAP-DD 2025 offers the most detailed recommendations for treating depression in patients with comorbid medical illnesses. Escitalopram is broadly preferred across conditions such as diabetes, thyroid disease, and renal or hepatic dysfunction, while sertraline is favored in cardiovascular disease, and duloxetine in cases with chronic pain.

In contrast, CANMAT 2023 and RANZCP 2020 emphasize individualized treatment, advising clinicians to avoid drug interactions and choose well-tolerated ADs. Both guidelines support SSRIs, especially sertraline and escitalopram, and recommend vortioxetine or duloxetine for patients with cognitive symptoms or fatigue.

BAP 2015 also supports SSRIs as first-line agents in medical illness, particularly sertraline and citalopram, and advises caution with TCAs and SSRIs in patients at cardiovascular or bleeding risk, respectively. NICE 2022 similarly favors SSRIs like sertraline in patients with chronic physical illness and supports collaborative care for those with functional impairment.

In summary, KMAP-DD 2025 is unique in offering disease-specific AD recommendations, while the other guidelines promote tolerability, safety, and individualized care, with consistent preference for SSRIs and avoidance of TCAs in high-risk patients.

### Non-pharmacological Biological Treatments

#### Electroconvulsive therapy

All five guidelines recognize ECT as a highly effective treatment for severe MDE and TRD, particularly when rapid symptom relief is needed. KMAP-DD 2025 recommends ECT for patients with psychotic features, catatonia, melancholic features, or acute suicide risk, and considers it relatively safe in older adults, those with medical comorbidities, and even during pregnancy. Especially when MDE with psychotic features presents with acute suicidal risk, ECT was the TOC. This is an increase in preference over the previous KMAP-DD 2021. However, the preference for ECT was found to be lower than other CPGs, such as the third treatment strategies for depression introduced above. This is thought to be due to the actual utilization of ECT surveyed together in KMAP-DD 2025, where 57% of all respondents said that it is difficult to implement due to problems in environmental conditions, and the actual utilization of ECT is declining compared to its clinical importance due to cost problems, manpower problems, and problems in composing treatment settings.

CANMAT 2023 supports ECT as one of the most effective options in TRD, especially when psychosis, suicidality, or functional impairment are present. It highlights ultra-brief right unilateral ECT as a preferred method due to fewer cognitive side effects and recommends continuation ECT or maintenance pharmacotherapy to prevent relapse. RANZCP 2020 likewise lists ECT as a first-line treatment for severe MDEs with melancholic or psychotic features, catatonia, or high suicide risk, and supports its use across special populations, including elderly patients, pregnant women, and adolescents. NICE 2022 recommends ECT when other treatments have failed, when the patient is at immediate risk, or has responded well to ECT in the past. BAP 2015 emphasizes ECT’s superior speed and efficacy compared to pharmacotherapy in urgent cases, but also notes concerns memory impairment, particularly in older adults and those with pre-existing cognitive vulnerabilities. Across all guidelines, ECT is positioned as a key option in severe, psychotic, or life-threatening depression, with attention to individual risk–benefit considerations and strategies for relapse prevention.

#### Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation

All five guidelines recognize rTMS as a safe and effective noninvasive treatment for TRD. KMAP-DD 2025 recommends rTMS for patients who have not responded to pharmacotherapy or who cannot tolerate ADs, positioning it as an appropriate option in moderate to severe depression or in cases requiring nonpharmacological approaches. This is consistent with CANMAT 2023, which provides the most detailed guidance. It endorses high-frequency left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) stimulation, low-frequency right DLPFC stimulation, and intermittent theta burst stimulation as first-line protocols, and supports newer approaches such as accelerated rTMS and sequential bilateral stimulation. rTMS may be used as a first-line neuromodulation strategy in patients unable to use ADs. RANZCP 2020 and BAP 2015 view rTMS as a viable option after multiple failed AD trials, noting modest but clinically meaningful efficacy. Both highlight its favorable safety profile and support its use particularly in TRD, while also cautioning about variability in outcomes and the need for maintenance strategies. NICE 2022 mentions rTMS as a potential intervention in specialist or research settings, advising clinicians to offer it only after discussing the benefits, limitations, and availability with patients.

In summary, KMAP-DD 2025 aligns with international consensus in recommending rTMS primarily for TRD or medication-intolerant patients, while CANMAT 2023 provides the most protocol-specific guidance. Other guidelines support its role as a second-line or adjunctive intervention, particularly in cases where standard pharmacotherapy is insufficient or not tolerated.

## CONCLUSION

In this review, we compared the recommendations from KMAP-DD 2025 with those from recently published international CPGs, including CANMAT 2023, NICE 2022, RANZCP 2020, and BAP 2015. While KMAP-DD 2025 is based on expert consensus and the others primarily reflect evidence-based approaches, there was substantial alignment in overall treatment principles. However, notable differences were observed in the level of specificity and clinical priorities.

KMAP-DD 2025 offers distinct advantages in areas where clinical evidence is limited or difficult to establish, such as depression with comorbid medical illness, PMDD, perinatal depression, and special populations including older adults, adolescents, and patients with high suicide risk. Its structured, situation-specific recommendations like condition-based first-line AD selection and early use of combination strategies reflect clinical realities and local practice needs, including a preference for more rapid and pragmatic treatment approaches.

While expert-based, KMAP-DD 2025 complements existing evidence-based guidelines by filling in decision-making gaps, particularly in complex or under-researched clinical scenarios. As such, it remains a valuable, practice-oriented reference for Korean clinicians and may serve as a culturally relevant supplement to international standards in the management of depressive disorders.
