# Meeting Highlights: The 11th Korean Breast Cancer Society Consensus Conference on Axillary Management in Early Breast Cancer

## Abstract

The 11th Korean Breast Cancer Society (KBCS) Consensus Meeting was convened to discuss the current expert opinions and practice patterns regarding controversial issues in the axillary management of early breast cancer in Korea. Fifty-three multidisciplinary experts participated in the panel voting. The case-based questions addressed three major domains: 1) omission of sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) in clinically node-negative disease, 2) management of sentinel lymph node (SLN) micrometastases after upfront surgery, and 3) axillary management after neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) for biopsy-proven node-positive disease. The panelists showed a cautious attitude toward SLNB omission in relatively younger postmenopausal patients, even when the clinicopathologic features were consistent with contemporary de-escalation trials, whereas support for omission increased substantially in older low-risk patients. For SLN micrometastases after upfront surgery, completion of axillary lymph node dissection (ALND) is rarely supported, although opinions vary regarding the need for axillary radiotherapy. In the neoadjuvant setting, routine clip placement in biopsy-proven positive nodes has not yet been universally adopted. However, there is strong support for omitting ALND in patients who convert to a clinically node-negative status with negative SLNs after NAC. The management of residual nodal disease after NAC remains controversial, particularly in cases of residual macrometastasis. The 11th KBCS Consensus Meeting demonstrated broad acceptance of axillary surgical de-escalation in selected clinical scenarios, while highlighting a persistently uncertain area. These findings emphasize the need for individualized, multidisciplinary decision-making and further prospective evidence to refine the axillary management of early breast cancer.

## INTRODUCTION

The axillary management of early breast cancer has evolved substantially over the past several decades, with an increasing emphasis on surgical de-escalation while maintaining oncologic safety. Sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) has replaced routine axillary lymph node dissection (ALND) for clinically node-negative breast cancer, and accumulating evidence supports the omission of additional axillary surgery in selected patients with limited sentinel lymph node (SLN) involvement. More recently, the role of SLNB has been questioned, and several contemporary trials, including SOUND, INSEMA, and BOOG, have provided evidence supporting SLNB omission in selected patients with clinically and radiologically node-negative low-risk breast cancer.

Axillary management following neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) has become increasingly complex. In patients with biopsy-proven node-positive disease who convert to a clinically node-negative status after NAC, SLNB, and targeted axillary dissection have emerged as alternatives to routine ALND. Nevertheless, several practical issues remain unresolved, including the routine use and timing of clip placement in biopsy-proven positive nodes, acceptable number of retrieved SLNs, and management of residual nodal disease after NAC. These questions are particularly relevant in the modern treatment era in which nodal downstaging is increasingly achieved with effective systemic therapies, including human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-targeted therapy and immunotherapy-based regimens.

The Korean Breast Cancer Society (KBCS) has regularly develops and revises clinical practice guidelines to provide evidence-based recommendations tailored to national clinical situations. As part of this effort, the KBCS has held periodic consensus conferences to address controversial issues where high-level evidence may be lacking or where clinical practice varies among experts. Following the success of previous conferences, the 11th KBCS Consensus Meeting was convened on January 24, 2026, in Seoul, Republic of Korea. Case-based questions were prepared to capture current practices and expert opinions on axillary management. The conference panel involved 53 experts, including 27 surgical oncologists, 18 medical oncologists, 6 radiation oncologists, and 2 pathologists (Supplementary Table 1).

Panelists were allowed to select “abstain” if they did not consider themselves sufficiently expert on a given question or found it difficult to choose one answer. The questions were organized into three domains: 1) Omission of SLNB in clinically node-negative patients with early breast cancer; 2) axillary management for SLN micrometastasis in patients who underwent upfront breast surgery; and 3) treatment for residual nodal disease after NAC in patients with biopsy-proven node-positive disease. Voting percentages were reported descriptively. Because the number of voting participants may have varied by question, and abstentions were permitted, formal statistical comparisons were not performed. Details of the recommendations regarding treatment decisions related to these topics are provided in Supplementary Data 1.

## CONFERENCE HIGHLIGHTS: PANEL DELIBERATION

### SLNB omission

The first case involved a 52-year-old postmenopausal woman with left breast cancer planned for breast-conserving surgery (BCS) and whole-breast irradiation. The tumor was cT1c (1.1 cm), ultrasonography (U/S)-negative in the axilla, strongly estrogen receptor (ER)- and progesterone receptor (PR)-positive, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative (HER2−), and had a low Ki-67 index (Q1). When asked whether they would omit SLNB in the current clinical practice, 20.0% of the panelists answered yes, whereas 47.5% would perform SLNB (Figure 1). Thus, although the case met the clinicopathologic features of the SOUND and INSEMA trials, broad omission had not become routine practice among panelists.

A related question asked when the omission of SLNB was appropriate for a 52-year-old postmenopausal woman (Q2). Only 4.9% supported the omission for all eligible U/S-negative T1-2 patients. The largest group (39.0%) supported omission only for very low-risk patients, such as postmenopausal women with T1, ER-strong positivity, low Ki-67, and low-grade disease, whereas routine SLNB was selected by 31.7%. This suggests that a substantial proportion of panelists still prefer SLNB and that even among those supporting SLNB omission, most favored selective application rather than unrestricted implementation across all U/S-negative T1-2 tumors.

The next case tested whether old age increased the acceptance of SLNB omission (Q3). In a 72-year-old postmenopausal woman with cT1bN0, strongly ER/PR-positive, HER2−, and low Ki-67 disease who planned to undergo BCS, 65.0% supported SLNB omission, 12.5% supported omission only in selected low-risk elderly patients, and only 5.0% favored SLNB. These results indicate that the Korean panel was substantially more comfortable omitting SLNB in older low-risk patients than in similar low-risk women in their 50s (Figure 1).

### Management of SLN micrometastasis after upfront surgery

The following two questions (Q4–5) addressed a 60-year-old postmenopausal woman with hormone receptor-positive (HR+), HER2− pT1c disease and one SLN micrometastasis measuring 0.3 mm. After BCS and SLNB, the most frequently selected options were axillary radiotherapy (RT) alone (40.9%) and SLNB with breast RT alone (38.6%). None of the respondents selected completion ALND; 6.8% favored a selective approach, and 13.6% abstained (Figure 1). These results show a clear rejection of completion ALND in this low-volume nodal disease scenario after BCS with SLNB; however, they also reveal uncertainty regarding whether the undissected axilla should be deliberately irradiated.

When the same pathologic scenario followed mastectomy rather than BCS, the experts’ preferences shifted slightly more toward axillary RT: 48.7% selected axillary RT without further axillary surgery and 35.9% selected no further axillary treatment, including ALND and axillary RT. Nevertheless, the very low support for the completion ALND (2.6%) reflects the broad acceptance of surgical de-escalation for SLN micrometastases (Figure 1).

### Axillary management after NAC

The first question in the neoadjuvant setting concerned clip placement for biopsy-proven positive axillary lymph nodes (Q6). The largest group (45.5%) did not routinely place a clip, whereas 31.8% favored clip placement before starting NAC. Only a small proportion of patients selected clip placement during or after completion of NAC. This highlights the gap between the technical rationale for targeted axillary dissection and its universal adoption in routine practice.

In a 45-year-old premenopausal woman with triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), initial cT2N1 disease, and biopsy-proven nodal metastasis who converted to clinically node-negative after NAC, 89.2% of the panelists selected SLNB alone without further axillary surgery when SLNB showed no malignancy in the three SLNs (Q7). Notably, none of the panelists selected the completion ALND. When only one or two negative SLNs were retrieved, support for SLNB alone remained high at 76.2% (Q8). These findings indicate that the panel strongly supported the omission of ALND in patients with clinical nodal complete response and negative SLNB after NAC, even when the number of SLNs removed was limited (Figure 2).

The final two questions addressed residual nodal disease after NAC (Q9–10). In a patient who underwent BCS and SLNB after downstaging to ycT0N0, with final pathology showing residual breast disease and one of the three SLNs containing a macrometastasis, voting was divided as follows: 33.3% favored completion ALND, 37.8% favored axillary RT without additional surgery, and 15.6% favored individualized management. In contrast, when residual nodal disease was limited to micrometastases or isolated tumor cells (ITCs), 83.3% favored axillary RT without additional surgery, whereas only 4.8% supported the completion ALND (Figure 2). Thus, the degree of residual nodal burden after NAC is an important factor in axillary lymph node management.

Taken together, the four post-NAC scenarios traced a graded pattern of axillary management decisions along the spectrum of residual nodal burden (Figure 2). As the disease evolved from completely negative SLNB (Q7) to limited-yield negative SLNB (Q8), ITC/micrometastasis (Q10), and finally to macrometastasis (Q9), the panel’s preference shifted progressively from SLNB alone through axillary RT, toward a divided opinion between axillary RT and completion ALND.

## DISCUSSION

A notable finding was the cautious attitude toward SLNB omission in a relatively young postmenopausal woman aged 52 years, even when the clinicopathologic features were consistent with contemporary de-escalation trials. Based on the results of the SOUND and INSEMA trials, the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) guidelines state that SLNB omission may be offered to postmenopausal women aged ≥ 50 years with cT1, grade I–II, HR+HER2− breast cancer, and no suspicious axillary lymph nodes on U/S. Nevertheless, in the present case, only a minority of panelists supported SLNB omission for all eligible U/S-negative, T1–2 tumors. These results indicate that many Korean experts remain reluctant to broadly omit SLNB in patients in their 50s, even those with low-risk diseases. Instead, the panel favored a highly selective approach based on very low-risk tumor biology, including small tumor size, strong HR positivity, low Ki-67 index, and tumor grade.

Previous studies have also reported that even among elderly patients aged 70 years or older, SLNB is more frequently performed in real-world clinical practice than SLNB omission. The NATALEE trial demonstrated the survival benefit of adjuvant cyclin-dependent kinases 4/6 inhibitors in patients with node-positive HR+HER2− negative early breast cancer, thereby increasing the potential therapeutic relevance of identifying nodal involvement. Thus, even when a patient appears eligible for SLNB omission based on the de-escalation trial criteria, some clinicians may still prefer nodal staging if the result could influence decisions regarding adjuvant systemic therapy. Although this issue was not fully addressed in the present consensus meeting, further investigation is needed to identify the barriers that prevent evidence from clinical trials from being directly translated into real-world clinical practice. Furthermore, axillary management in cases involving macrometastatic SLNs, particularly in settings similar to those in the SENOMAC trial, warrants detailed deliberation.

In contrast, support for SLNB omission increased markedly in older (72-year-old) low-risk patients. This finding suggests that age is an important factor in the clinical acceptance of SLNB omission. The panel appeared to weigh the expected clinical value of nodal staging against competing risks and lower likelihood that nodal information would substantially alter systemic treatment decisions in older patients. Therefore, although randomized controlled trials have expanded the evidence base for SLNB omission in selected patients (Table 1), real-world adoption in Korea may initially be concentrated on elderly patients with favorable tumor biology rather than on all trial-eligible patients.

Another important issue is that RT is moving toward de-escalation. Based on indirect evidence from the CALGB 9343 and PRIME II trials, the American Society for Radiation Oncology guidelines state that partial breast irradiation may be offered to patients aged ≥ 40 years with cT1, grade I–II, and ER-positive breast cancer. These criteria largely overlap with those of the ASCO guidelines for SLNB omission (Table 1). However, direct evidence is currently lacking regarding whether SLNB and RT can be omitted simultaneously or which treatment can be safely omitted in specific patient subgroups. Therefore, multidisciplinary decision making is particularly important.

For patients with SLN micrometastasis after upfront surgery, no experts recommended completion ALND in the BCS scenario, and only 2.6% selected completion ALND in the mastectomy scenario. These findings reflect the broad acceptance of ALND omission in cases of limited SLN metastasis and mirror the landmark results of the ACOSOG Z0011 trial. However, the voting pattern revealed uncertainties regarding the application of axillary RT. In the BCS scenario, votes were nearly evenly divided between axillary and breast RT alone, whereas in the mastectomy scenario, the panel shifted more toward axillary RT. This difference likely reflects the fact that tangential whole-breast irradiation may provide incidental coverage of part of the lower axilla, whereas mastectomy patients do not routinely receive this incidental axillary treatment unless regional nodal irradiation is deliberately planned.

The present case involved a single micrometastasis to an SLN. Although the IBCSG 23-01, AATRM, and SENOMIC trials have demonstrated that ALND omission is safe without axillary RT in cases limited to SLN micrometastases, a significant proportion of experts have maintained a conservative stance by selecting axillary RT. This indicates that, while there is a near-universal consensus that completion ALND is not necessary in this scenario, the necessity and optimal extent of regional nodal irradiation continue to rely on individualized clinical judgement.

Despite the technical rationale for targeted axillary dissection, routine clip placement in biopsy-proven positive nodes has not been widely adopted. The largest group did not routinely place a clip, whereas approximately one-third favored clip placement before NAC. This finding highlights a practical gap between the evidence supporting the targeted removal of initially positive nodes and its implementation in daily practice. In contrast to the present Korean consensus, an European Breast Cancer Research Association of Surgical Trialists (EUBREAST) survey showed that targeted axillary dissection was the most preferred axillary staging strategy after NAC among panelists and was selected by 54.2% of respondents. This discrepancy suggests that the use of targeted axillary dissection varies substantially across countries and practice environments. Possible reasons include variations in institutional workflow, radiologic accessibility, cost or reimbursement issues, confidence in SLNB alone, and uncertainty about whether clip retrieval is mandatory when an adequate number of SLNs are removed.

Among the practical issues raised during the panel deliberation, the current Korean reimbursement system, which covers only a single clip placement per patient, has been identified as a major driver of variation in clip practices. Under this constraint, many clinicians preferentially mark biopsy-proven positive axillary nodes, whereas clip placement in primary breast tumors is often omitted. However, in the contemporary therapeutic landscape—in which HER2-targeted therapy and immunotherapy-containing regimens increasingly produce a pathologic complete response in both the breast and axilla —panelists have expressed strong support for clip placement in all clinically relevant lesions, including primary tumors, satellite lesions, and biopsy-proven positive axillary nodes, to enable accurate surgical localization when imaging-detectable disease may no longer be present at the time of surgery. There is a broad consensus among the panelists that revision of the current reimbursement policy is necessary to align clinical practice with this evolving therapeutic context.

Meanwhile, the panel showed strong support for the omission of ALND in patients with biopsy-proven node-positive disease who converted to a clinically node-negative status after NAC. In a clinical scenario in which three SLNs were retrieved and all were negative, 100% of the voting panelists (excluding abstentions) supported SLNB alone. This indicates that the results of landmark trials, such as ACOSOG Z1071, SENTINA, and SN-FNAC, have been firmly established in routine clinical practice. Interestingly, although previous studies have shown an increased false-negative rate when fewer than three SLNs are retrieved, most experts (76.2%) still favor omitting ALND, even with a limited number of negative SLNs. These findings suggest that for many practitioners, the overall clinical context, including a robust response to NAC and negative axillary lymph node pathologic findings, takes precedence over the strict numerical thresholds for SLN retrieval.

The management of residual nodal disease after NAC has emerged as the most controversial topic among panelists. In cases in which only ITCs or micrometastases were identified by SLNB after NAC, most panelists favored axillary RT over completion ALND. This clinical preference is supported by two large international multicenter retrospective cohort studies, the OPBC-05/ICARO and OPBC-07/microNAC studies, which showed that the completion ALND did not improve survival in these clinical situations. A particularly interesting observation arose from the subgroup analysis of the microNAC study, which indicated that the completion ALND reduced axillary recurrence, specifically in TNBC. Despite the case being presented to the panel as a TNBC scenario, most experts still favored axillary RT over ALND. This choice reflects an acknowledgement of the inherent limitations of the microNAC study, such as the nature of retrospective subgroup analyses and the fact that most patients with TNBC were not treated with modern immunotherapy regimens.

An even more intriguing finding was observed when the residual nodal disease consisted of one SLN macrometastasis. Although definitive high-level evidence is not yet available, the votes were nearly evenly split between the completion ALND and axillary RT without additional surgery. This degree of support for the omission of ALND appears to be higher than that reported in previous international surveys. In the American Society of Breast Surgeons survey, the proportion of surgeons who would consider omitting ALND in patients initially presenting with cN1 disease was approximately 50% when the residual disease consisted of one micrometastatic SLN, but only in the low 20% range when one macrometastatic SLN was present. Moreover, respondents were particularly reluctant to omit ALND in patients with TNBC. Similarly, in the EUBREAST survey, ALND was selected by approximately 60% of the respondents when the residual nodal disease was micrometastatic or greater. However, these surveys should be interpreted in light of the fact that their responses were collected approximately 2–4 years earlier than the present consensus meeting, before the publication of several recent retrospective datasets, and broader clinical familiarity with ongoing de-escalation trials.

Ongoing studies, including retrospective macro-NAC studies and prospective randomized controlled trials, such as TAXIS, Alliance A011202, and ADARNAT, are expected to clarify whether axillary RT can safely replace ALND in this setting. Nevertheless, the fact that approximately half of the experts already favor axillary RT over ALND suggests a strong anticipation of positive outcomes in these ongoing trials. Moreover, this voting pattern reflects a significant clinical shift, as many experts now align with the hypothesis that regional nodal irradiation, with or without advances in systemic therapy, may provide sufficient locoregional control even in the presence of limited residual macrometastatic disease after NAC.

## CONCLUSION

The 11th KBCS Consensus Meeting highlighted the ongoing transition toward axillary de-escalation in early breast cancer while also revealing areas of persistent uncertainty in daily practice. The panel showed broad acceptance of omitting the completion ALND for SLN micrometastases after upfront surgery and for patients with biopsy-proven node-positive disease who converted to a clinically node-negative status and had negative SLNs after NAC. In contrast, SLNB omission in relatively younger postmenopausal patients, routine clip placement before NAC, and management of residual nodal disease after NAC remain controversial. These findings suggest that Korean experts are generally aligned with contemporary de-escalation principles but continue to apply them selectively, considering patient age, tumor biology, and expected impact on adjuvant systemic therapy and RT. Further prospective evidence and a multidisciplinary consensus are needed to refine patient selection and harmonize treatment strategies in the evolving landscape of axillary management.
