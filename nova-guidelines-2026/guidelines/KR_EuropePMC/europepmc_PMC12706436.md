# Defining early axial spondyloarthritis: insights and applications of the Assessment of Spondyloarthritis International Society international consensus

## Abstract

Axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) is a chronic inflammatory disease with variable clinical presentation and often delayed diagnosis. The Assessment of Spondyloarthritis International Society (ASAS) recently proposed a consensus definition of early axSpA to standardize research and improve comparability across studies. This review outlines the rationale and methodology of the ASAS-SPEAR (SPondyloarthritis EARly) initiative and discusses its implications for clinical research. Despite its promise, recent studies applying the definition have shown that treatment outcomes in early axSpA, defined as ≤2 years of symptom duration, do not consistently differ from established disease. Methodological limitations and heterogeneous criteria in earlier literature underscore the need for refined, evidence-based definitions. Future prospective trials using the ASAS criteria and stratified by symptom duration and clinical features are needed to clarify the value of early intervention and its potential impact on disease progression. The ASAS definition offers a valuable research framework, though its application in clinical practice awaits further validation.

## INTRODUCTION

Axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) is a chronic inflammatory disease primarily affecting the spine and sacroiliac joints. It presents with a range of symptoms that can severely diminish the quality of life. Managing axSpA is challenging due to its varied clinical manifestations and the unpredictable course of the disease, which varies substantially between individuals.

Detecting SpA early is pivotal, especially for axSpA, where symptoms involve deeper anatomical structures and are often manifested only by non-specific axial pain. This subtlety can delay both diagnosis and timely treatment initiation, which contribute to longer suffering by the patient. Traditionally, axSpA diagnosis occurs at advanced stages when persistent inflammation has already caused visible structural damage on conventional radiographs, reflecting a population with long-standing disease in most research studies.

The advent of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has transformed diagnostic approaches, enabling earlier detection of inflammatory changes unseen on conventional radiographs. This technological advancement has led to an increased representation of patients with shorter disease durations in clinical trials. Nonetheless, the lack of a standardized approach to classify “early axSpA” introduces significant variability and inconsistency in research, making it difficult to effectively investigate the early phase of the disease and obscuring the optimal timing for therapeutic interventions. This backdrop of variability and the absence of standardized definition have historically hindered the development of effective treatment strategies tailored to the early stages of the disease.

This review provides a comprehensive overview of the development and rationale behind the Assessment of Spondyloarthritis International Society (ASAS) consensus definition of early axSpA, with a focus on its role as a research instrument. It summarizes the challenges encountered in earlier literature due to heterogeneous definitions, outlines the methodological steps of the ASAS-SPEAR (SPondyloarthritis EARly) project, and highlights recent studies that have implemented the new definition. In doing so, this review aims to support researchers in applying standardized criteria in future studies, while acknowledging that clinical implications remain to be established through further evidence.

## MAIN SUBJECTS

### Challenges in standardizing early axSpA definitions: implications for research and clinical outcomes

A systematic literature review (SLR) conducted as part of the ASAS-SPEAR project included 336 publications from 183 studies and highlighted the challenges of defining and investigating early axSpA. The review found that only 62% of the studies provided a clear definition of early SpA, indicating that over one-third referred to the concept without defining it. Among those that did provide a definition, most were based on symptom or disease duration; however, the thresholds and additional criteria varied widely. Some definitions included radiographic findings or multiple composite criteria, underscoring the lack of standardization and complicating both the evaluation of treatment effects in early SpA and the comparability across studies. Only one study included a definition for early peripheral SpA; the vast majority focused on axSpA.

The concept of a “window of opportunity”—suggesting that an early intervention might prevent structural damage and improve long-term outcomes—has been frequently discussed in the context of axSpA. Several studies have explored the efficacy of biologic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (bDMARDs), particularly tumor necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors, in early versus established disease. While some findings may imply that early treatment could modify disease progression, many of these studies lacked standardized definitions of “early” and failed to adequately account for symptom duration. Moreover, treatment comparisons were often limited to patients already receiving bDMARDs, omitting proper control groups (e.g., placebo), which risks attributing favorable outcomes to treatment rather than natural disease variation.

A more recent SLR by Capelusnik et al., conducted within the ASAS-SPEAR framework, addressed this issue by thoroughly evaluating outcomes in both bDMARD and placebo arms. When controlling for both groups, the evidence supporting superior outcomes for early treatment in axSpA was limited. In most studies, clinical outcomes did not differ significantly between early and established disease. Some studies using a symptom-duration cutoff of less than 5 years reported improved outcomes in non-radiographic axSpA, but these results were inconsistent and generally not observed in radiographic axSpA. Additionally, when early axSpA was defined by disease duration or radiographic status, no clear treatment differences emerged.

Importantly, the impact of early intervention on long-term structural progression remains unproven in randomized controlled trials, and methodological challenges—such as inconsistent definitions, absence of comparators, and selection bias due to losses to follow-up over long periods of time—make it difficult to generate definitive evidence.

The lack of a unified definition for “early axSpA” complicated the evaluation of treatment efficacy and underscored the need for an homogeneous classifications to better understand and demonstrate the potential benefits of early treatment. Future studies should not only examine the effectiveness of early therapeutic interventions but also aim to refine and validate consistent criteria for what constitutes “early” axSpA, where needed, when supported by evidence. This includes exploring relevant variables such as symptom duration thresholds, imaging features, and inflammatory burden that might predict differential treatment response. Clarifying these aspects will support more robust study designs, improve comparability across research, and ultimately inform evidence-based clinical decision-making.

### SPEAR project: ASAS definition for early axSpA

The ASAS-SPEAR project was initiated to establish a standardized definition of early axSpA. Although the original plan included defining early peripheral SpA, this was not pursued due to the term’s limited usage in the literature and the absence of sufficient conceptual guidance. The focus was therefore placed exclusively on early axSpA.

The initiative was crucial for improving research consistency and enabling more precise and comparable studies in the field. By developing a consensus-based definition, ASAS-SPEAR addressed longstanding challenges in early axSpA research particularly the variability and lack of standardization in defining what constitutes “early” disease.

The methodology of the project reflected a structured and collaborative approach to filling this gap. Led by two convenors, the project brought together a steering committee of 20 ASAS members, including rheumatologists and methodologists with expertise in SpA, as well as two representatives from Young ASAS. This diverse expert panel was tasked with defining early axSpA.

The project began with the two above-mentioned SLRs that identified existing definitions and examined whether early treatment influences clinical outcomes. These reviews laid the groundwork for in-depth discussions within the working group and the broader ASAS membership.

To achieve consensus, a three-round Delphi survey was conducted among ASAS members. This iterative process enabled the refinement of definition criteria and ensured alignment with expert opinion. The outcomes were continuously discussed in ASAS meetings, maintaining transparency and engagement throughout the process.

The final definition was presented at the 2023 ASAS Annual Meeting, where it was formally endorsed following a vote in accordance with ASAS bylaws. The endorsed definition states that early axSpA is defined as axial symptoms (e.g., spinal or buttock pain, morning stiffness) of ≤2 years’ duration, considered by a rheumatologist to be related to axSpA (Figure 1). This marked a major milestone in efforts to standardize research in early disease.

### Emerging recent studies using the ASAS definition for early axSpA

The introduction of the ASAS standardized definition has facilitated research specifically focused on early axSpA. Despite having been published recently, the definition has already been used in several subsequent studies (Table 1). In the Be-GIANT cohort, 44.3% of patients met the ASAS definition for early axSpA. Patients in this group were significantly younger, yet showed no major differences in disease activity compared to those with established disease.

Ciurea et al. evaluated the effectiveness of TNF inhibitors in early versus established axSpA using the new definition to determine whether earlier intervention influences long-term outcomes. The study included a total of 3,064 patients who were classified as early (≤2 years, n=658) or established (>2 years, n=2,406). After adjustment, TNF inhibitor retention and ASAS 40 response rates at 1 year were comparable between the two groups. These findings suggest that initiating TNF inhibitor treatment within the first 2 years may not yield significantly better clinical outcomes than later initiation, indicating more flexibility in treatment timing than previously assumed.

Alonso et al. compared patients with early axSpA, as defined by ASAS, to those with established disease. While early patients were generally younger and had shorter symptom durations, both groups shared similar profiles in terms of sex distribution, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug usage, and levels of disease activity.

A post-hoc analysis from the SELECT-AXIS 1 and 2 trials assessed the efficacy of upadacitinib across symptom duration subgroups in both radiographic and non-radiographic axSpA. Results showed similar treatment responses between early (≤2 years) and established (>2 years) groups. The same was found when a 5-year cutoff of symptom duration was used. Similarly, a pooled analysis of two Phase 3 studies demonstrated that bimekizumab, BE-MOBILE 1 and 2 trials, provided consistent clinical benefit over 2 years, irrespective of symptom duration. Moreover, a meta-analysis of 11 randomized controlled trials further supported these results, showing no significant differences in short-term efficacy of bDMARDs and targeted synthetic DMARDs (tsDMARDs) between early and established axSpA groups.

Regarding quality of life data within studies applying the ASAS definition for early axSpA, Ciurea et al. analyzed EQ-5D (European Quality of Life 5-domains Questionnaire) and SF-12 (Short Form Questionnaire with 12 questions) scores and found no significant differences between early and established axSpA groups. Similarly, Alonso et al. and the SELECT-AXIS studies assessed ASAS-Health Index and likewise reported no notable differences between these groups.

Radiographic and imaging findings from studies applying the ASAS definition for early axSpA show that patients with early disease generally exhibit less structural damage on conventional radiographs compared to those with established disease. In the Be-GIANT cohort, early axSpA patients had less radiographic sacroiliitis despite similar levels of overall disease activity, while Alonso et al. likewise observed more pronounced structural changes (modified Stoke ankylosing spondylitis spinal score) in established cases. Ciurea et al. further reported a lower prevalence of sacroiliac joint damage on radiographs in early axSpA, but interestingly found that early patients more often showed inflammatory involvement on MRI compared to those with established disease. Meanwhile, post-hoc analyses from the SELECT-AXIS showed a numerically larger change from baseline in MRI inflammation in the sacroiliac joint and spine in patients with early axSpA, but without a statistically significant difference compared to patients with established axSpA.

## CONCLUSION

Korea has national recommendations, for axSpA that emphasize early detection, but no formal recommendations, specifically addressing “early axSpA” as defined by the ASAS-SPEAR initiative. Across Asia, most efforts similarly rely on international standards. The concept of a standardized definition for early axSpA based on symptom duration is a recent development, primarily driven by ASAS, and in an attempt to homogenize research in the field. This review has summarized and clarified the complexities involved in defining and investigating early axSpA. Central to these advancements is the ASAS-SPEAR initiative, which developed a consensus definition for early axSpA. While this definition was designed primarily for research purposes, it has provided a critical framework that may ultimately support clinical decision-making as new evidence emerges.

The standardization offered by ASAS-SPEAR enhances the consistency of clinical research by establishing clear inclusion criteria. This facilitates more comparable study designs and enables researchers to better investigate treatment effects in early disease. Although the initiative’s immediate goal is academic, the resulting insights have the potential to improve patient care and inform future treatment strategies after the adequate research studies are conducted. It must be emphasized that the ASAS early axSpA definition is only applicable in patients who have already been diagnosed with axSpA. Without an established diagnosis, the classification cannot be meaningfully applied. This reinforces that the definition is intended solely for research purposes, not for direct application in clinical practice.

To date, studies applying the ASAS definition of early axSpA have largely been observational cohorts, post-hoc analyses, and meta-analyses of prior trials. These studies consistently show that early treatment—defined as symptom duration ≤2 years—does not lead to superior outcomes compared to treatment in established disease. For example, Ciurea et al. found comparable TNF inhibitor retention and response rates between early and established axSpA; Alonso et al. reported similar disease burden and activity in both groups; and post-hoc analyses from the SELECT-AXIS (upadacitinib) and BE-MOBILE (bimekizumab) trials confirmed consistent efficacy across subgroups. A meta-analysis of 11 randomized trials further showed no significant difference in short-term efficacy of bDMARDs and tsDMARDs between early and established disease.

These findings challenge the notion of a clearly defined “window of opportunity” in axSpA, at least as currently operationalized by the ≤2-year symptom duration cutoff. This does not, however, exclude the possibility that a narrower timeframe—perhaps within the first year of symptom onset—might still influence outcomes. Several factors complicate the interpretation of current data. Most available evidence comes from observational cohorts, post-hoc analyses, or meta-analyses of prior trials, none of which were prospectively designed to directly test this hypothesis. Moreover, these studies have largely focused on short-term clinical endpoints such as ASAS 40 responses, whereas the “window of opportunity” concept fundamentally relates to preventing long-term consequences—an outcome not yet rigorously assessed in these cohorts. It is also possible that the 2-year cutoff, while useful for standardization, may not reflect the true biological threshold at which early intervention confers benefit. However, indirect evidence from cohorts like DESIR does support the hypothesis that early diagnosis and treatment may slow progression, though long-term comparator-controlled data remain lacking.

Future research should prioritize prospective randomized trials that incorporate precise stratification by symptom duration, imaging findings, and inflammatory burden to better identify which patients stand to benefit most from early intervention and to more definitively establish whether very early treatment can alter the disease course. Moving forward, it is essential to design clinical trials that apply the ASAS definition and incorporate advanced methods, including adaptive trial design and stratification by symptom duration. Incorporating predictive biomarkers and analytics may further improve the precision of early axSpA research. This refined approach could provide a more nuanced understanding of the disease and support more personalized therapeutic strategies.
