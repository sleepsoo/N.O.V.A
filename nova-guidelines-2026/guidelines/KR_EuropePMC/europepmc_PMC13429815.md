# Consensus on Behavioral Strategies for Adults with Obesity from the Korean Society for the Study of Obesity

## Abstract

Behavioral modification is a core component of obesity treatment. Many established behavioral programs have been developed within Western cultural and healthcare contexts; however, they may not directly translate to Korean lifestyles, eating patterns, and clinical settings. Thus, we used a consensus-based process to develop culturally adapted behavioral strategies for obesity management in Korean adults to address this gap. A modified nominal group technique was used to generate and refine behavioral strategies. A panel of experts independently proposed candidate strategies, which were subsequently discussed, merged, and ranked across iterative rounds. Strategies with high clinical feasibility and cultural appropriateness were finalized based on a consensus threshold of ≥75% agreement. The expert panel generated behavioral strategy statements, which were consolidated and refined to 10 core behavioral strategies. These strategies span five domains essential to obesity self-management: diet, physical activity, behavioral modification, environmental and clinical support, and psychosocial and physiological factors. This consensus statement provides a culturally grounded, clinically practical behavioral framework for obesity management in Korea. The strategies can be adapted for community health programs and primary care, as well as multidisciplinary obesity clinics. Future research is needed to evaluate implementation effectiveness and scalability in real-world Korean populations.

## INTRODUCTION

Obesity is a growing public health issue in Korea, with prevalence rising from 30.2% in 2012 to 38.4% in 2021, leading to substantial medical and economic burdens., As a complex, chronic disease driven by biological, psychological, and environmental factors, obesity requires a biopsychosocial stepped-care framework.,

While weight reduction is a key component, it should not be the sole objective. A meta-analysis showed individuals maintained only 23.4% of initial weight loss after 4.5 years, highlighting the limitations of weight-centered approaches. Sustainable management thus relies on self-regulation skills, shifting the treatment goal from simple weight loss to optimizing overall health and functioning.

Korean eating culture is distinguished by shared meals, a high frequency of social dining, and rice-centered carbohydrate intake. These cultural characteristics may complicate the application of rigid calorie-counting, portion-control, and pre-packaged meal prescriptions that are commonly recommended in Western obesity programs. In addition, Korean work-life structure, including long working hours, high academic stress, and limited leisure time, contribute to sedentary lifestyles, late-night meals, and sleep irregularity. Psychological factors, including weight stigma and stress-driven emotional eating, are also prevalent., Therefore, nutritional and behavioral strategies suitable for Korean adults with obesity are needed.

Recognizing the limitations of applying Western models, the Korean Society for the Study of Obesity (KSSO) has prioritized developing clinically applicable behavioral strategies tailored to Korean individuals. The aim of this study was to identify, refine, and prioritize these strategies using a nominal group technique (NGT) consensus process to ensure transparency and methodological rigor.

## METHODS

### Study design

This study used the NGT to generate and refine behavioral strategies for obesity management. The NGT is a structured consensus process designed to synthesize expert judgment when empirical evidence is insufficient, heterogeneous, or requires contextual adaptation. The study process was conducted by the Behavioral Therapy Committee of the KSSO between March and June 2025. The NGT was selected for its structured and replicable framework, enabling balanced participation and transparent decision-making among subject-matter experts. The procedure followed the classical NGT framework described by Delbecq and Van de Ven, and was adapted to an online format using Zoom to accommodate logistical feasibility.

### Panel formation and roles

The panel comprised nine expert psychiatrists and psychologists on the Behavioral Therapy Committee of the KSSO. Each participant had at least 5 years of professional experience in clinical care or research related to obesity, eating behavior, or behavioral therapy. Experts were purposively invited based on their recognized expertise and active involvement in obesity treatment or behavioral research.

### Strategy development process

The modified NGT consisted of three sequential stages: (1) silent idea generation, (2) round-robin sharing, clarification, and consolidation, and (3) independent evaluation, ranking, and final selection. In Stage 1 (silent generation), each panelist independently proposed up to 10 candidate strategies in written form, generating 90 initial items. In Stage 2 (round-robin sharing), two structured online meetings were held via Zoom, moderated by one facilitator and one recorder. Each expert presented one idea at a time in rotation until all suggestions were exhausted. Overlapping or semantically similar ideas were merged by group discussion. In Stage 3 (evaluation, ranking, and final selection), the panel first reviewed the consolidated list in an open discussion, during which items were clarified, rephrased, combined, or removed. Panel members then independently evaluated the remaining strategies for clinical feasibility, behavioral specificity, and cultural appropriateness. Strategies meeting the prespecified consensus threshold of ≥75% agreement were retained, resulting in a concise and comprehensive final set of strategies. The three structured stages of the consensus process are presented in Table 1.

Feedback and revision across rounds were conducted between stages. Notes from each session were reviewed by two members and synthesized into conceptually distinct categories. Panel members reviewed the summary documents and provided written feedback. Strategies were reworded to improve clarity, clinical usefulness, and communication to individuals and healthcare providers.

### External review of strategies

The 10 final strategies developed by the Behavioral Therapy Committee were reviewed and confirmed by experts on the KSSO Board of Directors, which included specialists in medicine, nutrition, exercise science, and pediatrics. This step ensured that the resulting strategies reflected multidisciplinary perspectives on obesity treatment. Each strategy was then reformulated into a slogan-style motto, accompanied by representative behavioral action plans to enhance public accessibility and clinical applicability.

### Data analysis

Qualitative analyses of thematic synthesis were performed. Overlapping concepts were grouped, and consensus statements were drafted based on repeated agreement across meetings.

### Ethical considerations

Institutional review board approval was not required as the study did not involve human subjects or identifiable patient data.

## RESULTS

### Overview of consensus outcomes

The expert panel generated 90 preliminary behavioral strategies using the modified NGT, which were sequentially merged, clarified, and refined across three consensus rounds. After consolidation and ranking based on clinical feasibility, behavioral specificity, and cultural relevance, 10 core behavioral strategies were selected. The strategies were logically structured to guide individuals through the behavioral change process: goal setting (Strategies 1–2), establishing core lifestyle habits in diet and activity (Strategies 3–5), enhancing self-regulation skills (Strategies 6–8), and ensuring sustainability through professional support and resilience (Strategies 9–10). The 10 core behavioral strategies are summarized in Table 2.

### Domains for obesity management

The panel categorized the 10 strategies into five core domains: diet, physical activity, behavioral therapy, environmental and health-care systems, and psychosocial and physiological factors. The domains and their interrelationships are presented in Fig. 1.

### Domain 1: diet

Caloric reduction is essential for achieving clinically meaningful weight loss. Dietary recommendations should consider individual metabolic risk profiles and food preferences. Strategies in this domain include “Timely meals and balanced nutrients; slow eating, chewing well, and enjoying every bite!”

### Domain 2: physical activity

Lifestyle modifications for obesity generally recommend ≥150–180 minutes/week of moderate aerobic activity. Although physical activity alone produces limited short-term weight loss, multicomponent interventions combining nutrition and physical activity are effective. The key strategy is ‘move more and move often.’

### Domain 3: behavioral therapy

Behavioral therapy provides structured tools to support sustained changes in eating and activity patterns. The core elements include self-monitoring, stimulus control, problem-solving, motivational interviewing, and cognitive restructuring., Acceptance-based approaches may support long-term adherence. Strategies in this domain include ‘setting realistic goals and having specific plans; focusing on health, not just weight; controlling cravings and managing binge urges; and learning from setbacks and recovering.’

### Domain 4: environmental and healthcare systems factors

Integrating personalization in stepped care can optimize obesity management. This supports collaboration with experts and timely integration of structured professional care when self-guided strategies are insufficient. The relevant strategy is ‘don’t go it alone—work with a specialist!’

### Domain 5: psychosocial and physiological factors

Chronic stress can impair self-regulation and increase the consumption of calorie-dense foods. Stress reduction and psychological support can enhance treatment adherence. Sleep deprivation affects appetite regulation and energy balance, contributing to weight gain. The relevant strategies include ‘sleeping well (maintaining regular restorative sleep) and fighting stress (soothing emotions without eating).’

### Ten behavioral strategies for people with obesity

#### Strategy 1. Realistic goals, specific plans!

Explanation and evidence

This strategy emphasizes the establishment of realistic, individualized goals supported by concrete and actionable plans in behavioral obesity management. Clear goal-setting provides a structured framework that enhances early motivation, supports behavioral adherence, and contributes to long-term weight management. Individuals who set explicit and attainable weight-loss goals are significantly more likely to succeed, with studies demonstrating approximately 10% greater weight reduction compared with those who do not set defined goals.

Target weight and behavioral objectives should be determined based on a comprehensive assessment of the individual’s current weight status, metabolic risk, and obesity-related comorbidities. Setting achievable and incremental goals allows individuals to accumulate small, consistent successes, which helps to restore confidence, improve self-efficacy, and support sustained lifestyle changes.

Examples of specific behavioral recommendations

The initial goal is a 5%–10% reduction in baseline body weight over approximately 6 months. Individuals should translate broad intentions (e.g., “eat healthier,” “exercise more”) into specific behavioral plans detailing when, where, and how the behavior will be applied. Flexibility is essential. Thus, plans should be adjusted in response to changing personal, social, or environmental circumstances to maintain engagement and prevent relapse.

Cultural adaptation and personalization in Korea

Asian populations accumulate visceral fat and develop metabolic complications at lower body mass index (BMI) levels compared with Western populations. Therefore, personalized goals for Koreans should consider waist circumference, metabolic indices, and individual health risk profiles, and not weight alone.

Target population

This strategy is particularly beneficial for individuals who have experienced repeated weight-loss failures, often due to overly ambitious or unrealistic expectations.

#### Strategy 2. Weight down, health up!

Explanation and evidence

Effective obesity care should focus on comprehensive health improvement, including healthier dietary patterns, increasing physical activity and functional capacity, making favorable body composition changes (e.g., reduced fat mass, preserved or increased lean mass), and regulating metabolic indicators, such as blood pressure, lipid profile, and glycemic control.,

Examples of specific behavioral recommendations

Self-monitoring, such as meal logging, daily step counts, or activity tracking, should be encouraged to enhance awareness and reinforce positive behavioral patterns., Improvements in daily habits and metabolic markers should be reinforced, not just weight loss.

Cultural adaptation and personalization in Korea

Since Korean and other East Asian populations experience higher cardiometabolic risk at lower BMI levels than Western populations, treatment goals in Korea should extend beyond achieving a specific weight. They should prioritize reducing obesity-related health risks, improving metabolic function and physical well-being, and maintaining health-supportive lifestyle routines.

Target population

This strategy is particularly important for individuals with obesity-related chronic conditions, such as those with hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and cardiovascular disease. For these individuals, improving metabolic health and overall functioning may provide greater clinical benefit than focusing solely on weight reduction.

#### Strategy 3. Timely meals, balanced nutrients!

Explanation and evidence

Establishing timely meals and maintaining balanced nutrient intake is a practical, evidence-supported approach to improving metabolic health and preventing weight regain. The dual-focus approach of ‘when’ to eat and ‘what’ to eat provides a practical and sustainable framework supporting metabolic health, particularly as irregular eating habits become increasingly common.

Irregular eating patterns and meal skipping have been consistently associated with adverse metabolic outcomes, including weight gain, elevated fasting glucose, and markers of metabolic syndrome. Skipping breakfast, in particular, has been linked to compensatory overeating, poorer nutrient adequacy, and higher BMI values.-

Individuals are encouraged to maintain regular meal schedules and ensure balanced nutrient composition, including sufficient intake of protein, vegetables, and whole grains, to mitigate these risks. These components support satiety, glycemic stability, and healthy weight regulation.,

Examples of specific behavioral recommendations

Meals should be eaten at consistent times each day to establish a stable eating routine. Skipping meals, even on busy days, should be avoided by preparing simple and accessible meal options in advance. At least one meal per day should include protein, vegetables, and whole grains to support satiety and nutritional balance.

Cultural adaptation and personalization in Korea

While the traditional Korean diet has been high in carbohydrates and low in fat, recent national data indicate a shift toward lower carbohydrate and higher protein intake across age groups. Younger adults are experiencing a notable increase in saturated fat consumption. These trends highlight the importance of promoting nutritional balance tailored to age-specific dietary patterns. Nutritional plans should also be adapted to account for individual preferences, lifestyle patterns, and comorbid metabolic conditions. Sustained adherence to balanced macronutrient intake rather than short-term, restrictive dieting is critical for long-term weight and metabolic health.

Target population

This strategy is particularly relevant for individuals who engage in cycles of excessive dietary restriction followed by binge eating, or practice highly restrictive or single-food diet patterns that compromise nutritional balance. Providing a structure of regular, nutritionally balanced meals can help stabilize appetite regulation and reduce the likelihood of relapse into extreme or compensatory eating behaviors.

#### Strategy 4. Slow down, chew well, enjoy every bite!

Explanation and evidence

This strategy promotes mindful eating by encouraging individuals to slow down and pay attention to the eating experience. Eating rapidly can contribute to overeating, as the brain requires time to register satiety signals. In contrast, slower eating has been shown to reduce energy intake, in part by increasing the release of anorexigenic (appetite-suppressing) gut hormones that enhance satiety.

Mindful eating also involves being fully present during meals, with an emphasis on sensory awareness. Strategies such as removing distractions (e.g., television and smartphones) and savoring flavors and textures support the recognition of internal hunger and fullness cues. Research indicates that mindful eating can reduce binge-eating episodes, improve body image satisfaction, and contribute to healthier long-term eating behaviors.

Examples of specific behavioral recommendations

Individuals should put down utensils between bites to slow eating and increase their awareness of satiety. Each bite should be chewed thoroughly (approximately 20–30 chews) to enhance sensory enjoyment and digestion. Distractions, such as television and smartphones, should be avoided during meals to maintain attention to eating. The colors, aromas, textures, and flavors of the food should be intentionally noticed to deepen sensory engagement.

Cultural adaptation and personalization in Korea

In Korea, meals are often shared family-style and consumed relatively quickly, especially in workplace or social dining contexts. Research indicates that rapid eating speeds are independently associated with increased risk of metabolic syndrome in the Korean population. Therefore, implementing mindful eating strategies may require culturally sensitive adjustments. Encouraging pauses for conversation during shared meals can create natural breaks between bites, supporting slower eating without disrupting social harmony. Providing brief, adaptable mindful eating practices is useful for individuals who eat in short lunch breaks or communal dining environments common in Korean workplaces.

Target population

This strategy is particularly beneficial for individuals who eat in response to stress or emotions, have binge-eating tendencies, or habitually eat quickly and struggle to recognize fullness cues.

#### Strategy 5. Move more and move often!

Explanation and evidence

This strategy emphasizes increasing daily physical activity as an essential component of obesity management. While exercise contributes to weight reduction, its benefits extend well beyond weight loss to include improvements in cardiometabolic health, mood regulation, and sleep quality. Reducing sedentary time and incorporating frequent movement breaks throughout the day can increase energy expenditure and improve insulin sensitivity and metabolic markers.,

Integrating movement into daily routines is especially important in Korea, where prolonged sitting is common among office workers, students, and older adults. Small and consistent increases in physical activity can yield meaningful health benefits, even when structured exercise is difficult to maintain.

Examples of specific behavioral recommendations

Whenever possible, take the stairs instead of using the elevator. Get off one bus or subway stop earlier to add short walking intervals to daily routines. If sitting for more than one hour, break up sedentary time by standing, stretching, or walking briefly. On busy days, focus on “a little more, a little longer” rather than perfection (small increments count). Aim for at least 30 minutes of walking per day and resistance training twice per week, adjusting intensity to individual ability.

Cultural adaptation and personalization in Korea

High public transportation use and walkable urban environments in Korea provide natural opportunities to incorporate activity into daily life. Emphasizing everyday movement (e.g., walking, household activity, and stair climbing) may be more acceptable and sustainable than prescribing structured workouts for individuals who face time constraints, limited access to fitness facilities, or psychological resistance to formal exercise. Phrasing such as “move your body more often” may be more motivating than the word “exercise,” especially for individuals with previous negative experiences or exercise avoidance.

Target population

This strategy is particularly appropriate for individuals with limited access to exercise facilities or low motivation for structured exercise, people with prolonged sedentary patterns, such as office workers, homemakers, and older adults, and individuals with low initial weight loss response who may benefit from increasing daily movement before progressing to more intensive exercise programs.

#### Strategy 6. Sleep well—get your regular dose of rest!

Explanation and evidence

Adequate and restorative sleep plays a pivotal role in regulating energy balance, appetite, and mood, all of which significantly influence health behaviors and body weight. Sleep deprivation disrupts neuroendocrine signaling by reducing leptin (a hormone that promotes satiety) and increasing ghrelin (a hormone that stimulates hunger), causing increases in appetite and caloric intake. Short sleep duration, commonly defined as fewer than 6–7 hours per night, has been strongly linked to increased BMI values, central adiposity, impaired glucose metabolism, and heightened cortisol levels due to activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis., Experimental studies have shown that individuals subjected to sleep restriction may consume up to 500 kcal more per day, and meta-analyses estimate a 55% greater risk of obesity among those with chronically short sleep duration.,

Poor sleep quality can also impair circadian rhythm alignment and reduce insulin sensitivity, all of which contribute to weight gain, even in individuals who achieve sufficient sleep duration., Improving sleep parameters has been shown to enhance metabolic outcomes and reinforce behavioral weight-loss efforts.

Examples of specific behavioral recommendations

Individuals should aim for at least 7 hours of sleep per night, maintain a consistent sleep–wake schedule throughout the week, and limit smartphone and other screen use before bedtime.

Implementing these practices must account for real-world barriers, such as irregular work schedules, caregiving demands, and entrenched cultural or academic norms. Individual variability in sleep responses and reliance on self-reported sleep measures should be considered when designing interventions.

Cultural adaptation and personalization in Korea

Chronic sleep deprivation is a widespread concern in Korea. The contributing factors include academic pressure, extended work hours, and the prevalent evening use of digital devices. National surveys indicate that Korean adults average less than 6.8 hours of sleep per night. This finding may partially explain the increasing prevalence of obesity and related metabolic disorders. Importantly, the collectivist cultural context in Korea, which emphasizes shared routines and values within families and workplaces, presents a valuable opportunity to disseminate sleep-promoting behaviors at the community level.

Target population

Health education programs and clinical interventions should be tailored to the social and occupational realities of target populations to maximize efficacy and be delivered with sensitivity to these contextual challenges.

#### Strategy 7. Relieve stress, care for yourself!

Explanation and evidence

This strategy focuses on developing healthy coping mechanisms to manage psychosocial stress. Stress not only acts as a contributing factor in the development of obesity but also as a significant barrier to its treatment. Individuals may experience reduced self-regulatory capacity during periods of stress, which can lead to maladaptive eating behaviors including emotional overeating and binge-eating episodes. Therefore, strengthening stress management skills is an essential component of supporting self-regulation and maintaining progress in obesity management.

Examples of specific behavioral recommendations

A variety of adaptive coping strategies can help mitigate stress responses, including the following: (1) Attentional distraction: briefly shifting focus to uplifting or neutral activities (e.g., walking, listening to music) can reduce emotional intensity and interrupt stress-triggered eating. (2) Emotional expression: techniques such as expressive writing, have been shown to reduce psychological distress and promote improved mental and physical well-being. (3) Relaxation practices: methods such as deep breathing, progressive muscle relaxation, or guided meditation, reduce physiological arousal associated with stress. (4) Social support activation: engaging with trusted family members, friends, or peer support groups provides emotional buffering and enhances resilience.

Cultural adaptation and personalization in Korea

Korean society places strong emphasis on interdependence and social connectedness, and family and community relationships play a major role in shaping emotional experiences. Therefore, stress management interventions should leverage social support systems, such as family involvement, shared coping activities, or community-based programs.

At the same time, it is important to recognize that no single strategy works for everyone. Individuals should be encouraged to identify coping approaches that feel meaningful, enjoyable, and sustainable, and to practice them consistently as part of daily routines.

Target population

This strategy is particularly beneficial for individuals who engage in emotional or stress-related overeating or experience co-occurring depression, anxiety, or psychological distress. Strengthening self-compassion and stress regulation capacity can support healthier eating patterns and improve adherence to long-term obesity treatment goals.

#### Strategy 8. Control cravings, tame binge urges!

Explanation and evidence

Binge eating and obesity-related overeating often arise when negative emotions trigger impulsive eating, which may, over time, evolve into a habitual and compulsive pattern. Two principal mechanisms commonly interact in the development of binge-eating behavior: heightened reward sensitivity, in which food is experienced as intensely pleasurable or comforting, increasing cravings for palatable foods, and reduced inhibitory control, which manifests as difficulty resisting urges to eat despite the awareness of negative consequences., These mechanisms reinforce compulsive overeating cycles, making binge-related weight gain difficult to reverse without targeted psychological and behavioral interventions.

Behavioral approaches to binge eating and obesity-related overeating include nutritional guidance, physical-activity promotion, behavioral skills and cognitive-control training, and motivational or supportive counseling.

Examples of specific behavioral recommendations

Regular meal and snack schedules should be maintained to prevent excessive hunger and reduce vulnerability to binge episodes. Low-glycemic index carbohydrates should be selected to stabilize blood glucose and avoid rapid hunger rebound. Urge-management techniques, such as deep breathing, short walks, or “urge surfing,” should be practiced when cravings arise. Social support can be cultivated by connecting regularly with trusted people who provide empathy, accountability, and encouragement.

Cultural adaptation and personalization in Korea

In Korea, the widespread availability of food-delivery services and convenience foods allows for instant access to palatable, energy-dense meals. These environmental factors can amplify impulsive eating tendencies. Effective interventions include environmental control strategies, such as planning meals in advance and pre-packing healthy options, limiting exposure to delivery-app prompts (e.g., disabling push notifications and removing saved payment methods), and identifying alternative coping strategies (for example, calling a friend, stretching, or journaling, before cravings occur).

Such preventive measures can reduce impulse-driven ordering and help individuals maintain structured eating patterns outside of regular mealtimes.

Target population

This strategy is particularly beneficial for individuals who experience binge-eating or loss-of-control episodes, eat in response to emotional distress, stress, or loneliness, or struggle to resist highly palatable foods despite awareness of negative health impacts.

#### Strategy 9. Don’t go it alone—work with a specialist!

Explanation and evidence

If weight loss goals are not achieved, the level of support needs to be systematically increased, for example, with structured, therapist-guided behavioral treatment. Importantly, clinicians trained in obesity management provide more than general advice. They offer skills-based behavioral support, therapeutic guidance, and medical oversight rather than simply instructing individuals on ‘what’ to do.

Healthcare professionals, including primary care physicians, nurses, registered dietitians, psychologists, and exercise specialists, play key roles by assessing and managing obesity-related medical conditions, discussing weight concerns nonjudgmentally, and helping individuals identify and access evidence-based weight management programs.

Professional support helps individuals identify appropriate nutrition and activity strategies, develop coping skills, and maintain progress safely over time.

Examples of specific behavioral recommendations

Low-energy diets providing 800–1,200 kcal/day and very-low-energy diets providing fewer than 800 kcal/day should be used only as part of a professionally supervised, multicomponent obesity management program. Very-low-energy diets should be reserved for individuals with a clinically assessed need for rapid weight loss, such as to improve surgical safety or feasibility. Both low-energy and very-low-energy diets should be nutritionally complete and generally limited to no more than 12 weeks.

Cultural adaptation and personalization in Korea

In Korea, unverified weight-loss methods are widely promoted online and in social media, often without medical oversight. Rapid, aggressive weight loss approaches are especially popular but are associated with high rates of weight regain, metabolic disruption, and psychological distress.

Emphasizing gradual, sustainable weight loss within a safe and realistic range is essential. Educating individuals about the risks of fad diets and guiding them toward evidence-based, professionally supported care can prevent rebound weight gain and long-term harm.

Target population

This strategy is particularly important for individuals who are unsure how to identify credible, evidence-based treatment options or who seek rapid weight loss without an awareness of the potential health risks. Professional guidance can support safer, more effective, and more sustainable progress toward long-term obesity management goals.

#### Strategy 10. Learn from mistakes, progress steadily!

Explanation and evidence

This strategy encourages individuals to treat both successes and setbacks as part of the learning process. Obesity management is not achieved through a single act of willpower; it requires sustained, long-term behavioral change. Efforts to regulate eating and physical activity are continually challenged by internal cues (e.g., cravings and stress) and external cues (e.g., social meals and food availability). As a result, lapses such as skipping planned exercise or overeating are common and expected.

How individuals interpret these lapses strongly influences treatment outcomes. Those with perfectionist or all-or-nothing perspectives may view any deviation from their plan as total failure, which can lead to guilt, discouragement, and premature treatment termination. In contrast, adopting a flexible, growth-oriented perspective helps individuals view lapses as temporary setbacks that provide valuable information for adjusting goals and strategies.

When lapses occur, it is helpful to identify contributing factors. These may include unrealistic goals, insufficient coping strategies, and a lack of planning for high-risk situations. Recognizing these factors enables the creation of relapse-prevention strategies and more sustainable behavioral plans.

Examples of specific behavioral recommendations

High-risk situations (e.g., social gatherings and emotional stress) should be identified and coping strategies should be prepared in advance. When a lapse occurs, review what happened without judgment and adjust the plan accordingly. Practice flexible thinking, reminding oneself that progress is made over time and not defined by one moment or one day.

Cultural adaptation and personalization in Korea

Dietary and activity lapses may occur during work dinners, family gatherings, or social drinking contexts in collectivist cultures like Korea, where declining food may feel socially uncomfortable. Teaching polite refusal skills, portion negotiation, or planning ahead can help maintain goals while preserving social harmony.

Additionally, self-care may sometimes be perceived as selfish in communal cultural contexts. Thus, it is important to reinforce that caring for one’s health is a responsible and necessary act, benefiting both the individual and those around them.

Target population

This strategy is particularly relevant for individuals who have perfectionistic tendencies or rigid expectations, who experience self-blame or discouragement after minor lapses, or who struggle with maintaining behavior change over time.

Supporting these individuals in reframing setbacks as part of the process can promote resilience and sustained progress in obesity treatment.

## DISCUSSION

This consensus statement presents 10 culturally adaptive behavioral strategies for obesity management in Korea. Developed through a structured consensus process, these strategies address domains including diet, physical activity, sleep, and stress management. By reflecting Korea’s unique dietary patterns and social environments, these strategies are designed to be acceptable to both individuals and clinicians and sustainable long-term.

The consensus process integrated clinical expertise with behavioral theory to ensure the strategies were theoretically sound and feasible. Although the core strategies were developed by behavioral experts, the final recommendations were reviewed and refined by the multidisciplinary KSSO Board—including specialists in medicine, nutrition, and exercise science—to ensure clinical validity and comprehensive care. Importantly, the framework extends beyond weight reduction to encompass biopsychosocial well-being. By integrating emotional regulation, stress management, and sleep hygiene (Strategies 6, 7, 10), it recognizes the dynamic interaction of physiological and psychological factors. Furthermore, the strategies are tailored to the Korean context; for instance, mindful eating (Strategy 4) addresses communal dining, while physical activity (Strategy 5) emphasizes feasible everyday movement like stair use. This cultural tailoring enhances adherence by aligning goals with real-world conditions.

These strategies can be implemented as brief interventions in clinical settings or as self-help resources in the community. Dissemination via public health campaigns and digital platforms could enhance accessibility, bridging the gap between short-term interventions and long-term lifestyle modification.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the expert panel primarily consisted of mental health professionals. Although this composition ensured a deep focus on behavioral modification and psychological mechanisms, it may have limited the breadth of perspectives compared to a panel with equal representation from nutrition and exercise physiology. Second, as these strategies relied on expert consensus, empirical validation will be necessary. Future research should assess their effectiveness and scalability through pragmatic trials across diverse populations, including adolescents and individuals with eating disorders. Digital translation, such as smartphone-based coaching, represents a promising next step. Third, while these strategies primarily focus on individual behavioral modification and clinical guidance, they do not comprehensively address broader obesogenic environmental factors or policy-level interventions, which are also critical for effective obesity management.

## CONCLUSION

In conclusion, these 10 strategies for obesity management in Korea provide a practical, evidence-informed framework for behavioral change. Highlighting self-regulation and collaboration with experts, they serve as accessible components of a comprehensive, multimodal care model. This framework emphasizes personalization, ensuring that interventions are tailored to individual characteristics and cultural context to optimize treatment outcomes.
