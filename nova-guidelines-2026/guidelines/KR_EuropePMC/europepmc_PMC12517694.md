# Is the next STEP on the BPROAD to intensive blood pressure lowering for all type 2 diabetic patients?: consensus statements from the Korean Society of Hypertension

## Abstract

The optimal blood pressure (BP) target in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) continues to be debated. The 2022 guidelines from the Korean Society of Hypertension (KSH) recommend intensive BP lowering only for patients with diabetes who are at high cardiovascular (CV) risk. However, recent trials have demonstrated favorable outcomes associated with intensive BP lowering in T2DM. In response, the updated KSH consensus statements provide evidence-based recommendations supporting the implementation of intensive BP control strategies in hypertensive patients with diabetes, including those at low to moderate CV risk. The KSH consensus statements are as follows: 1) Hypertension is a common comorbidity of T2DM, with a prevalence of 59.6% among adults with diabetes aged 30 years and older in Korea. 2) In patients with T2DM, coexisting hypertension increases the risk of both macrovascular and microvascular complications; however, tight BP control reduces diabetes-related morbidity and mortality. 3) Recent guidelines advocate tailored BP targets based on individual CV risk profiles to balance treatment safety and effectiveness, and recommend a BP target of < 130/80 mmHg for patients with T2DM. 4) The BPROAD (Intensive Blood-Pressure Control in Patients with Type 2 Diabetes) trial provides the strongest evidence for intensive BP control in patients with T2DM, while the STEP (Trial of Intensive Blood-Pressure Control in Older Patients with Hypertension) and the ESPRIT (Effects of Intensive Blood Pressure Lowering Treatment in Reducing the Risk of Cardiovascular Events) trials support intensive BP lowering in high-risk diabetic patients and extend the findings to broader high-risk populations, respectively. 5) A nationwide Korean study suggests that, if patients with T2DM can safely tolerate it, lower BP levels in patients with T2DM may provide protection even without established CV disease. 6) As white coat hypertension becomes more frequent following treatment in diabetic patients, precise BP measurement is essential to avoid overtreatment, particularly in real-world clinical settings. 7) The proportion of patients with T2DM who are at low to moderate risk is small. Accordingly, the updated consensus statement from the KSH recommends a target BP of 130/80 mmHg for most patients with T2DM, provided that this target is well tolerated.

## INTRODUCTION

The global prevalence of diabetes, including in Korea, is rising. Hypertension commonly coexists with diabetes in up to two-thirds of patients. This significantly augments the risk of cardiovascular (CV) disease and related complications such as coronary artery disease, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, stroke, kidney disease, and mortality. The UKPDS (UK Prospective Diabetes Study) 36 showed that controlling blood pressure (BP) in diabetic patients reduces CV risk, highlighting the importance of optimal BP management. However, ideal BP targets remain a matter of debate due to inconsistent trial results.

The ACCORD (Action to Control Cardiovascular Risk in Diabetes) trial showed that intensive BP control (systolic blood pressure [SBP] < 120 mmHg) did not significantly reduce CV outcomes compared to standard treatment (SBP < 140 mmHg), generating controversy regarding the effectiveness of BP lowering in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). By contrast, the SPRINT (Randomized Trial of Intensive versus Standard Blood-Pressure Control) trial demonstrated that clear CV benefits from intensive BP control in high-risk individuals, although it excluded diabetic patients, leaving uncertainty about its applicability to this population. The STEP (Trial of Intensive Blood-Pressure Control in Older Patients with Hypertension) trial supported intensive BP lowering in older patients at high CV risk, but enrolled a relatively small portion of diabetic patients (19.1%). These findings informed the 2022 Korean Society of Hypertension (KSH) guidelines, which recommend a BP target of < 140/90 mmHg for low- to intermediate-risk patients and < 130/80 mmHg for those at high CV risk.

Recently, 2 trials involving more diabetic patients have provided further clarity. The ESPRIT (Effects of Intensive Blood Pressure Lowering Treatment in Reducing the Risk of Cardiovascular Events), which included 39% of participants with diabetes, showed a favorable but non-significant trend toward CV risk reduction. In contrast, the BPROAD (Intensive Blood-Pressure Control in Patients with Type 2 Diabetes) trial, which focused exclusively on patients with T2DM aged over 50 years with elevated CV risk, demonstrated clear CV benefits from intensive BP lowering. Despite these encouraging findings in high-risk populations, evidence remains limited regarding the benefits of intensive BP control in diabetic patients at low- to moderate-risk. However, since most individuals with diabetes already have elevated CV risk, truly low-risk patients are uncommon and unlikely to significantly impact clinical practice.

Accordingly, the updated consensus statements from the KSH, based on comprehensive reviews of recent big trials and international guidelines, provide evidence-based recommendations for implementing intensive BP control in patients with diabetes and coexisting hypertension, including those at low to moderate CV risk.

## IMPORTANCE OF BP CONTROL IN T2DM

According to the Korean Diabetes Association (KDA)’s 2024 Fact Sheet, hypertension affects 59.6% of patients with diabetes aged 30 years and older and 73.4% of those aged 65 years and older, with BP control rates of just 60.8% and 62.5%, respectively. This highlights inadequate BP control among diabetic patients. The coexistence of hypertension and T2DM significantly increases the risk of both macrovascular and microvascular complications, markedly elevating CV disease morbidity and mortality. Therefore, effective BP control is a critical component of T2DM care. The MRFIT (Multiple Risk Factor Intervention Trial) demonstrated that diabetes amplifies the CV disease risk associated with elevated BP, reinforcing the need for tight BP control in this population.

Pathophysiologically, hypertension and T2DM are closely interlinked. Insulin resistance contributes to endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, inflammation, arterial stiffness, and activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, all of which can elevate BP. Additionally, sympathetic nervous system overactivation, common in T2DM, raises BP through increased vascular tone and sodium retention. Emerging mechanisms, including altered gut microbiota, dysregulated extracellular vesicles/microRNAs, and enhanced renal sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 activity, further contribute to insulin resistance and T2DM-induced hypertension. Hyperglycemia exacerbates oxidative stress and vascular damage, creating a vicious cycle in which poorly managed BP and glucose levels mutually exacerbate each other. Hence, simultaneous BP and glucose control is essential to disrupt this feedback loop.

Numerous studies support the benefits of BP control in T2DM. The UKPDS 38 trial showed that tight BP control reduced diabetes-related deaths by 32%, stroke by 44%, heart failure by 56%, and microvascular complications by 37%. The ADVANCE (Effects of a Fixed Combination of Perindopril and Indapamide on Macrovascular and Microvascular Outcomes in Patients with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus) trial found that even modest BP reductions, averaging 5.6 mmHg, significantly lowered coronary events by 14% and renal complications by 21%. These findings underscore BP management as essential for preventing complications in T2DM.

## SUMMARY OF RECENT GUIDELINES ON BP CONTROLS IN T2DM

Recent guidelines propose varying BP targets based on individual CV risk levels. The 2017 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) guidelines recommend initiating antihypertensive therapy at a BP of ≥ 130/80 mmHg in diabetic patients, with a treatment goal of < 130/80 mmHg. The 2024 European Society of Cardiology guidelines advise initiating medication if office BP remains ≥ 130/80 mmHg after up to 3 months of lifestyle intervention, targeting SBP < 130 mmHg if well tolerated, while cautioning against lowering SBP below 120 mmHg. The 2023 European Society of Hypertension guidelines recommend that drug treatment strategies in patients with T2DM should be the same as for patients without diabetes, with the primary aim being to lower BP below < 130/80 mmHg, with a more lenient BP target in elderly patients. The 2022 KSH guidelines suggest a less strict target of < 140/90 mmHg for low- to intermediate-risk diabetic patients, but recommend a more stringent target of < 130/80 mmHg for those at high CV risk. The 2025 American Diabetes Association (ADA) and 2025 KDA guidelines uniformly recommend a BP target of < 130/80 mmHg for all diabetic patients. Discrepancies in BP targets among these major guidelines for patients with diabetes can be attributed to several key factors. These include differing interpretations of clinical evidence, variations in patient-centered care philosophies, and heterogeneity in study populations and inclusion criteria across clinical trials. Additionally, the time lag between new research findings and updated guideline publication contributes to inconsistencies. To balance treatment safety with individualized CV risk reduction, some guidelines advocate stratified BP targets based on patient risk profiles, whereas others recommend uniformly stringent BP goals for all patients with diabetes to achieve more aggressive CV risk reduction. In the clinical setting, implementing these varied recommendations requires careful consideration of specific factors and balancing treatment safety with CV risk reduction. Awareness of these differing guideline perspectives and their development timelines is essential for optimal individualized hypertension management in patients with diabetes.

## COMPARISON OF BPROAD, STEP, AND ESPRIT TRIALS

The BPROAD, STEP, and ESPRIT trials each evaluated the impact of intensive BP lowering but differed in their study populations and treatment strategies. BPROAD trial enrolled 12,821 Chinese patients with T2DM and elevated CV risk, randomizing them to either intensive (SBP < 120 mmHg) or standard (SBP < 140 mmHg) treatment arms. After 12 months, the mean SBP was 121.6 mmHg in the intensive group and 133.2 mmHg in the standard group, with a clear separation of achieved BP. This trial employed a flexible, algorithm-based protocol modeled after the SPRINT, encouraging the use of angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs), calcium channel blockers (CCBs), and thiazide-type diuretics, with a preference for ACEIs or ARBs in those with albuminuria. Approximately 44% of participants were prescribed ARBs at baseline, and specific agents were not mandated, allowing for individualized treatment. Concomitant use of ACEIs and ARBs was explicitly avoided.

In contrast, STEP trial enrolled 8,511 Chinese patients aged 60 to 80 years with hypertension, only 19.1% of whom had diabetes. Participants were randomized to an intensive SBP target of 110–129 mmHg or a standard target of 130–149 mmHg. At 12 months, the mean achieved SBP was 127.5 mmHg in the intensive group and 135.3 mmHg in the standard group. This trial adopted a semi-fixed, stepped-care approach, using olmesartan, amlodipine, and hydrochlorothiazide as initial agents in various combinations. It also incorporated home BP monitoring via smartphone apps to enhance long-term adherence and BP tracking in older adults.

ESPRIT trial further extended the evidence base by enrolling 11,255 high-risk patients, including those with and without diabetes or previous stroke. Participants were assigned to either intensive (< 120 mmHg) or standard (< 140 mmHg) SBP targets. At 12 months, the mean achieved SBP was 120.3 mmHg in the intensive group and 135.6 mmHg in the standard group. This trial addressed evidence gaps in patient groups often excluded from prior research, such as those with diabetes or a history of stroke, which were underrepresented or excluded in previous trials such as SPRINT.

In terms of outcomes, the BPROAD demonstrated a 21% relative risk reduction in its primary composite outcome (combination of nonfatal stroke, myocardial infarction, heart failure hospitalization, and CV death) with intensive therapy (hazard ratio [HR], 0.79; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.69–0.90; P < 0.001). These benefits were consistent across subgroups and sustained throughout a median follow-up of 4.2 years. Although rates of symptomatic hypotension and hyperkalemia were higher in the intensive group, the incidence of serious adverse events did not differ significantly between the 2 groups.

The STEP trial reported a 26% relative risk reduction in its broader composite outcome (including stroke, acute coronary syndrome, heart failure, coronary revascularization, atrial fibrillation, and CV death; HR, 0.74; 95% CI, 0.60–0.92; P = 0.007). Stroke and heart failure hospitalization were notably reduced, though there was no significant impact on all-cause mortality or isolated myocardial infarction. The inclusion of heterogeneous endpoints, such as atrial fibrillation, complicates direct comparison with other trials that focus specifically on atherosclerotic outcomes.

The ESPRIT trial demonstrated a 12% relative risk reduction in major vascular events with intensive SBP lowering (HR, 0.88; 95% CI, 0.78–0.99; P = 0.028). However, among patients with diabetes (n = 4,359), this effect was not statistically significant (HR, 0.91; 95% CI, 0.77–1.08). The broader and more heterogeneous population enrolled in the ESPRIT enhances its generalizability, but may have diluted the overall treatment effect.

Despite their contributions, each trial had limitations. The BPROAD exclusively enrolled Chinese patients with T2DM, most of whom were already on antihypertensive therapy, while this may limit generalizability to other ethnic groups or treatment-naïve patients. Additionally, 40% of participants in the intensive arm failed to achieve SBP < 120 mmHg by one year, raising questions about real-world feasibility. The trial also provided limited insight into medication adherence and dose escalation practices. The STEP trial excluded younger patients and underrepresented those with diabetes, while its open-label design and broader endpoint definition introduced potential biases. The ESPRIT, also an open-label trial, included a more diverse high-risk population, but showed only modest benefits, particularly in diabetic subgroups.

Taken collectively, these trials support the benefits of intensive BP lowering in high-risk patients. The BPROAD uniquely supports this strategy in patients with T2DM, the STEP extends findings to older adults with hypertension, and the ESPRIT demonstrates potential applicability to a broader high-risk population. These findings underscore the importance of individual BP targets and shared decision-making tailored to patient risk profiles and comorbidities (Table 2).

## EVIDENCE FOR THE BENEFIT OF INTENSIVE BP LOWERING FOR ALL T2DM PATIENTS

The ACCORD trial, one of the earliest to assess intensive BP control in patients with T2DM, compared SBP targets of < 120 mmHg and < 140 mmHg. While the primary composite outcome showed no significant difference, stroke incidence was notably lower in the intensive group. However, the ACCORD trial has faced criticism for its factorial design and potential confounding by the simultaneous glucose-lowering intervention, and insufficient statistical power for the BP comparison. These limitations tempered enthusiasm for the universal implementation of intensive BP targets in patients with T2DM. Nevertheless, the observed reduction in stroke risk suggested that certain subsets of T2DM patients might benefit from more aggressive BP control. Further interest in intensive BP lowering was stimulated by the SPRINT trial, which demonstrated substantial CV benefits with an SBP target of < 120 mmHg in nondiabetic individuals at high CV risk. Although SPRINT excluded patients with diabetes, its compelling results reinvigorated the discussion on whether similar benefits could extend to diabetic populations.

A particularly impactful recent study was the BPROAD trial, which focused specifically on patients with T2DM. This trial demonstrated a 21% relative risk reduction over a median follow-up of 4.2 years. While symptomatic hypotension and hyperkalemia occurred more frequently in the intensive group, the overall rate of serious adverse events was similar between the 2 groups, supporting the safety and tolerability of intensive BP lowering in this population. The results of the BPROAD trial represent a major milestone in clarifying the optimal BP target in patients with T2DM. For the first time, a well-powered, randomized controlled trial specifically targeting a T2DM population provided definitive evidence that SBP reduction to below 120 mmHg confers significant CV benefits. These findings are consistent with previous observational studies and subgroup analyses that suggested potential benefit but lacked robust prospective validation.

These results align with evidence from pooled analyses of prior trials. A meta-analysis combining data from the STEP and the ACCORD trials found that intensive SBP lowering significantly reduced major CV events in patients with diabetes (HR, 0.77; 95% CI, 0.64–0.93), with particularly strong effects on stroke incidence (HR, 0.56; 95% CI, 0.36–0.86). These benefits extended across glucose status categories-including normoglycemia, prediabetes, and diabetes-suggesting that intensive SBP lowering may have broader applicability beyond specific subgroups.

Real-world data from large population-based cohorts further corroborate these findings. A nationwide Korean study involving hypertensive patients with diabetes but no prior CV disease showed that individuals aged < 70 years with BP < 120/70 mmHg had the lowest CV event rates. In contrast, for patients aged ≥ 70 years, the benefit plateaued, with no significant differences between those with BP < 120/70 mmHg and those with BP 130–139/80–89 mmHg. This suggests that while intensive BP control is generally beneficial, age and comorbidities may influence the risk-benefit profile and should be considered when individualizing treatment targets. Although not derived from intervention trials, real-world evidence suggests that if lower BP can be safely achieved in routine clinical settings for patients with T2DM, the long-term protective effects may be substantial.

As a result of these accumulating data, major clinical practice guidelines have begun to reflect a shift toward more intensive BP targets in T2DM. The 2025 KDA guidelines, endorsed by KSH, recommend a target BP < 130/80 mmHg for most patients with diabetes, acknowledging the strong evidence for CV risk reduction. Similarly, the ADA and ACC/AHA advocate a BP goal of < 130/80 mmHg, while European guidelines recommend lowering SBP to < 130 mmHg, but advise caution against reducing it below 120 mmHg in certain patients due to potential adverse effects. These nuanced recommendations reflect the need to balance maximizing CV benefits with minimizing potential harms, particularly in older or frail individuals. Therefore, while a general target of SBP < 130 mmHg—and even < 120 mmHg in appropriate candidates—supported by evidence-based, clinical judgment is essential in tailoring therapy based on the patient’s overall risk profile, frailty status, and treatment tolerability.

Moreover, it should be noted that patients with diabetes are more prone to white coat hypertension. In a prospective trial of antihypertensive treatment in patients with T2DM, the prevalence of white coat hypertension increased from 12% at baseline to 39% after treatment, with nearly all affected individuals exhibiting a white coat effect exceeding 10/5 mmHg. This finding has significant practical implications: if treatment intensification is guided solely by clinic BP measurements without confirmation from out-of-office BP monitoring, patients may be at risk of overtreatment. This issue is particularly relevant in real-world Korean practice, where clinic BP measurements are often performed under suboptimal conditions, typically yielding higher readings than those obtained in standardized research settings. The Korean Hypertension Cohort demonstrated that in patients with clinic SBP ≥ 150 mmHg, the corresponding BP measured under standardized national health examinations was often significantly lower, indicating substantial overestimation of BP in routine practice. In such settings, where the diagnosis and titration of hypertension therapy rely primarily on clinic BP, intensive treatment regimens may inadvertently introduce second-line agents like spironolactone, β-blockers, or α-blockers, which carry higher risks of orthostatic hypotension or electrolyte imbalances compared to first-line agents such as CCBs, ARBs, or thiazide diuretics. Therefore, implementing an intensive BP-lowering strategy in patients with diabetes requires precise BP measurement, careful patient selection, and close monitoring of electrolytes. With these safeguards, the CV benefits demonstrated in clinical trials can be effectively translated into real-world practice, enhancing outcomes while minimizing potential harms.

## IS THERE ENOUGH EVIDENCE TO RECOMMEND INTENSIVE BP LOWERING IN LOW-RISK T2DM PATIENTS?

Despite the negative findings of the earlier ACCORD trial, the BPROAD trial convincingly demonstrated that intensive BP lowering significantly reduced CV outcomes in patients aged 50 and older with T2DM and elevated CV risk. Therefore, the remaining question is this. Is there enough evidence to recommend intensive BP lowering for all diabetic subjects with hypertension, including diabetic subjects with low to intermediate risk? Despite the positive findings from the BPROAD trial, there is still no randomized trial evidence to support intensive BP lowering in diabetic patients at low to intermediate risk.

However, there are arguments in favor of recommending intensive BP lowering for all diabetic patients. First, an analysis of the 271,174 subjects with T2DM from the Swedish National Diabetes Register found that those without 5 key CV risk factors-elevated HbA1c, elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, elevated BP, albuminuria, and smoking-did not have an increased risk of CV events such as acute myocardial infarction and stroke or mortality compared to controls, while the risk of CV events increased with the number of accompanying CV risk factors. However, only 5% of the diabetic patients met this low-risk profile. In Korea, among the estimated 5.61 million subjects with diabetes between 2013 to 2021, 98.7% would be classified as high risk according to the 2022 KSH guideline and therefore indicated for intensive BP lowering. Therefore, the majority of patients with diabetes are at increased risk of developing CV disease, and the proportion of those considered to be at low risk is too small to have significant practical relevance in clinical practice. Second, a meta-analysis of BP-lowering trials stratified by baseline CV risk found that the relative risk reduction from intensive BP treatment was consistent across risk levels, although the absolute benefit was greater in individuals at higher risk.

For frail, elderly diabetic patients, especially those with symptoms or evidence of orthostatic hypotension, the treatment target should be individualized. Careful titration of BP medication to an initial goal of 140/90 mmHg seems prudent. Further lowering of BP should not be done at the expense of worsening symptoms of orthostatic hypotension. Starting with low-dose renin-angiotensin system inhibitors and/or CCBs is recommended, with the use of low-dose thiazide-like/type diuretics if needed. It is recommended to avoid beta-blockers or alpha-blockers unless indicated.

Taken together, since the majority of diabetics are at high risk for CV disease, the evidence mentioned above suggests that most patients with diabetes will benefit from intensive BP lowering treatment. Even a small proportion of patients not at high CV risk may still benefit, although the absolute risk reduction would be smaller.

## CONCLUSIONS

Based on key research findings and a review of various clinical guidelines, the KSH recommends the following consensus statements on BP lowering in patients with T2DM.

### Consensus statements from KSH on intensive BP lowering for all T2DM

1. Hypertension is a common comorbidity of T2DM, with a prevalence of 59.6% among adults with diabetes aged 30 years and older in Korea.

2. In patients with T2DM, coexisting hypertension increases the risk of both macrovascular and microvascular complications; however, tight BP control reduces diabetes-related morbidity and mortality.

3. Recent guidelines advocate tailored BP targets based on individual CV risk profiles to balance treatment safety and effectiveness, and recommend a BP target of < 130/80 mmHg for patients with T2DM.

4. The BPROAD trial provides the strongest evidence for intensive BP control in patients with T2DM, while the STEP and the ESPRIT trials support intensive BP lowering in high-risk diabetic patients and extend the findings to broader high-risk populations, respectively.

5. A nationwide Korean study suggests that, if patients with T2DM can safely tolerate it, lower BP levels in patients with T2DM may provide protection even without established CV disease.

6. As white coat hypertension becomes more frequent following treatment in diabetic patients, precise BP measurement is essential to avoid overtreatment, particularly in real-world clinical settings.

7. The proportion of patients with T2DM who are at low to moderate risk is small. Accordingly, the updated consensus statement from the KSH recommends a target BP of 130/80 mmHg for most patients with T2DM, provided that this target is well tolerated.
